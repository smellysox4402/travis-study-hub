THE BIG PUNNETT

Two genes at once: segregation + independent assortment, and the famous 9:3:3:1.

🎲

Two genes, one grid

segregation · independent assortment · dihybrid cross · 9:3:3:1

Segregation: the two alleles of each gene separate into different gametes during meiosis — each gamete gets one allele per gene.

Independent assortment: for genes on different chromosomes, the allele pairs sort independently — the gamete's allele of gene 1 doesn't care which allele of gene 2 it's carrying. The result: a heterozygous dihybrid (RrYy) makes four equally common gametes: RY, Ry, rY, ry.

Cross two dihybrids (RrYy × RrYy) and the 16-box grid collapses to the famous 9:3:3:1 phenotype ratio — 9 round-yellow : 3 round-green : 3 wrinkled-yellow : 1 wrinkled-green. If the ratio is off, the genes aren't assorting independently — a hint of linkage (Act 11).

Figure 10 · The 16-box dihybrid grid and its 9:3:3:1 collapse. Genes on different chromosomes assort independently.
Exam trick: you rarely need all 16 boxes. Split it into two monohybrid crosses (Rr × Rr → 3:1, Yy × Yy → 3:1) and multiply: 3:1 × 3:1 = 9:3:3:1. Saves time and catches errors.

THE VARIATION FLOOR

Discrete vs continuous variation — and the box-and-whisker plot that displays the spread.

📊

Discrete club vs continuous dancefloor

polygenic inheritance · continuous variation · box-and-whisker

Discrete variation (ABO blood type, pea colour): a few genes, separate categories, no in-between. Continuous variation (height, skin colour, mass): many genes (polygenic inheritance) plus environmental influence → a smooth range from one extreme to the other.

Continuous data is summarised with a box-and-whisker plot: minimum, lower quartile (Q1), median, upper quartile (Q3), maximum. The box spans the interquartile range (IQR = Q3 − Q1) — the middle 50% of the data. Whiskers run to the extremes; points beyond 1.5 × IQR are plotted separately as outliers.

D3.2.18 · D3.2.19 · D3.2.20 · D3.2.21 · AHL

THE AHL BACK ROOM

Loci, gene linkage, recombinants, and the chi-squared test that tells you if ratios are lying.

🔗

When genes ride together

locus · linked genes · recombinants · chi-squared (χ²)

Each gene sits at a locus (plural loci) on a specific chromosome — humans have ~20,000 genes spread across the autosomes (1–22) and sex chromosomes. When two genes sit close together on the same chromosome, their alleles tend to be inherited together — that's autosomal gene linkage. They don't assort independently, so a dihybrid cross gives a skewed ratio, not 9:3:3:1.

Crossing over in meiosis can shuffle segments between non-sister chromatids, producing recombinants — new allele combinations. In a test cross of a double heterozygote: unlinked genes → 1:1:1:1; linked genes → parental types dominate, recombinants are rare (their frequency reflects how far apart the genes are).

The chi-squared (χ²) test decides if an observed ratio fits an expected one (3:1, 9:3:3:1, 1:1:1:1): χ² = Σ (observed − expected)² / expected. Compare with the critical value at p = 0.05 and degrees of freedom = classes − 1. If χ² is inside the critical region → reject the null hypothesis → the ratio doesn't fit.

Figure 12 · AHL: linkage skews ratios, crossing over makes recombinants, and χ² tells you whether the deviation is real or just sampling noise.

Corn numbers — the worked example

Test cross of CcNn × ccnn (coloured/shrunken corn seeds). If unlinked, expect 1:1:1:1. Observed: 638 CcNn · 21,379 Ccnn · 21,096 ccNn · 672 ccnn. The parental combos (Cn, cN) swamp the recombinants (CN, cn) — so C and N are linked on the same chromosome. Crossing over produced the rare recombinants.

Exam shortcut: in any test cross, parental types = most frequent, recombinants = least frequent. The recombination frequency ≈ distance between loci. And χ² always uses (observed − expected)² / expected, summed over every class.
