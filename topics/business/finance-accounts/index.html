FORMULA CHEAT ⚡

Every formula the exam can ask you to use. All on the formula sheet — but know which to reach for.

1 · GROSS PROFIT MARGIN = gross profit ÷ sales × 100 · PROFIT MARGIN (NPM) = profit before interest & tax ÷ sales × 100
2 · ROCE = profit before interest & tax ÷ capital employed × 100 · capital employed = non-current liabilities + equity
3 · CURRENT RATIO = current assets ÷ current liabilities · ACID TEST = (current assets − stock) ÷ current liabilities
4 · STOCK TURNOVER = cost of sales ÷ average stock · ×365 → days held
5 · DEBTOR DAYS = debtors ÷ sales × 365 · CREDITOR DAYS = creditors ÷ cost of sales × 365
6 · GEARING = non-current liabilities ÷ capital employed × 100 · >50% = highly geared (risky)
7 · WORKING CAPITAL = current assets − current liabilities · NET CASH FLOW = inflows − outflows
8 · CLOSING BALANCE = opening balance + net cash flow · becomes next month's opening balance
9 · PAYBACK = cost ÷ annual net cash flow · uneven flows → count whole years + fraction of next
10 · ARR = (total profit ÷ years of use) ÷ initial cost × 100 · compare to the target rate
11 · NPV = Σ(present values of cash flows) − initial cost · positive → ACCEPT
12 · DEPRECIATION (straight line) = (cost − residual) ÷ useful life · UNITS OF PRODUCTION = (cost − residual) × units made ÷ total units
Act 1 · 3.1 + 3.2

RAISING THE DOUGH 🤑

Finance exists to fund the club's life: starting up, growing, and just keeping the lights on tonight.

WHY A BUSINESS NEEDS FINANCE (3.1)

The role of finance is to give the business the money to start, to expand and to keep trading. Every dollar of spending falls into one of two buckets:

Capital expenditure

Buying the assets 🎛

Money spent on fixed assets that last longer than one year — the sound system, lighting rig, furniture, a new DJ booth.

It appears on the balance sheet as an asset, and the cost is spread over its life as depreciation (Act 3).

Revenue expenditure

Paying to run the night 🌙

Money spent on day-to-day operations — DJ fees, staff wages, drinks stock, utilities, marketing, rent.

It appears in the profit & loss account as an expense, deducted from revenue in the same period.

Exam reflex: capital = the assets themselves; revenue = running the business. A new mixer is capital; the drinks you pour with it are revenue.

INTERNAL vs EXTERNAL SOURCES (3.2)

Internal finance = money from inside the business. External = money from outside — lenders, investors or platforms.

Internal · no outsiders

🏠 PERSONAL FUNDS

The sole trader's own savings. Zero interest, zero control loss — but capped by what the owner personally has.

Internal · no outsiders

📦 RETAINED PROFIT

Profits kept in the business instead of paid out as dividends. The cheapest finance of all — no interest, no new shares — but only exists if the club actually made profit.

Internal · no outsiders

🪑 SALE OF ASSETS

Sell unused or obsolete assets — old decks, spare lighting — and turn them into cash. Best when upgrading: sell the old, fund the new.

External · ownership

📈 SHARE CAPITAL

Investors buy shares = part-ownership. No repayment, dividends only if profits allow. Only limited companies can use it. New shares dilute the founders' control.

External · borrowing

🏦 LOAN CAPITAL

Borrow from a bank, repay with interest over a fixed term. Certain and planned — but interest must be paid regardless of profit, raising gearing (Act 5).

External · borrowing

💳 OVERDRAFTS

Borrow up to a limit, pay interest on what you use. Flexible for short-term cash gaps — but high interest and repayable on demand.

External · borrowing

📄 TRADE CREDIT

Suppliers deliver now, you pay in 30–60 days. Free short-term finance — but late payment damages goodwill and could cost discounts.

External · new-style

📣 CROWDFUNDING

Raise small amounts from many people online, often pre-selling perks (early tickets, merch). Great for a launch — but public, and you must deliver the rewards.

External · asset use

🔑 LEASING

Rent equipment instead of buying it. Preserves cash for other uses and gives flexibility — but costs more overall and you never own the asset.

External · new-style

🌱 MICROFINANCE PROVIDERS

Small, short-term loans for entrepreneurs who can't access bank credit. Vital for start-ups in developing economies — small amounts, often higher relative cost.

External · ownership

🦅 BUSINESS ANGELS

Wealthy individuals who invest their own money in early-stage ventures, often mentoring too. They take equity — so they share control and expect a big return.

Fig 1. Sources of finance: internal vs external, matched to how long the money is needed. The exam loves asking whether a short-term or long-term source fits a given situation (AO3).
⚠ Exam trap — "Share capital" and "business angels" give away ownership (equity); "loan capital", "overdrafts", "trade credit" and "leasing" create debt (interest). Also remember: share capital is only available to limited companies — a sole trader can never sell shares.
💭 TOK — When Neon Pulse pitches to a bank vs a business angel, do objective financial data or emotional storytelling convince more? Different sources of finance are persuaded by different evidence.

APPROPRIATENESS — MATCH THE SOURCE TO THE SITUATION (3.2 AO3)

The exam gives you a scenario and asks: which source fits best? Three questions to ask:

  1. How long is the money needed? Day-to-day cash gaps → overdraft/trade credit. A new venue → loan capital or share capital.
  2. What is it for? Replacing an obsolete asset → sale of assets. Starting up → personal funds, microfinance, business angels, crowdfunding.
  3. What can the business afford? Interest on a big loan raises gearing and risk (Act 5) — an unprofitable club can't service debt; it may need equity or retained profit instead.
✅ Apply it — Neon Pulse needs $60k this week to pay the drinks supplier before they cut supply. Short-term → overdraft or negotiate trade credit. They need $300k for a second venue → long-term: loan capital, share capital, or retained profit.
Act 2 · 3.3

PRICE OF THE PARTY 🧾

Every cost has a personality. Know which ones panic when the crowd shrinks — and which ones don't care.

THE FOUR COST PERSONALITIES

Cost typeWhat it meansNightclub example
FIXEDStays the same however many customers comeRent, salaries of permanent staff, insurance, loan interest
VARIABLEChanges directly with output/salesDrinks stock, bar staff wages on busy nights, ice & glassware
DIRECTTraceable to a specific product/serviceCost of the drinks sold, the DJ's fee for a specific event
INDIRECT (OVERHEADS)Shared across the whole business — not traceable to one productRent, utilities, security, marketing, admin salaries

A cost can be both: rent is fixed AND indirect. Drinks stock is variable AND direct. Don't treat the pairs as mutually exclusive.

Total revenue

🎟 TOTAL REVENUE = price × quantity

All the money the club earns from selling its main product. 400 guests × $40 ticket = $16,000 on the door.

Revenue streams

💰 MULTIPLE STREAMS

Revenue from different sources: tickets, bar sales, VIP booth hire, merchandise, DJ guest nights, sponsorship and advertising. Diversified streams = less risk if one dries up.

⚠ Exam trap — Total revenue = one main product × quantity sold. Revenue streams = the different products/services a business earns from. The question will punish you for confusing "total revenue" with "revenue streams".

THE CLUB'S REPORT CARD 📊

At year-end the club publishes two statements: one film of the year (profit & loss), one photograph of a single moment (balance sheet).

WHY ACCOUNTS EXIST — THE STAKEHOLDERS

Different people read the same accounts and look for different things:

StakeholderWhat they want from the accounts

ManagersPerformance of departments, where to cut costs, planning

SuppliersWill they be paid? (liquidity + creditworthiness)

STATEMENT OF PROFIT OR LOSS (P&L)

A film of the year: revenue in at the top, every cost stripped out, until you reach the money that actually stays in the club.

Fig 2. The P&L waterfall — every line strips away another cost. The two numbers the exam builds on: gross profit (600) and PBIT (400).
Intangible assets (3.4)

👻 MONEY YOU CAN'T TOUCH

Non-physical assets with value: goodwill (the club's reputation & loyal crowd), patents (exclusive right to an invention), copyright (the club's own music/logo), trademarks (the Neon Pulse brand).

They sit on the balance sheet under non-current assets — often worth more than the physical gear.

Depreciation (HL)

⏳ ASSETS WEAR OUT

Depreciation = spreading the cost of a fixed asset over its useful life, matching the cost to the years that benefit from it.

It's a non-cash expense — no money leaves the club, but profit is reduced each year.

DEPRECIATION METHODS (HL)

MethodFormulaWhen it fits
STRAIGHT LINE(cost − residual value) ÷ useful life
$120,000 sound system, $20,000 resale, 4 years → $25,000/yr every year		Assets that wear out evenly over time — furniture, lighting rigs. Simple, predictable, same charge each year.
UNITS OF PRODUCTION(cost − residual) × units made ÷ total expected units
$50,000 stage lighting, $10,000 resale, 100,000 hrs life; 12,000 hrs used → $4,800 this year		Assets whose wear depends on use, not time — machinery, vehicles. Charge matches actual activity — high output year = high depreciation.
✅ Which to choose? The method should reflect how the asset wears out. Even use over time → straight line. Use that varies with output → units of production. State the reason in the exam — that's the AO3 mark.

STATEMENT OF FINANCIAL POSITION (BALANCE SHEET)

A photograph at a single moment: what the club owns (assets), owes (liabilities), and the owners' stake (equity). The two sides must balance — it's the same money seen from two angles.

Fig 3. The balance sheet equation. Working capital = current assets − current liabilities (57 − 42 = 15) is buried inside this statement — know where to find it.
⚠ Exam trap — P&L = a flow over time (the year's performance). Balance sheet = a stock at one date (31 Dec). A club can show a great P&L profit and still be drowning — that's Act 6.
Act 4 · 3.5

THE HEALTH CHECK 💓

Profit figures are raw data. Ratios turn them into a verdict — and a target for improvement.

PROFITABILITY RATIOS — IS THE CLUB MAKING MONEY?

RatioFormulaNeon PulseReading it
GROSS PROFIT MARGINgross profit ÷ sales × 100600 ÷ 1,000 = 60%How much of each sales $ survives after the cost of what was sold. Falling GPM = rising cost of sales or price cuts.
PROFIT MARGIN (net)profit before interest & tax ÷ sales × 100400 ÷ 1,000 = 40%What's left after ALL operating costs. Falling while GPM holds = expenses creeping up.
ROCEprofit before interest & tax ÷ capital employed × 100
capital employed = non-current liabilities + equity = 300 + 195		400 ÷ 495 ≈ 81%The big one: return the whole business earns on the money tied up in it. Compare to other clubs & to the interest rate.
Strategies to improve PROFITABILITY

📈 Raise GPM / NPM / ROCE

  • Raise prices (brand loyalty permitting)
  • Cut cost of sales — cheaper suppliers, bulk buying
  • Cut overheads — renegotiate rent, energy, staff rosters
  • Sell higher-margin items (VIP booths, premium bottles)
  • Improve productivity per employee / per hour open
The trade-off

⚖ Margin vs volume

Cheaper tickets = more guests but lower margin per head. Expensive VIP = high margin, fewer buyers. GPM can't be judged alone — it's a balance with sales volume.

LIQUIDITY RATIOS — CAN THE CLUB PAY TONIGHT'S BILLS?

RatioFormulaNeon PulseReading it
CURRENT RATIOcurrent assets ÷ current liabilities57 ÷ 42 = 1.36 : 1Roughly 2:1 is comfortable, >1:1 minimum. Below 1 = current assets can't cover what's due soon.

Strategies to improve LIQUIDITY

💧 Free up cash

  • Sell slow-moving stock / reduce stock levels
  • Chase debtors harder — discounts for early payment
  • Negotiate longer trade credit from suppliers
  • Convert unused assets into cash (sale of assets)
  • Short-term overdraft to bridge a genuine gap
The trap

🥶 Profitable but illiquid

Neon Pulse is hugely profitable (81% ROCE) yet its acid test is 0.52. Money is sitting in stock and credit sales instead of the bank — one slow week and the DJ walks. Profitability and liquidity are different fights.

⚠ Exam trap — The acid test excludes stock — always subtract it. And remember the syllabus formula for net profit margin uses profit before interest and tax, not profit after tax.
Act 5 · 3.6 · HL ONLY

BLOOD WORK 🩸

Efficiency ratios answer: how well is the club running its stock, its customers, its suppliers — and how much of the business actually belongs to the owners?

EFFICIENCY RATIOS

RatioFormulaNeon PulseReading it
STOCK TURNOVERcost of sales ÷ average stock (×365 → days)400 ÷ 35 ≈ 11.4 × / yr (~32 days)How fast stock sells. Perishable goods → need HIGH turnover. Luxury goods → low is fine. High-end jewellers hold stock for ages and don't panic.

Strategies to improve efficiency

⚙ Tune the machine

  • Stock: JIT ordering, narrower range, clear obsolete stock
  • Debtors: cash discounts, tighter credit control, shorter credit terms
  • Creditors: negotiate longer terms, switch suppliers
  • Gearing: repay loans, use retained profit / new share capital instead of borrowing
High gearing isn't always doom

🎢 Gearing = risk, not failure

If interest rates are low and the loan funds growth that out-earns the interest, high gearing can be affordable. The exam wants the nuance, not just "high = bad".

INSOLVENCY vs BANKRUPTCY

Insolvency

💀 A FINANCIAL STATE

The business cannot pay its debts as they fall due — liabilities exceed assets or cash is gone. It can be temporary: fix the cash flow (Act 6) and you're solvent again.

Bankruptcy

⚖ A LEGAL PROCESS

A court procedure that formally declares an individual or sole trader unable to pay debts — assets are seized and shared among creditors. For companies the equivalent legal process is liquidation.

Insolvency is the condition; bankruptcy/liquidation is the legal response to it. You can be insolvent without being bankrupt — that's the whole point of cash-flow management.

⚠ Exam trap — Debtor days ÷ creditor days use DIFFERENT denominators: debtors ÷ sales; creditors ÷ cost of sales. Swapping them costs easy marks.
Act 6 · 3.7

CASH IS KING 👑

Profit is an opinion. Cash is a fact. The club can be profitable AND unable to pay tonight's wages.

PROFIT ≠ CASH FLOW

Profit is booked when the sale happens (accrual accounting). Cash arrives only when the money actually lands — often weeks later if customers buy on credit.

  • Net cash flow = cash inflows − cash outflows over a period.
  • A business can be profitable with negative cash flow — sales on credit, overstocking, big investments.
  • A business can be loss-making with positive cash flow — e.g. customers paying upfront for a future event.
  • Working capital = current assets − current liabilities — the money circulating through day-to-day operations.
  • Liquidity = how easily the club can turn assets into cash to meet short-term bills.
✅ The classic line — "A profitable business can still go bankrupt if it runs out of cash." That sentence is worth a whole 10-mark question. Learn it, live it, write it.
Fig 4. The working capital cycle. The shorter the loop (cash → stock → sale → cash), the healthier the club. Supermarkets & barbers spin it fast; shipbuilders wait years.

The examiner's favourite twist: a profitable month still ends with a negative closing balance — then they ask for two causes and two fixes.

INVESTMENT, PROFIT AND CASH FLOW — THE RELATIONSHIP

  • Investment (capital expenditure) creates an immediate cash outflow — but is expected to generate profit over several future years.
  • Short term: cash suffers. Long term: if the investment works, profit and cash flow both improve.
  • At the end of an asset's life, selling it (minus depreciation) brings cash back in.
  • Not investing at all is also a risk — Blockbuster, HMV and Kodak ignored change and collapsed.

STRATEGIES FOR CASH FLOW PROBLEMS

Cut the outflow

🩹 Reduce spending

  • Longer trade credit from suppliers
  • Lease instead of buy equipment
  • Hold less stock (JIT)
  • Renegotiate rent / cheaper premises
Boost the inflow

📢 More cash in

  • Marketing push — more guests
  • Cash discounts for early payment
  • Raise prices on loyal products
  • Drop prices where competition is fierce
Extra finance

🏦 Bridge the gap

  • Overdraft / short-term loan (costs interest)
  • Sell unused fixed assets
  • Form a partnership for fresh capital
⚠ Exam trap — If the cash flow forecast has a negative closing balance, your job is to fix it (cut outflows, boost inflows, extra finance) — not to panic. Identify the problem months, explain causes, propose fixes.
Act 7 · 3.8

SHOULD WE BUILD THE VIP ROOM? 🚧

The club wants a $60,000 sound system. Three tools decide if it's worth it: payback, ARR, and (HL) NPV.

THE PROJECT — NEW SOUND SYSTEM

Initial cost $60,000 · expected net cash flows:

Year12345
Net cash flow ($'000)2018161412
Payback period

⏱ How long to get the money back?

Count the years until the cost is recovered:

20 + 18 + 16 = 54 after 3 years → 6 left → 6 ÷ 14 (year 4's flow) = 0.43

Payback = 3.43 years (~3y 5m)

Loves: simplicity, liquidity focus, quick decisions. Hates: ignores cash after payback, ignores profitability, ignores timing/value of money.

Average rate of return

📊 Profit as a % of the cost

Total profit = 80 − 60 = 20 · average per year = 20 ÷ 5 = 4

ARR = 4 ÷ 60 × 100

ARR = 6.67%

Compare to the target (say 10%): 6.67% < 10% → REJECT. Loves: profitability, easy comparison. Hates: ignores timing, uses profit not cash, predictions get shaky long-term.

NPV — MONEY LATER IS WORTH LESS (HL)

$1,000 today buys more than $1,000 in five years — inflation, and the lost interest you could have earned. NPV discounts future cash flows back to today's value using the discount rate (cost of capital), then compares with the cost.

Fig 5. NPV in action. Each future cash flow shrinks by its discount factor; if the total present value beats the initial cost, the investment is worthwhile. The discount table is provided in the exam.
⚠ Exam trap — Accept rules: payback — within the firm's target time. ARR — above the target rate of return. NPV — positive. And remember NPV's discount table is GIVEN in HL exams — don't try to memorise factors.
💭 TOK — NPV looks mathematical, but the discount rate and the forecast cash flows are human guesses. Is a number that feels precise actually more reliable than a judgement call?
Act 8 · 3.9 · HL ONLY

PLAN VS ACTUAL 📋

A budget is the club's financial plan for the year. Variance analysis is the scoreboard that shows where reality drifted off the plan.

COST CENTRES vs PROFIT CENTRES

Cost centre

🧾 RESPONSIBLE FOR COSTS ONLY

A department held accountable for its own costs, with no direct revenue. Marketing, HR, R&D, security, customer service.

They add to the cost of running the club — but without them, the club can't function.

Profit centre

💸 RESPONSIBLE FOR COSTS AND REVENUE

A division accountable for both its costs and its revenue — hence its own profit. Each branch of a chain; the VIP room could be one.

Managers of profit centres can be judged on the profit they produce.

Roles: monitoring & control of each unit · faster, better decisions (managers empowered) · motivation via responsibility · accountability. Limits: unhealthy internal rivalry · arbitrary splitting of shared fixed costs (how do you split the electricity bill?).

CONSTRUCTING A BUDGET + VARIANCES

Budget the revenues and costs for the year (or month), then compare actual results. The difference is the variance:

  • Favourable (F) = better than planned: revenue higher or costs lower than budget.
  • Adverse (A) = worse than planned: revenue lower or costs higher than budget.

WHY BUDGETS MATTER (AND WHEN THEY LIE)

Why they matter

🎯 Planning & control

  • Planning — operations built on expected money
  • Cash-flow forecasting — time the spending
  • Prioritising — forces choices (which training? which DJ?)
  • Controlling — spot problems early, correct course
  • Target-setting & motivation — goals for departments
  • Accountability — budget holders answer for overspend
  • Benchmarking — a yardstick for success/failure
When they lie

🎭 Limitations

  • Expensive & time-consuming to build and update
  • Inflexible budgets demotivate staff when reality shifts
  • Forecasts are only guesses — dynamic markets move fast
  • Budgets become politics: holders inflate them for status
  • "Use it or lose it" → wasteful end-of-year spending sprees
⚠ Exam trap — A cost variance is favourable when actual is LOWER than budget (spent less = good). A revenue variance is favourable when actual is HIGHER (earned more = good). Getting the F/A direction backwards is the classic 1-mark massacre.
Practice

SELF-TEST ✍

Click an answer. Instant feedback. No mercy.

1. Neon Pulse buys a new $120,000 sound system. Capital or revenue expenditure?

✅ Capital expenditure = spending on fixed assets that last over a year. It's depreciated over its life, not expensed in one year.
❌ Capital — it's a fixed asset. Revenue expenditure is day-to-day running costs (wages, stock, utilities).

2. Which of these is an INTERNAL source of finance?

✅ Retained profit is generated inside the business. The other three all come from outside.
❌ Retained profit — profits kept in the business. Shares, overdrafts and angels are external.

3. Who CANNOT raise finance by selling share capital?

✅ Share capital means selling part-ownership — only companies (with shareholders) can do that. A sole trader is the business.
❌ Only limited companies can issue shares. Sole traders rely on personal funds, loans, or partners.

4. The cost of the drinks the club buys for resale is a…

✅ Variable — it changes directly with how many guests (and drinks) there are. Rent, by contrast, is fixed.
❌ Variable. Drink stock rises and falls with sales volume — that's the definition of a variable cost.

5. Which of these is NOT a revenue stream for the club?

✅ Rent is an EXPENSE (cash out), not a revenue stream (cash in). Revenue streams are the different ways the club earns.
❌ Rent is money LEAVING the club — a cost, not a revenue stream. Revenue streams = tickets, bar, VIP, merch, sponsorship.

6. Sales $1,000k, cost of sales $400k. Gross profit margin?

✅ Gross profit = 1,000 − 400 = 600 → 600 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = 60%.
❌ GP = 600, so 600 ÷ 1,000 = 60%. (40% would be the cost of sales ratio.)

7. What does the acid test ratio exclude that the current ratio includes?

✅ Acid test = (current assets − stock) ÷ current liabilities. Stock is the slowest asset to convert to cash.
❌ Stock is excluded. Debtors and cash stay in — the acid test is a harsher, faster liquidity check.

8. The numerator of ROCE is…

✅ ROCE = PBIT ÷ capital employed × 100. Using PBIT means financing decisions don't distort the return on the whole business.
❌ It's profit before interest and tax (PBIT). Gross profit is for GPM; after-tax profit misses the point of ROCE.

9. A supermarket selling perishable goods should want a…

✅ High turnover — perishables rot on the shelf. Luxury jewellers, by contrast, tolerate low turnover because margins are huge.
❌ High. Perishable goods MUST turn over fast — that's why the ratio varies by industry.

10. The club made a healthy profit this year yet can't pay this month's wages. Most likely why?

✅ Classic scenario: profit is booked at the sale, but cash only arrives when debtors pay. A profitable business CAN run out of cash — that's the whole of Act 6.
❌ It's the cash-flow gap: profit is earned at the point of sale, but the cash hasn't landed yet. That's why "profitable but bankrupt" happens.

11. Cost $60,000; net cash flows 20, 18, 16, 14, 12 ($k). Payback period?

✅ After 3 years 54k recovered → 6k left → 6 ÷ 14 = 0.43 → 3.43 years.
❌ 20+18+16 = 54 → 6 remaining → 6/14 = 0.43 → 3.43 years.

12. The NPV accept/reject rule is…

✅ Positive NPV = the discounted cash flows exceed the initial cost — the project earns more than the discount rate.
❌ Positive. Negative NPV means the project destroys value — reject it.

13. Sales budgeted at $500k, actual $520k. The variance is…

✅ Actual > budget on revenue = favourable. (Remember: on COSTS, spending less than budget is favourable.)
❌ +20 favourable — the club earned MORE than planned. Revenue above budget is always favourable.
