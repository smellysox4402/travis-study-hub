A1.1.1

WATER AS THE MEDIUM FOR LIFE

The first cells originated in water, and water remains the medium in which most of life's processes take place.

Life is water-based. Every theory of the origin of life places the first cells in water, and inside almost every living cell the chemical reactions of metabolism take place in an aqueous solution.

  • Water is the medium — the solvent — in which most biological reactions occur.
  • It is also the transport medium: blood in animals and xylem/phloem in plants carry dissolved substances in water.
  • Its properties (below) all trace back to the polar covalent bonds and hydrogen bonds between molecules.
A1.1.2

STRUCTURE & POLARITY OF WATER

Water (H₂O) has polar covalent bonds because the shared electrons are pulled more strongly toward oxygen, creating a δ− and a δ+ end. You should be able to draw two or more water molecules with their partial charges.

An oxygen atom forms a covalent bond with each of two hydrogen atoms, sharing an electron pair. But oxygen is much more electronegative, so it pulls the shared electrons closer — the electrons are unequally shared.

  • Oxygen carries a slight negative charge (δ−) — it has the electrons more of the time.
  • Each hydrogen carries a slight positive charge (δ+) — it is left with a partial deficit.
  • Because the two O–H bonds are not opposite, the molecule has a polar shape: a "bent" molecule with a δ− oxygen end and a δ+ hydrogen end.
FIG 1 — Two water molecules. Each has a δ− oxygen and δ+ hydrogens; a hydrogen bond (dashed, purple) forms between a δ+ hydrogen of one molecule and the δ− oxygen of another.
A1.1.2

HYDROGEN BONDING

Hydrogen bonds are weak intermolecular attractions between a δ+ hydrogen and a δ− oxygen (or nitrogen, fluorine) of another molecule. They explain most of water's special properties.

A hydrogen bond forms when a δ+ hydrogen atom of one water molecule is attracted to a δ− oxygen atom of a neighbouring water molecule. Because oxygen, nitrogen and fluorine are the most electronegative elements, these bonds are noticeably stronger than ordinary intermolecular attractions — though still much weaker than covalent bonds.

The continuous, ever-shifting network of hydrogen bonds between water molecules gives water its cohesion, high specific heat capacity, high latent heat of vaporisation, and its solvent properties.

FIG 2 — A hydrogen-bonding network. Each water molecule can hydrogen-bond with up to four neighbours, giving water its strong cohesion and high heat capacity.
A1.1.3

COHESION & SURFACE TENSION

Cohesion is the attraction of water molecules to each other via hydrogen bonds. It drives transport under tension in xylem and creates surface tension.

Water is cohesive — molecules stick to each other through hydrogen bonds. Within a body of water each molecule is pulled equally in all directions, but at the surface the molecules are only pulled downward and inward, making the surface act like a stretched film. This is surface tension.

  • Surface tension supports small organisms such as water striders, which distribute their weight over the surface using long legs.
  • Transpiration stream / xylem transport: water evaporates from leaves, pulling a continuous chain of cohesive molecules up through narrow xylem vessels under tension.
FIG 3 — Cohesion in action. Left: surface tension supports a water strider. Right: the cohesive column of water in a xylem vessel is pulled upward (under tension) by transpiration.
A1.1.4

ADHESION & CAPILLARY ACTION

Adhesion is the attraction of water to polar or charged surfaces. It produces capillary action in soil and within plant cell walls.

Water is also adhesive — it sticks to other polar or charged substances (like the cellulose of plant cell walls). The combination of adhesion (to the walls) and cohesion (between water molecules) allows water to climb narrow tubes against gravity: this is capillary action.

  • In soil, water moves through tiny pores from the deeper water table toward the surface.
  • In plants, adhesion to the narrow xylem walls plus cohesion between molecules helps a pressure gradient draw water up the stem.
  • The narrower the tube, the stronger the capillary action.
FIG 4 — Capillary action. Water climbs the narrow tube above the surrounding free surface because the water molecules adhere to the walls and cohere to one another.
A1.1.5

WATER AS A SOLVENT

Water dissolves a wide range of hydrophilic molecules, making it the medium for metabolism and for transport. Some molecules must remain hydrophobic and insoluble.

Because water is polar, it dissolves hydrophilic substances (polar molecules and ions), which then collide more easily with enzymes and react. This makes water an ideal metabolic medium: most enzymes catalyse reactions in aqueous solution, and water even absorbs heat from exothermic reactions and helps maintain acid–base neutrality.

  • Transport: blood plasma carries dissolved amino acids, sugars, urea and small amounts of O₂/CO₂; in plants, xylem carries dissolved minerals and phloem carries dissolved sugars.
  • Hydrophobic molecules (e.g. lipids) do not dissolve in water — this insolubility is essential for cell membranes and for some functions. Lipids must be packaged with proteins into lipoproteins to be transported.
  • So water's solvent power is useful because it is polar, and the hydrophobic behaviour of lipids is useful because they are non-polar.

PHYSICAL PROPERTIES & AQUATIC ANIMALS

Buoyancy, viscosity, thermal conductivity and specific heat capacity differ between water and air, and drive adaptations of aquatic vs terrestrial animals (e.g. the black-throated loon and the ringed seal).

Buoyancywater is denser → greater upward forceloon has lighter bones to float and dive; seal has dense bones to stay submerged
Viscositywater is more viscous (resists flow)aquatic animals have streamlined bodies; loon's rear-set legs suit swimming, not walking

AHL · A1.1.7

THE ORIGIN OF EARTH'S WATER

Most of Earth's surface water is hypothesised to have come from asteroids, and was retained because the planet was large enough (gravity) and cool enough for the water to condense.

Water is essential for life: it lets non-polar lipids form membranes, stabilises internal conditions for homeostasis, dissolves the molecules needed for metabolism, and is itself a reagent (in condensation reactions).

When Earth first formed, water vapour was present but too hot to condense, and the planet's gravity was too weak to hold it — so most of it escaped to space. The large bodies of water we have are therefore thought to have arrived later from asteroids that formed further from the Sun, where it was cold enough for water to freeze as ice.

  • Origin: asteroids (icy bodies) that collided with Earth.
  • Retention: by then Earth had enough gravity to hold the water, and had cooled enough for it to condense and remain liquid.
AHL · A1.1.8

WATER & THE SEARCH FOR EXTRATERRESTRIAL LIFE

Liquid water is a key target in the search for habitable planets, because it is essential for life as we know it.

The Goldilocks zone (habitable zone) is the range of distances from a star at which temperatures allow liquid water to exist on a planet's surface. Its distance depends on the star's size and temperature — a hotter star pushes the zone further away.

Very few planets found in a Goldilocks zone also have a suitable mass and atmosphere to actually hold liquid water on the surface. That is why the search for extraterrestrial life focuses on finding liquid water.

FIG 6 — The Goldilocks (habitable) zone. Liquid water can exist at just the right distance from a star; too close and it boils, too far and it freezes.

10 questions. Pick an answer — instant feedback.

1. The bonds within a water molecule are…

Correct — oxygen pulls electrons more strongly, giving δ− and δ+ ends.
Hydrogen bonds are BETWEEN molecules; inside the molecule the bonds are polar covalent.

2. A hydrogen bond in water forms between…

Correct — opposite partial charges attract between neighbouring molecules.
Hydrogen bonds are between the δ+ H of one molecule and the δ− O of a different molecule.

3. A water strider can stand on water because of…

Correct — cohesive hydrogen bonds at the surface form a stretched film.
Surface tension (a cohesion effect) supports the strider, not adhesion or density.

4. Capillary rise in a narrow tube relies on…

Correct — water adheres to the wall and coheres to itself, climbing against gravity.
Capillary action needs BOTH adhesion (to the wall) and cohesion (molecule to molecule).

5. A substance that dissolves readily in water is described as…

Correct — hydrophilic ('water-loving') polar molecules and ions dissolve; hydrophobic ones do not.
Hydrophilic = water-loving and soluble; hydrophobic = water-hating and insoluble.

6. Water's tendency to stay at a stable temperature is due to its high…

Correct — breaking hydrogen bonds takes a lot of energy, so temperature rises slowly.
Specific heat capacity is the heat needed to raise temperature → stable temperatures.

7. Why does water exert a greater buoyant force than air?

Correct — buoyancy depends on the density of the medium.
Buoyancy is a result of water's greater density, not viscosity or heat capacity.

8. The ringed seal has dense bones mainly so it can…

Correct — denser bones reduce buoyancy so the seal can stay underwater.
Dense bones make the seal LESS buoyant so it can dive and stay submerged.

9. AHL — Earth's water is hypothesised to have originated mainly from…

Correct — early Earth was too hot and too weakly gravitating to keep its water, so water came from asteroids.
The accepted hypothesis is an extraplanetary (asteroid) origin for most of Earth's water.

10. AHL — The Goldilocks zone is…

Correct — it depends on the star's temperature and size, and is key to the search for life.
The Goldilocks (habitable) zone is where temperatures allow liquid water.
