CLUB SETPOINT

Your body runs the tightest nightclub in biology: no matter how cold, hot, sugary or salty the world outside gets, the dance floor inside stays at the perfect setting. Every DJ, bouncer and water-valve on this page exists for one job — drag the vibe back to the set point.

D3.3.1

THE SETTINGS

Homeostasis = keeping the internal environment inside preset limits, no matter what the outside world throws at you.

🎚️

Four dials the club never lets drift

internal environment · set point · tolerance limits · disease

Every cell in your body swims in an internal environment — the tissue fluid around it. Homeostasis is the maintenance of that internal environment, keeping variables within preset limits even when the external environment fluctuates wildly. In humans the big four are core temperature, blood pH, blood glucose and blood osmotic concentration.

If a variable drifts outside its narrow tolerance band, disease is the result — that's what "disrupted homeostasis" means. The whole point of being multicellular is that the internal environment can be regulated to stay near-optimal for every cell, which lets organisms live in hostile places (ice swimmers, desert camels, you).

FIG 1 — The four dials of the club. A set point is the level the body tries to hold (37°C, pH 7.4, ~5 mmol L⁻¹ glucose, ~300 mOsm); each variable swings inside a tolerance band without triggering a response.
D3.3.2

THE BOUNCER

Negative feedback = the bouncer who pushes the crowd back to the set point from above OR below. Positive feedback is the mosh pit that amplifies — great for childbirth, useless for stability.

🪪

Negative feedback: the reverse-gear rule

stimulus → receptor → control centre → effector → response

A change is detected by a receptor, a control centre (usually the brain or a gland) compares it against the set point, then an effector produces a response that reverses the change — pushing the variable back to the set point. Because the response opposes the stimulus, the loop is self-correcting: it works from values above AND below the set point.

Why negative and not positive for homeostasis? Negative feedback decreases the gap between current level and set point — it restores balance. Positive feedback increases the gap, amplifying the change until the stimulus is removed — it promotes change, not stability, so it's unsuitable for homeostasis (though perfect for childbirth contractions, ovulation and lactation).

FIG 2 — The bouncer's loop. Negative feedback reduces the gap to the set point and works from both directions; positive feedback grows the gap, so it's only used where an avalanche is wanted (birth, ovulation, milk let-down).
NEGATIVE

Reverse gear 🪪

Response opposes the stimulus. Returns the variable to set point from above or below. Basis of ALL homeostatic systems: thermoregulation, blood sugar, osmoregulation.

POSITIVE

Amplifier 🎸

Response reinforces the stimulus — the gap grows until the stimulus stops. Examples: childbirth (contraction → more stretching → stronger contraction), ovulation, lactation. Not for homeostasis.

Exam trap: the question "why negative rather than positive feedback?" wants exactly this — negative feedback decreases the gap between the variable and the set point and restores balance; positive feedback increases the gap and promotes change, which is the opposite of what homeostasis needs.
D3.3.3

THE SUGAR FLOOR

Blood glucose is a dance floor kept perfectly crowded: when it's too full, insulin clears people out; when it's too empty, glucagon lets more in.

🍬

Insulin and glucagon — the antagonistic DJ pair

islets of Langerhans · α cells · β cells · liver · target cells

Glucose is the fuel for ATP via cell respiration, but demand fluctuates — and high glucose in the blood is damaging (it makes the blood hypertonic and damages cells). Two antagonistic hormones from the islets of Langerhans in the pancreas keep it in range, acting mainly on the liver: insulin (β cells) lowers it, glucagon (α cells) raises it.

Hormones travel in the blood to reach their target cells, which carry specific receptors. The whole loop runs on negative feedback: high glucose → insulin → glucose falls → insulin secretion stops; low glucose → glucagon → glucose rises → glucagon stops.

FIG 3 — The sugar floor. Insulin (β cells) acts on the liver + fat: glycogen formation (glycogenesis), glucose uptake, faster respiration → glucose falls. Glucagon (α cells): glycogen breakdown (glycogenolysis), glucose release → glucose rises.
INSULIN · β CELLS

Lowers glucose 🍬⬇

Stimulates glucose → glycogen in liver & muscle; boosts glucose uptake by liver and adipose tissue; speeds up respiration. Secretion starts minutes after eating and lasts hours.

GLUCAGON · α CELLS

Raises glucose 🍬⬆

Stimulates glycogen → glucose in the liver (glycogenolysis); promotes glucose release from liver and fat; slows respiration to save glucose.

PANCREAS

Two glands in one 🫘

Mostly exocrine (digestive enzymes into ducts to the gut) + islands of endocrine tissue (islets of Langerhans) that pour hormones straight into the blood.

Exam gold: the textbook's glucose balance diagram lists four ways glucose enters the blood (absorption from gut; glycogen breakdown; production from amino acids/glycerol) and three ways it leaves (respiration; glycogen conversion; fat conversion). Insulin and glucagon simply turn those taps — they are antagonistic because they act in opposite directions on the same variable.
D3.3.4

THE SUGAR CRASH

Diabetes = the club that can't clear the dance floor. Type 1: the DJ (insulin) never shows up. Type 2: the speakers (receptors) are blown.

📉

Diabetes mellitus: hyperglycaemia that won't clear

type 1 · type 2 · risk factors · prevention · treatment

In diabetes, blood glucose stays elevated even during fasting. Normally insulin binds receptors on target cells and opens glucose channels; when the signal fails, glucose piles up in the blood → hyperglycaemia → glucose spills into urine, water reabsorption suffers, and the person is thirsty, tired, and urinating constantly.

Type 1: the body can't produce enough insulin — autoimmune destruction of β cells, early onset, treated with insulin injections (timing matters: insulin doesn't last long in the blood). Type 2: the body can't respond — insulin receptors down-regulated, later onset, managed with diet, exercise and weight loss.

FIG 4 — Type 1 = production failure (no insulin, β cells destroyed by the immune system); Type 2 = response failure (receptors down-regulated, insulin ignored). Both end in hyperglycaemia.

Nature of Science: the glucose tolerance test is a model — a standard glucose drink, then repeated blood glucose measurements. A diabetic curve peaks higher and takes far longer to fall back to baseline. Fasting glucose predicts higher-than-normal values in diabetes because the liver keeps releasing glucose and nothing is clearing it.
D3.3.5

THE THERMOSTAT

Core temperature is held near 37°C by a hypothalamus that reads the room, then switches on heaters or air-con via nerves and hormones.

🌡️

Set point 37°C, controlled by the hypothalamus

thermoreceptors · hypothalamus · TRH → TSH → thyroxin · muscle & adipose effectors

Temperature is detected by thermoreceptors: peripheral ones in the skin (which let you react before the core even changes — you feel the cold door handle, not just a cold core) and central ones deep in the body. The hypothalamus is the integrating centre: it compares blood temperature against the 37°C set point.

When the core is too cold, the hypothalamus works with the pituitary to boost thyroxin: hypothalamus secretes TRH (tripeptide) → anterior pituitary secretes TSH (glycoprotein hormone) → thyroid gland secretes thyroxin (contains iodine) → increases metabolic rate in liver, muscle and brain → more heat generated. The effectors are muscle and adipose tissue.

FIG 5 — Cold chain: thermoreceptors → hypothalamus (TRH, a tripeptide) → anterior pituitary (TSH, a glycoprotein) → thyroid (thyroxin, iodine-containing) → faster metabolism in liver/muscle/brain → heat. Notice thyroxin is a hormonal pathway — slower but long-lasting, layered on top of instant nervous shivering.
Remember: TRH is a tripeptide (3 amino acids), TSH is a glycoprotein, thyroxin contains iodine — these are classic data-about-hormones questions. Iodine deficiency → no thyroxin → low metabolic rate → feeling cold (goitre).
D3.3.6

HEAT CONTROL

When you're too hot: vasodilation + sweating. Too cold: vasoconstriction, shivering, hair raising, and brown fat burning without ATP.

❄️🔥

Effector moves — the rapid-response toolkit

vasodilation · vasoconstriction · shivering · sweating · piloerection · brown fat

Too hot? Skin arterioles dilate (vasodilation) → more warm blood at the surface → heat radiates away (and skin looks pink). Sweat glands secrete sweat; evaporation pulls off latent heat — the most powerful cooling tool. Too cold? Arterioles constrict (vasoconstriction) → less blood at the surface → less heat loss (skin looks pale). Shivering: rapid involuntary muscle contractions generate heat from the effort. Piloerection: hair muscles raise hairs — a dead end in humans (traps a thin air layer if you're furry).

The heavyweight: uncoupled respiration in brown adipose tissue — mitochondria burn fuel but make no ATP, releasing all the energy as heat. Vital in new-borns and hibernating mammals.

FIG 6 — Hot toolkit: dilated arterioles + sweat (up to 2 L/hr) shed heat. Cold toolkit: constricted arterioles + shivering + piloerection + brown-fat uncoupled respiration, where mitochondria burn fuel without making ATP so all the energy becomes heat.
Exam trap: arterioles don't "move" — only their diameter changes, which alters blood flow through skin capillaries. And vasodilation alone is mild; sweating is the serious cooler (that's why humid heat is miserable — sweat can't evaporate).
AHL · D3.3.7

THE KIDNEY CLUB

The kidneys are the club's two bouncers at the back door: they chuck out toxic waste (excretion) and hold the door policy on water (osmoregulation).

🫘

Excretion vs osmoregulation — two jobs, one organ

urea · osmolarity · nephron · cortex · medulla

Excretion = removing metabolic waste from the blood. The big one is urea (made in the liver from excess amino acids — deamination), plus excess salt and water. Osmoregulation = controlling the osmotic concentration of body fluids, measured in osmoles per litre (osmol L⁻¹). A solute gradient in the kidney medulla drives water reabsorption.

The working unit is the nephron — about a million per kidney. Each nephron: Bowman's capsule + glomerulus (filter), proximal convoluted tubule (reabsorption), loop of Henle (salt ladder), distal convoluted tubule + collecting duct (final tuning under ADH). The whole system filters ~180 L of fluid per day and returns almost all of it — you pee only ~1.5 L.

FIG 7 — The nephron: filter at the top (glomerulus + Bowman's capsule, cortex), reabsorption along the PCT, a salt-ladder loop of Henle diving into the medulla, and the ADH-tuned collecting duct. The vasa recta is the blood vessel hugging the loop so salts don't leak away.

Units matter: exam answers that write "concentration" get marked down — write osmol L⁻¹ (osmolarity). The medulla gradient runs roughly 300 mOsm L⁻¹ (cortex) up to ~1200 mOsm L⁻¹ (deep medulla) — that gradient is what pulls water out of the collecting duct.
AHL · D3.3.8

THE FILTER

Blood is pushed through a high-pressure filter at the glomerulus, then everything valuable gets grabbed back in the proximal tubule.

🕸️

Ultrafiltration, then selective reabsorption

glomerulus · fenestrations · basement membrane · podocytes · PCT · microvilli

Ultrafiltration at the glomerulus: blood arrives under high pressure (the afferent arteriole is wider than the efferent), so fluid is forced through the capillary wall into Bowman's capsule. The filter is three layers: fenestrated capillary endothelium (pores ~100 nm), the basement membrane (glycoprotein mesh — the real filter; it's negatively charged so it repels plasma proteins), and podocyte foot processes (slit pores). Result: water, ions, glucose, amino acids and urea pass; blood cells, platelets and plasma proteins stay behind.

Selective reabsorption in the PCT: all glucose and amino acids, ~80% of water and mineral ions are pumped back into the blood. The PCT is built for it: microvilli (huge surface area), tight junctions (nothing leaks between cells — everything goes through them), many mitochondria (energy for active transport). Glucose is moved by cotransport with Na⁺.

FIG 8 — The filter stack: fenestrations (big pores) → basement membrane (charged glycoprotein mesh that actually decides) → podocyte slits (small gaps). What passes: water, ions, glucose, amino acids, urea. What stays: cells, platelets, plasma proteins.
Exam gold — "why does the basement membrane block plasma proteins?" Two reasons: pore size and negative charge repelling the negatively charged proteins (and most are simply too big). That's why kidney damage shows protein in urine — the filter has failed.
AHL · D3.3.9

THE SALT LADDER

The loop of Henle builds a salty escalator in the medulla — the gradient that later lets the collecting duct drain water out of the filtrate.

🪜

Countercurrent multiplier: climbing to 1200 mOsm

descending limb · ascending limb · active transport · countercurrent · vasa recta

The loop of Henle has two limbs with opposite personalities. The descending limb lets water leave but not salts — so the filtrate gets saltier as it descends. The ascending limb lets salts leave but not water — Na⁺ is pumped out by active transport (each pump cycle pushing ~200 mOsm of salt out). The two limbs run side by side, so the salt pumped out of the ascending limb makes the surrounding fluid saltier, which pulls more water out of the descending limb, making it even saltier, which lets the ascending limb pump even more… multiplied, round after round.

Result: the medulla interstitial fluid runs 300 mOsm L⁻¹ at the cortex down to ~1200 mOsm L⁻¹ deep in the medulla. The vasa recta (blood vessel alongside the loop) flows in the same countercurrent pattern so it doesn't wash the gradient away. This gradient is what the collecting duct uses in Act 10.

FIG 9 — Opposite limbs, one multiplier: descending = water out (salts stay), ascending = salts out (water stays). Each pass boosts the medulla gradient toward 1200 mOsm L⁻¹, and the vasa recta's parallel flow stops blood from rinsing it away.
Exam gold — "explain how the loop of Henle creates the gradient": (1) ascending limb actively transports Na⁺ out, (2) that salt raises the osmolarity of medulla fluid, (3) the descending limb responds by losing water → filtrate gets saltier, (4) the saltier filtrate lets the ascending limb pump out even more Na⁺, (5) repeated cycling multiplies the gradient to ~1200 mOsm L⁻¹. Mention countercurrent and the vasa recta to top out the marks.
AHL · D3.3.10

THE WATER BOSS

ADH is the bouncer at the door of the collecting duct: when you're dehydrated he opens more water channels; when you're topped up he lets water walk out.

🚰

ADH: osmoreceptors → pituitary → aquaporins

osmoreceptors · hypothalamus · posterior pituitary · aquaporins · collecting duct

Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus monitor blood osmolarity. If it rises (dehydrated — you're salty), they signal the posterior pituitary to release ADH (antidiuretic hormone, also called vasopressin). ADH travels to the kidney and makes the collecting duct more permeable to water: aquaporin channels stored in vesicles move to and fuse with the cell membrane, so water can escape the filtrate into the salty medulla and be reabsorbed. More ADH → more aquaporins → less water in urine → concentrated, low-volume urine.

Drink lots? Osmolarity falls → less ADH → aquaporins return to vesicles → dilute urine. That's the whole loop — negative feedback on blood osmolarity.

FIG 10 — The water boss: high osmolarity → osmoreceptors → posterior pituitary → ADH → aquaporin vesicles fuse into the collecting duct wall → water is dragged out by the medulla's salt gradient → concentrated urine. Negative feedback shuts ADH off as osmolarity falls.
Mnemonic — "Are De Hydrated": ADH is secreted when you ARE DEhydrated. And remember: ADH is made in the hypothalamus, released by the posterior pituitary — an exam favourite.
Data question skill: given urine volume + osmolarity curves, high urine osmolarity + low volume = high ADH; low osmolarity + high volume = low ADH. Aquaporin density in the duct wall rises with ADH — that's the mechanism questions want.
AHL · D3.3.11

THE BLOOD ROUTER

The heart pumps the same blood every minute — the art is who gets it. Arterioles are the traffic lights that reroute flow to whatever organ is working.

🚦

Blood flow follows demand

cardiac output · arterioles · precapillary sphincters · shunt vessels

Cardiac output (blood pumped per minute) rises with activity — from ~4 L min⁻¹ asleep to ~5 at rest to ~25 L min⁻¹ in hard exercise. But the brain always gets its share: ~20% of cardiac output at all times, because it can't store fuel. Blood is distributed by arteriole diameter: vasodilation opens a capillary bed, vasoconstriction shuts it; precapillary sphincters are the valves at each bed, and shunt vessels bypass beds entirely.

During exercise: muscle beds open wide; gut and kidneys constrict (digestion and urine can wait). In sleep: muscles shut down, kidneys reduce flow (no midnight toilet trips), and the brain gets extra during REM. Wakeful rest sits in between.

FIG 11 — Blood routing: muscle gets a flood in exercise, gut/kidneys are throttled back; sleep reroutes away from muscle and kidneys toward the brain (especially in REM). The brain's ~20% share never drops.
Nature of Science: the textbook data come from invasive catheter studies plus modern non-invasive flow imaging — a classic story of measurement technology changing what we can claim about the body. Expect a data question: identify which organ a line on a graph represents by matching its pattern (muscle = exercise spike, brain = flat, gut/kidney = reciprocal).

Bouncer check

THE TEST ✍️

10 questions. Pick an answer and you'll instantly see why.

1. Homeostasis is best described as…

✅ Set points have tolerance bands — the variable swings slightly without triggering a response, and responses push it back. "Perfectly constant" is wrong; so is matching the outside.
❌ Homeostasis keeps variables near a set point within preset limits — it doesn't freeze them, and it actively resists the external environment.

2. Why does homeostasis rely on negative feedback?

✅ Negative feedback responses oppose the stimulus and shrink the gap to the set point — that's exactly what stability needs. Positive feedback grows the gap, which suits childbirth, not homeostasis.
❌ Amplifying is positive feedback — the opposite. Negative feedback decreases the gap and restores balance.

3. After a sugary meal, blood glucose falls because…

✅ Insulin (β cells, islets of Langerhans) clears glucose: liver converts it to glycogen, tissues take it up, respiration speeds up. Glucagon does the opposite and only appears when glucose is LOW.
❌ Glucagon RAISES glucose (glycogen → glucose), and healthy kidneys don't leak glucose into urine — that's a diabetes symptom.

4. Type 2 diabetes is fundamentally…

✅ Type 2 = response failure: insulin is made but target cells don't respond (down-regulated receptors). Risk factors: sugary/fatty diet, obesity, no exercise, genetics. Managed with diet + exercise + weight loss.
❌ That's Type 1 (autoimmune, no insulin, injections). ADH failure is diabetes INSIPIDUS, not mellitus — a different disease entirely.

5. The control centre for temperature regulation is…

✅ Hypothalamus = integrating centre (compares vs set point). It triggers TRH → TSH → thyroxin for long-term heat, and nervous responses for instant heat control. The thyroid is just an effector; skin receptors are inputs.
❌ The thyroid is an effector in the chain; the skin holds receptors (inputs). The comparing and deciding happens in the hypothalamus.

6. Brown adipose tissue generates heat by…

✅ Brown fat mitochondria run "uncoupled" respiration: no ATP is produced, so the energy comes straight out as heat. Crucial in new-borns and hibernators; shivering is muscle-based and piloerection barely works in humans.
❌ Shivering is skeletal muscle, and hair-raising is a dead end in humans. Brown fat's trick is burning fuel without making ATP.

7. In ultrafiltration, the structure that actually blocks plasma proteins is…

✅ The basement membrane is the real filter: its negative charge repels the negatively charged plasma proteins (plus size matters). Fenestrations are big; podocyte slits are gaps, not a solid wall.
❌ Fenestrations are ~100 nm pores (big enough to let almost anything through). It's the charged basement membrane that does the selecting.

8. The loop of Henle builds the medulla salt gradient because…

✅ Countercurrent multiplier: ascending pumps Na⁺ out (active), that salt pulls water from the descending limb, the saltier filtrate lets the ascending pump even more — the gradient multiplies to ~1200 mOsm L⁻¹.
❌ No evaporation in the kidney; ADH controls water permeability of the collecting duct, not medulla salt. The gradient is built by the loop's two opposite limbs.

9. When you're dehydrated, ADH causes…

✅ ADH (from the posterior pituitary, made in the hypothalamus) puts aquaporins into the collecting duct wall; water flows out into the salty medulla and is reabsorbed. More ADH = more aquaporins = concentrated urine.
❌ Dehydration means MORE water retention, so MORE aquaporins. Fewer aquaporins = dilute urine (well-hydrated state).

10. Which organ's blood supply stays at ~20% of cardiac output in ALL states?

✅ The brain always gets ~20% of cardiac output (asleep, awake, exercising). Muscle floods in exercise but rests in sleep; gut and kidneys throttle back when the body is busy.
❌ Muscle and gut are exactly the organs that get rerouted depending on activity. The brain's ~20% is non-negotiable.
