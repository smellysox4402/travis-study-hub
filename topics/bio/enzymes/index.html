C1.1.1

ENZYMES AS CATALYSTS

A catalyst increases the rate of a chemical reaction but is not changed by it. Enzymes are biological catalysts: they let the substrates through the door and turn them into products — and the enzyme is never used up, so one enzyme works the whole night, again and again.

🚪

What is an enzyme, and why is it never used up?

catalyst · biological catalyst · substrate → product · reuse

A catalyst is a substance that increases the rate of a chemical reaction but is not changed by the reaction. Because catalysts are not used up, they can catalyse reactions many times — only small amounts are needed relative to the quantity of reactants. Platinum is an inorganic example: it sits in car catalytic converters, converting unburned hydrocarbons in exhaust gases into carbon dioxide and water.

Enzymes are biological catalysts, made by living cells to speed up biochemical reactions. The general equation: enzyme + substrate → enzyme + products — the enzyme comes out the other side exactly as it went in. If cells didn't make enzymes, respiration, digestion, growth and movement would all crawl at ambient temperatures. Different enzymes accelerate reactions by different amounts — data-based questions love the ratios (up to ~10⁸ for OMP decarboxylase).

FIG 1 — The enzyme's loop: substrate → product through the enzyme, with the enzyme unchanged and reusable. Platinum (in catalytic converters) is an inorganic catalyst; enzymes are the biological ones.
Exam gold: a catalyst's defining property is not being changed by the reaction — that's why it can be reused and why only tiny amounts are needed. Rate data questions (with/without enzyme, e.g. carbonic anhydrase ~10⁶) test whether you can calculate and compare the ratio of rates.
C1.1.2

ROLE OF ENZYMES IN METABOLISM

Metabolism is the cell's whole operation — a complex network of interdependent chemical reactions. Almost every step is staffed by a enzyme (enzyme), so substrate specificity (control over metabolism) is really a staffing decision: make more of an enzyme and that reaction runs hotter.

🕸

Thousands of reactions, one enzyme per step

metabolism · metabolic pathway · chains and cycles · enzyme specificity

Metabolism is the complex network of interdependent and interacting chemical reactions that occurs in living organisms. Most happen inside cells, but some are extracellular — like digestion of food in the intestine. Reactions are organised into pathways: one type of molecule is transformed into another by a series of small steps. Most pathways are chains, but some are cycles — the urea cycle (5 reactions, 5 enzymes) is the textbook example.

Almost all metabolic reactions are catalysed by an enzyme, and enzymes are specific: each catalyses one reaction or a small group. That's a huge difference from platinum, which catalyses many different reactions. So organisms must make huge numbers of enzymes — hundreds even in a simple prokaryote, thousands in a liver cell. Because a cell can make more or less enzyme, it can control the rate of each reaction, its metabolism, and therefore its activities and chemical composition.

FIG 2 — Pathways are chains or cycles of small steps; each step needs its own specific enzyme. Urea cycle: 5 reactions, 5 enzymes. Control = hiring more or fewer enzymes (making more/less enzyme).
Don't mix up: specificity is what forces cells to make thousands of enzymes — one enzyme per reaction (or small group). A non-biological catalyst like platinum gets away with catalysing many reactions; enzymes don't.
C1.1.3

ANABOLIC & CATABOLIC REACTIONS

Metabolism has two paths: anabolism assembles bigger molecules from small ones using ATP; the catabolism (catabolism) smash big molecules into small ones and bank the energy. Condensation builds, hydrolysis breaks — and water tells you which is which.

🔨

Anabolism uses energy; tearing down old sets releases it

anabolism · catabolism · condensation · hydrolysis · ATP coupling

Anabolic reactions build smaller molecules into larger ones and require energy. Macromolecules are made from monomers in condensation reactions — water is the by-product — using energy from ATP. Examples: protein synthesis (translation) by ribosomes, DNA replication, synthesis of complex carbohydrates (starch, cellulose, glycogen), and photosynthesis (CO₂ + H₂O → larger molecules, powered by light).

Catabolic reactions break larger molecules into smaller ones and release energy — in some cases captured by coupling the reaction to ATP synthesis. Examples: digestion of food (mouth, stomach, small intestine), cell respiration (glucose or lipids oxidised to CO₂ and water), and decomposers breaking down dead organic matter. Mnemonic: think of the "anabolic steroids" misused for body building — anabolism builds.

FIG 3 — Anabolism (condensation, ATP in, H₂O out). Catabolism = catabolism (hydrolysis, energy out, ATP banked).
Remember: both halves of metabolism run on enzyme enzymes too — the same enzymes that speed everything up. The glycogen question from the book: making glycogen from glucose phosphate is anabolic (building a macromolecule, needs energy), and two different enzymes are needed because glycogen has two kinds of bonds (1,4 and 1,6).
C1.1.4

ENZYMES: GLOBULAR PROTEINS WITH AN ACTIVE SITE

Enzymes are globular proteins with a precise 3D structure. The active site — the door — is only a few amino acids, but they are brought together from far apart in the chain by folding. Mess up the folding and you mess up the door.

🚪

A door built from a few amino acids that aren't even neighbours

globular protein · active site · folding · 3D structure

Enzymes are globular proteins with precise three-dimensional structure and chemical properties that let them act as catalysts. The substrate binds to a special region on the enzyme's surface called the active site — the door. The shape and chemical properties of the active site and the substrate match each other, so the substrate binds while most other substances cannot.

Typically just a few amino acids at the active site create the chemical conditions that convert substrates into products. Those amino acids are often not next to each other in the polypeptide — they're brought together by the folding of the whole protein. That's why the overall 3D structure is crucial: alter any part of the enzyme and the active site may change, and catalysis is unlikely to happen.

FIG 4 — The active site: a few amino acids (e.g. positions 42 and 217 — far apart in the sequence) folded together into a door whose shape and chemistry match the substrate. Sucrase-isomaltase: 12 amino acids form its active site.
Exam gold: "a few amino acids only" is the syllabus phrase — and the twist is that they aren't adjacent in the polypeptide; folding brings them together. That's why denaturation anywhere in the protein can kill catalysis: the door is only as good as the building.
C1.1.5

SUBSTRATE-ACTIVE SITE BINDING (INDUCED FIT)

It was once called lock-and-key — a rigid key slotting into a rigid lock. That model is wrong. Binding is induced fit: the enzyme shifts his stance and the substrate leans in — BOTH change shape, bond angles and bond lengths alter, and only then does the chemistry happen.

🕺

Not a lock and key — a handshake that changes both hands

induced-fit binding · bond angles · both molecules change · catalytic cycle

As a substrate approaches the active site, its direction of movement is random — until it is close enough for the enzyme surface's chemical properties to attract it towards the active site. Then it binds. The old "key in a lock" model is inappropriate: interactions between substrate and active site cause both to change — bond angles and bond lengths are altered, changing the 3D shapes of both molecules. That's induced-fit binding. If there's a second substrate, it binds another part of the active site, again with mutual change (hexokinase: glucose then phosphate → glucose phosphate).

The changes to the substrate make it easier for bonds within it to break and new bonds to form — substrates convert to products, which detach. The active site then returns to its original state, empty and ready for the next substrate: the catalytic cycle repeats.

FIG 5 — Induced fit: approach → mutual shape change (bond angles altered) → product release, active site restored. The lock-and-key model fails because it treats both shapes as rigid.
Don't mix up: induced fit is NOT the substrate bending to fit a fixed site. The syllabus says it explicitly: both substrate and enzyme change shape — the enzyme surface attracts the substrate, and the handshake changes both hands.
C1.1.6

MOLECULAR MOTION & COLLISIONS

Nothing reaches the door without moving. Molecules in a liquid jiggle randomly; when a substrate molecule happens to collide with an active site at the right angle, it can bind. More molecules or more heat = more collisions = more entries. Sometimes the enzyme is immobilised (immobilised) — then the substrate must do all the moving.

🕺

Random motion, right angle, right moment

molecular motion · substrate–active site collision · orientation · immobilised enzymes

A substrate can only bind if it moves very close to the active site — a substrate–active site collision. These aren't high-velocity impacts like car crashes: in a liquid, molecules are packed together but free to move, changing direction repeatedly and at random. The collision rate rises if there are more substrate or enzyme molecules (higher concentration) or if the temperature rises (faster molecular motion).

At a collision the substrate may be at any angle — only aligned collisions succeed. Short-range chemical forces draw substrates in or adjust their orientation, but only when already very close. Who moves depends on size: in the cytoplasm both are dissolved, but the substrate is usually smaller, so it moves more. Very large substrates don't move — the enzyme moves instead (DNA replication/transcription enzymes do this). Some enzymes are embedded in membranes and immobilised — then the substrate does all the movement.

FIG 6 — Random molecular motion brings substrates near active sites; only aligned collisions bind. Concentration ↑ or temperature ↑ → collision rate ↑. Membrane-embedded enzymes are immobilised — the substrate moves.
Exam gold: collision rate vs success rate — the syllabus wants both: higher concentration/temperature raises the number of collisions, but only correctly oriented collisions succeed. Big substrates (DNA) flip it: the enzyme moves to them.
C1.1.7

SPECIFICITY, STRUCTURE & DENATURATION

The shape and chemistry of the active site decide which substrates can bind: some doors are absolute (glucokinase admits glucose only), others are broad (hexokinase admits any hexose). But the active site's structure depends on weak bonds, so heat or acidity can wreck it — that's denaturation, a enzyme too exhausted to work, permanently.

🎫

Enzyme–substrate specificity, and the enzyme who collapses

specificity · glucokinase vs hexokinase · weak interactions · denaturation

Enzyme–substrate specificity: the active site's shape and chemistry allow substrate molecules to bind but not other substances. Some enzymes are absolutely specific — glucose is the only substrate that binds glucokinase. Others are less specific: hexokinase binds any of a group of hexose sugars. Proteases are broad — a few types can digest polypeptides with any amino acid sequence.

The enzyme's precise 3D shape depends on weak interactions between amino acids — hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonds. Heat and acidity disrupt these, so enzymes are easily altered. Even a change far from the active site ripples through the protein and changes the door. Small changes prevent binding or catalysis; if the changes are too great to reverse, the enzyme is denatured — permanently off duty.

FIG 7 — Glucokinase's door admits only glucose; hexokinase's admits any hexose (G, F, M…); denaturation crumples the door permanently. Proteases are broader still — a few types digest any polypeptide.
Don't mix up: denaturation ≠ reversible shape wobble. It's the irreversible loss of the enzyme's precise structure when weak bonds break. And specificity is graded — absolute (glucokinase), grouped (hexokinase), broad (proteases) — not one-size-fits-all.
C1.1.8

FACTORS AFFECTING ENZYME ACTIVITY

Three factors affect enzymes: temperature (heat speeds the rate, then denatures the enzyme), pH (a logarithmic scale — one unit is a tenfold change in acidity), and substrate concentration (more substrates, faster entry — until every door is busy and the line can't move faster).

🌡

Two effects of temperature, one logarithmic pH scale, one saturating curve

optimum temperature · denaturation · logarithmic pH · saturation · Bacillus licheniformis

Temperature acts twice: heating gives molecules kinetic energy → more collisions → faster activity; but it also makes bonds in the enzyme vibrate and break → denaturation. The result is a peak at the optimum temperature — which is not always 40°C and can be much higher for enzymes of organisms adapted to hot environments. pH is logarithmic: one unit lower = 10× more acidic (pH 5 is 10× more acidic than pH 6). Deviating from the optimum pH alters ionic bonds between amino acids, changing the active site; beyond a point the enzyme is irreversibly denatured. Most enzymes peak near pH 7, but not all — the protease of Bacillus licheniformis has an optimum between pH 9 and 10, which is exactly why it's cultured for alkaline biological laundry detergents.

Substrate concentration: more substrate → more collisions → faster rate. But active sites occupy as soon as they bind, so as concentration rises, more collisions are blocked by occupied doors: the curve rises less and less steeply and never quite reaches a maximum — saturation of active sites.

FIG 8 — Three factors: temperature (peak at optimum, denaturation beyond), pH (logarithmic, one unit = 10×; detergent protease optima 9–10), substrate concentration (saturating curve that never quite reaches a maximum).
Exam gold: two classic traps. (1) pH is logarithmic — pH 4 is 100× more acidic than pH 6, not 2×. (2) The substrate curve never quite reaches a maximum — say "approaches a plateau as active sites saturate", never "reaches a maximum". And optimum temperature is not always 40°C — that phrase is examinable.
C1.1.9

MEASURING ENZYME-CATALYSED REACTIONS

Scien tific method rules: change one thing (independent variable), keep everything else fixed (control variables), and measure what happens (dependent variable). Reaction rate = amount of chemical changed ÷ time — in millimoles per second. The catalase experiment is the house special: hydrogen peroxide in, water and oxygen out, oxygen collected in a measuring cylinder.

🧪

Variables, rates, and the catalase special

independent · control · dependent · mmol s−1 · catalase · reliability

Independent variables are deliberately varied to see the effect — commonly temperature, substrate concentration, enzyme concentration or pH. Control variables are kept constant for a "fair test" and monitored regularly. Dependent variables are what you measure to calculate reaction rate — often called enzyme activity. Repeat experiments to show reliability (consistent results).

Rate = change in amount of chemical ÷ time, e.g. millimoles per second (mmol s⁻¹). Two approaches: (1) run a fixed short time and measure substrate used or product formed; (2) start with a known amount and time the reaction to completion. The house experiment: yeast (or macerated liver, kidney, germinating seeds) provides catalase, which converts toxic hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen — oxygen volume in a measuring cylinder gives the rate. Safety goggles are non-negotiable.

FIG 9 — The catalase special: H₂O₂ → H₂O + O₂, oxygen volume timed and measured. Rate = amount ÷ time (mmol s⁻¹). Independent varied, controls constant, dependent measured.
Remember: percentage concentrations — 100 cm³ of a 1% starch solution contains 1 g of starch; grams per 100 cm³ can be expressed as a percentage. And "rate" questions want units: mmol s⁻¹, g min⁻¹, or similar — always divide quantity by time.
C1.1.10

LOWERING ACTIVATION ENERGY

Before substrates become products, they must climb an energy hill — the activation energy, needed to reach the transition state. The enzyme's real trick: he doesn't add energy, he LOWERS the hill. Same start, same finish, smaller climb — and the reaction starts a million times faster.

⛰

Same net energy, smaller activation energy

transition state · activation energy · bond breaking/making · million-fold

Chemical reactions aren't single-step: substrates pass through a transition state before becoming products. Energy is required to reach it — the activation energy — and it's used to break bonds in the substrate. Energy is then released as new bonds form. In an exothermic reaction the net release is greater than the activation energy.

With an enzyme, the net amount of energy released is unchanged — but the activation energy is smaller, because bonds in the substrate are weakened as it binds to the active site, so less energy is needed to break them. Result: the rate rises — typically by a factor of a million or more. That's why the same reaction that takes geological time without an enzyme takes milliseconds with one.

FIG 10 — Enzymes don't change the net energy of the reaction — they shrink the activation energy by weakening substrate bonds at the active site, so the same reaction runs ~10⁶× (or more) faster.
Exam gold: "explain how lowering activation energy increases rate" — with a lower hill, a much larger fraction of substrate molecules have enough energy to reach the transition state at any moment, so more successfully convert to product per unit time. Net ΔE between substrate and product is unchanged.
C1.1.11

INTRACELLULAR VS EXTRACELLULAR ENZYME REACTIONS

Some enzymes work inside the cell (intracellular — glycolysis in the cytoplasm, Krebs in the mitochondria); others work the street outside (extracellular exoenzymes — digestion in the gut). The uniform even tells you where they work: ER-bound ribosomes build exoenzymes, free ribosomes build intracellular ones.

🌃

Who works where, and who built them

exoenzymes · ER ribosomes vs free ribosomes · glycolysis · Krebs · fumarase · gut digestion

Extracellular enzymes (exoenzymes) are released from the cell and work outside it — synthesized by ribosomes attached to the endoplasmic reticulum. In humans they do the digestion of solid food in the digestive system, breaking down macromolecules so the monomers can cross the plasma membrane. Intracellular enzymes are made by free ribosomes in the cytoplasm and catalyse pathways such as glycolysis — its first step, adding phosphate to glucose, is catalysed by hexokinase in the cytoplasm. Some work inside organelles: Krebs cycle enzymes in the mitochondrial matrix, e.g. fumarase converting fumarate to malate.

Unicellular heterotrophic archaea, bacteria and fungi can't take in macromolecules — their cell walls block endocytosis — so they secrete exoenzymes to digest food outside the cell and absorb the monomers across the plasma membrane. Bonus case: cystic fibrosis blocks the pancreatic duct with mucus, so digestive enzymes can't reach the small intestine — enzyme pills restore digestion.

FIG 11 — Intracellular enzymes (glycolysis/hexokinase in cytoplasm; Krebs/fumarase in mitochondrial matrix) built by free ribosomes. Street enzymes (exoenzymes) built on ER ribosomes, working outside — gut digestion, and microbial feeding.
Don't mix up: the ribosome story is examinable — ER ribosomes → extracellular enzymes, free ribosomes → intracellular enzymes. And don't forget fumarase: fumarate → malate, Krebs cycle, mitochondrial matrix.
C1.1.12

HEAT GENERATION BY METABOLISM

Energy conversion is never 100% efficient — some of it always leaks out as heat. That is the body heat that keeps the body warm. Birds and mammals run on it: they shiver, burn brown fat with uncoupled respiration, and huddle (emperor penguins) to share the warmth.

🔥

Wasted energy? For warm-blooded enzymes, it's the heating bill

heat from metabolism · shivering · brown fat · uncoupled respiration · emperor penguins

The conversion of energy from one form to another is never 100% efficient: in metabolic reactions, products contain less energy than reactants, and the additional energy is ultimately converted to heat. Birds and mammals use this metabolic heat to keep body temperature above the environment. When there's too much heat — during exercise — humans sweat and use evaporative cooling to dump the excess.

When basal metabolism doesn't release enough heat, animals raise their metabolic rate. A human whose core temperature falls experiences shivering — involuntary muscle contractions that generate heat. Many mammals have brown fat tissue: its cells carry many mitochondria that perform uncoupled respiration — oxidising substrates to generate heat without producing ATP. And in very cold conditions, emperor penguins huddle together to take advantage of the metabolic heat released by their neighbours. Even compost heaps steam — microbial metabolism at work.

FIG 12 — Metabolic heat: inevitable because energy transfer is never 100% efficient. Used for warmth (shivering, brown fat uncoupled respiration, penguin huddles), dumped when excess (sweating).
Exam gold: the phrase "uncoupled respiration" = oxidising substrates to make heat WITHOUT producing ATP — that's what brown fat mitochondria do (why infants, with relatively more brown fat, need it: they can't shiver effectively). Connect heat to the enzyme theme: higher temperature → faster molecular motion → faster catalysis.
C1.1.13

CYCLICAL & LINEAR METABOLIC PATHWAYS

Metabolic pathways are mostly linear chains of small steps — glycolysis runs nine reactions), and a few are cycles (like Krebs and Calvin) where every intermediate is both a product and a substrate, and some pathways branch like specific pathways.

🪩

Nine small steps, or a loop that never ends

glycolysis · nine reactions · Calvin cycle · Krebs cycle · branching

Most enzyme-catalysed reactions cause only a small change in a substrate; large transformations happen in sequences of small steps — metabolic pathways (like the word game TREAD→BREAD→BREED…). Glycolysis converts glucose to pyruvate through nine different reactions, each catalysed by a different enzyme. In all but one step one molecule becomes one molecule; the exception splits a six-carbon sugar into two three-carbon sugars — so the later stages of the chain run twice per glucose molecule.

Branching is very common — metabolism overall is a network of interlinked pathways. Cycles are rarer: in a cycle every intermediate is a product of one reaction and a substrate of another, so the loop turns indefinitely. The big two: the Calvin cycle (photosynthesis) and the Krebs cycle (respiration) — plus the urea cycle already met.

FIG 13 — Linear pathways (glycolysis: 9 reactions, one split, later steps ×2 per glucose) vs cyclical loops (Krebs, Calvin, urea) where every intermediate is both product and substrate.
Remember: the word-game analogy (TREAD→BREAD→BREED…) is the book's own model for pathways: each step is a small change, each step needs its enzyme. Cycles "close the loop" — no end product, the intermediates are regenerated.
C1.1.14

ALLOSTERIC SITES & NON-COMPETITIVE INHIBITION

Beyond the main door (active site), many enzymes carry a second door at the back — the allosteric site. It is allosteric regulation: when a specific substance binds the allosteric site, the enzyme's shape changes and the active site closes. The substrate never competes — this is non-competitive inhibition, and it's reversible.

🎛

Allosteric regulation that reshapes the whole enzyme

allosteric site · conformational change · activation or inhibition · non-competitive · reversible

Every enzyme has an active site, but many have a second binding site where a different specific substance can bind and unbind. Because binding changes the enzyme's shape, it's called an allosteric site. Allosteric sites exist because they let the enzyme be regulated: switching between the two states alters the structure and properties of the active site.

In some cases allosteric binding activates the enzyme; in others it prevents catalysis — reversible inhibition. Substances that inhibit by binding the allosteric site rather than the active site do not compete with substrates, so they're called non-competitive inhibitors. Only specific substances fit the allosteric site — it's another substrate-specific site.

FIG 14 — Allosteric site = manager's override switch: a specific substance binds at the back, the enzyme changes shape, the front door slams. Non-competitive = no competition with substrate, reversible.
Exam gold: "non-competitive" literally means the inhibitor doesn't compete for the active site — so raising substrate concentration cannot overcome it. Compare with competitive inhibition's graph: non-competitive lowers the maximum rate; competitive shifts the curve right.
C1.1.15

COMPETITIVE INHIBITION

Competitive inhibitors are competitive inhibitors who look exactly like the substrates. They sit in the active site reversibly, blocking the real substrates. But they can be outcompeted: raise the substrate concentration and the competitive inhibitor loses the race. Statins are the famous example — lookalikes for HMG-CoA, blocking cholesterol production.

🕵️

Same face, same door, reversible — and beatable

competitive inhibitor · structurally similar · reversible · statins · HMG-CoA reductase · sarin

Competitive inhibitors bind to the active site and, while bound, block the substrate. They are structurally similar to the substrate, so they fit the same door — but unlike the substrate they are not converted into products, so they remain bound longer. With a vacant active site, substrate or inhibitor can bind — whichever arrives first wins. More inhibitor → more inhibition; but if inhibitor concentration is low and substrate concentration rises, inhibition shrinks until the enzyme is effectively uninhibited — real substrates simply outnumber the fakes.

Statins work this way: they bind the active site of HMG-CoA reductase (3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A reductase), which catalyses the rate-limiting step of cholesterol synthesis in liver cells — lower that step, less cholesterol is made, so dosage must be tuned. Also in the family: sarin, a competitive inhibitor of acetylcholinesterase — nerve agent chemistry with a dark history.

FIG 15 — Competitive inhibitors that look like substrates sit in the door (reversibly). Flood with real substrate and they're outcompeted — unlike non-competitive inhibitors, which substrate concentration can't overcome. Statins: HMG-CoA lookalikes vs HMG-CoA reductase.
Remember: the graph test — with a fixed low dose of competitive inhibitor, raising substrate concentration returns the rate to the no-inhibitor curve. With a non-competitive inhibitor it never does (lower maximum). Statins must be dosed carefully: enough to cut cholesterol, not so much it's excessive.
C1.1.16

FEEDBACK INHIBITION & END PRODUCTS

The rule of feedback inhibition: when the product has built up it switches the pathway off. Feedback inhibition does exactly that — the end product of a pathway binds the allosteric site of the FIRST enzyme and switches the whole chain off. Enough is enough: negative feedback keeps every substance in the right amount.

📋

The end product is the manager — it walks back to enzyme 1

feedback inhibition · end product · allosteric site · threonine → isoleucine · threonine deaminase

Metabolic pathways must make enough, but not too much. In feedback inhibition, the end product of the last reaction inhibits the first reaction: it binds the allosteric site of the first enzyme, changing the active site's shape so catalysis stops while the end product remains bound. This is non-competitive inhibition and negative feedback in one mechanism. Too much end product → pathway switched off; too little → minimal inhibition → pathway open. Because the FIRST enzyme is the target, the intermediates between steps never pile up — nothing is wasted.

The textbook example: the amino acid threonine is converted to isoleucine through five reactions. As isoleucine accumulates, it binds the allosteric site of the first enzyme, threonine deaminase, and acts as a non-competitive inhibitor. Substrate list capped — the chain waits for demand.

FIG 16 — Threonine → (5 steps) → isoleucine. When isoleucine builds up it binds threonine deaminase's allosteric site: the first enzyme switches off, the whole pathway stops, intermediates don't accumulate.
Exam gold: name all three labels in one sentence: feedback inhibition = end product → allosteric site of the first enzyme → non-competitive inhibition → negative feedback. Efficiency point: inhibiting step 1 means intermediates never build up when the end product isn't needed.
C1.1.17

MECHANISM-BASED INHIBITION

Some inhibitors don't just block the door — they handcuff the enzyme forever. Mechanism-based inhibition: the inhibitor binds irreversibly, forming a permanent covalent bond with the active site. Heavy metals glue any enzyme's cysteine –SH groups; penicillin handcuffs the bacterial enzyme transpeptidase; Novichok agents do the same to acetylcholinesterase.

⛓

Irreversible: one molecule, one permanently inactivated enzyme

mechanism-based · irreversible · covalent bond · Hg/Pb · cysteine –SH · penicillin · transpeptidase · Novichok

Irreversible inhibition is permanent. Heavy metals — mercury and lead — are non-specific: they bind irreversibly to the –SH (thiol) groups of cysteine wherever they occur in an enzyme's structure, wrecking a wide range of enzymes — that's why they're so toxic and such dangerous pollutants. Specific irreversible inhibitors are structurally similar to the substrate, bind the active site, and are then converted by the enzyme into a form that forms a permanent covalent bond — a stable inhibitor–enzyme complex that can never catalyse again. This is mechanism-based inhibition: every molecule of inhibitor permanently inactivates one enzyme molecule, so a vital enzyme at a lethal concentration means death.

Penicillin is the classic: it binds the active site of transpeptidase, the enzyme that cross-links carbohydrate strands into the giant peptidoglycan molecule forming bacterial cell walls. Penicillin forms a permanent covalent bond with a particular amino acid in the active site — the cross-links can't be remade, the wall weakens, and the bacteria are killed by bursting (lysis). Bacteria that change transpeptidase's active site gain resistance. Novichok agents are mechanism-based inhibitors of acetylcholinesterase, lethal in minute quantities.

FIG 17 — Sticky handcuffs: Hg/Pb glue cysteine –SH groups (non-specific); penicillin forms a permanent covalent bond with transpeptidase's active site → no cross-links → cell wall bursts; Novichok inactivates acetylcholinesterase irreversibly.
Don't mix up: competitive and non-competitive inhibition are reversible; mechanism-based inhibition is irreversible — a covalent bond, a stable inhibitor–enzyme complex, the enzyme can never catalyse again. That one word — covalent — is the exam's favourite discriminator.

Quick recap

⚡ CHEAT

Fifteen one-liners. If you can say them out loud, you know the show.

🚪 Enzymes = elite enzymes — catalysts: speed up reactions, never used up, reused all night.
🎫 Specificity = who gets in — glucokinase admits glucose only; hexokinase admits any hexose.
⚡ Catalysis = everything faster — often by a factor of a million or more.
🧱 Anabolism — condensation, needs ATP; catabolism = catabolism — releases energy.
🚪 Active site = the door — just a few amino acids brought together by folding.
🤝 Induced fit — enzyme shifts stance: BOTH substrate and enzyme change shape (no lock-and-key).
🕺 Collisions — more molecules or more heat = more chances at the door.
🌡 Temperature — rise = faster, too hot = denatured; optimum isn't always 40°C.
🧪 pH is logarithmic — one unit = 10× acidity; detergent protease loves pH 9–10.
📈 Substrate curve — rises, flattens, never quite max: active sites saturate.
⚖️ Rate = amount ÷ time — mmol s⁻¹; IV changed, CV fixed, DV measured (catalase special).
⛰ Barrier lowered — enzymes shrink activation energy, never the net energy.
🔄 Cycles vs pathways — Krebs/Calvin cycles; glycolysis = 9-step one-way pathway.
🚫 Three inhibitors — competitive inhibitors (competitive, beatable with substrate), manager's switch (allosteric), owner's cap (feedback: end product stops enzyme 1).
⛓ Sticky handcuffs — penicillin binds transpeptidase forever (covalent); Hg/Pb glue cysteine –SH.
Test yourself

✍ SELF-TEST

10 questions. Pick an answer and you'll instantly see why.

1. What defines a catalyst?

✅ Catalysts are not used up — that's why small amounts catalyse reactions many times. Enzymes are the biological version; platinum (catalytic converters) is the inorganic one.
❌ The defining property is being unchanged by the reaction, so the catalyst can catalyse again and again. (And not all catalysts are proteins, nor do they need 40 °C.)

2. Which is an example of an ANABOLIC reaction?

✅ Anabolism builds larger molecules from smaller ones (condensation, water out, ATP in) — glycogen synthesis, protein synthesis, DNA replication. Digestion and respiration are catabolic.
❌ Catabolism breaks large molecules into smaller ones and releases energy — digestion and respiration are catabolic. Anabolism builds (condensation reactions needing ATP).

3. What is the active site made of?

✅ Only a few amino acids create the catalytic conditions, and they're often NOT neighbours in the sequence — folding brings them together. That's why the 3D structure is everything.
❌ The active site is small (a few amino acids) and its residues are usually brought together by folding from distant parts of the chain — alter the folding and you destroy the door.

4. What happens during induced-fit binding?

✅ Induced fit means mutual change: the enzyme surface attracts the substrate, and both molecules are altered — that's why the old lock-and-key model was rejected.
❌ Lock-and-key is explicitly rejected in the syllabus. Binding changes BOTH molecules — bond angles and lengths alter in the substrate AND the active site.

5. The pH scale is logarithmic. What does one pH unit mean?

✅ One pH unit = 10× change in [H⁺]. pH 4 is 100× more acidic than pH 6. Enzymes are sensitive because H⁺ alters ionic bonds between amino acids, changing the active site.
❌ It's logarithmic, not linear: each unit is a 10× change in acidity, and pH affects enzymes at any temperature (shifting pH from optimum changes activity).

6. What is the shape of the substrate-concentration curve for an enzyme?

✅ More substrate → more collisions → faster rate at first; but as active sites stay occupied, proportionally more collisions are blocked — the curve flattens and never quite reaches a maximum.
❌ The curve rises steeply then less and less steeply — it never quite reaches a maximum (that's the saturation of active sites, not a fall to zero).

7. How do enzymes speed up reactions?

✅ The enzyme weakens substrate bonds during binding → smaller activation energy → far more molecules reach the transition state per second. Net energy change is identical with or without enzyme.
❌ Enzymes never add energy and never change the net ΔE of the reaction — they only lower the activation energy hill (weakening substrate bonds), which is why rates jump by a million or more.

8. How do statins lower blood cholesterol?

✅ Statins look like HMG-CoA and win the race for HMG-CoA reductase's active site — the rate-limiting step of cholesterol synthesis — so less cholesterol is made. Dose must be tuned.
❌ Statins are COMPETITIVE inhibitors of HMG-CoA reductase (structurally similar to the substrate HMG-CoA, reversible active-site binding), not non-competitive or irreversible.

9. In feedback inhibition (threonine → isoleucine), what does the end product do?

✅ The end product inhibits the FIRST reaction by binding its allosteric site (threonine deaminase). Too much isoleucine → pathway off; too little → pathway on. Intermediates never pile up.
❌ Feedback inhibition targets the first enzyme only — the end product binds its allosteric site, changing the active site shape so catalysis stops. That's negative feedback + non-competitive inhibition.

10. Why is penicillin lethal to gram-positive bacteria?

✅ Penicillin is a mechanism-based (irreversible) inhibitor: a covalent bond locks the inhibitor–enzyme complex forever, cross-links can't be remade, the wall weakens and the bacteria lyse. Changed transpeptidase = resistance.
❌ Penicillin's binding is IRREVERSIBLE — a permanent covalent bond with the active site (mechanism-based inhibition), not reversible competition and certainly not heat denaturation.

  • ✓C1.1.1-2Catalysts: increase rate, not changed by the reaction, reusable, small amounts needed; enzymes = biological catalysts (platinum = inorganic example). Metabolism: complex network of interdependent reactions; pathways of small steps, chains + cycles (urea cycle: 5 reactions, 5 enzymes); almost all reactions catalysed by enzymes; specificity → hundreds/thousands of different enzymes; control by making more/less enzyme.
  • ✓C1.1.3Anabolism: monomers → macromolecules by condensation (water by-product), requires ATP — protein synthesis, DNA replication, starch/cellulose/glycogen, photosynthesis. Catabolism: macromolecules → monomers, releases energy, coupled to ATP — digestion (mouth, stomach, small intestine), cell respiration (glucose/lipids → CO₂ + H₂O), decomposition.
  • ✓C1.1.4Enzymes are globular proteins with precise 3D structure; active site = special region where substrate binds; shape + chemistry of site and substrate match; just a few amino acids, often not adjacent in sequence — brought together by folding; overall 3D structure crucial; products released, site free for next reaction.
  • ✓C1.1.5Induced-fit binding: substrate attracted when close; BOTH substrate and active site change shape (bond angles and lengths altered) — lock-and-key model rejected; second substrate can bind another part of the site; changes make bonds easier to break/form; products detach, active site returns to original state; catalytic cycle repeats.
  • ✓C1.1.6Molecular motion: substrate must come very close to the active site (substrate–active site collision); random movement in liquids; higher concentration of substrate/enzyme or higher temperature → more collisions; only aligned (correct orientation) collisions succeed; short-range attractive forces; large substrates → enzyme moves (DNA enzymes); membrane-embedded enzymes immobilised → substrate moves.
  • ✓C1.1.7Enzyme–substrate specificity: active site shape/chemistry binds substrate but not other substances; glucokinase absolutely specific (glucose only) vs hexokinase (any hexose); proteases broad. Denaturation: structure depends on weak interactions (hydrophobic, H-bonds) — heat/acidity disrupt them; changes even far from the active site ripple through; irreversible loss of function = denatured.
  • ✓C1.1.8Temperature: kinetic energy ↑ → more collisions → faster, but heat breaks enzyme bonds → denaturation; optimum temperature NOT always 40°C (higher in hot-spring organisms). pH: logarithmic — one unit = 10× acidity; ionic bonds altered away from optimum; Bacillus licheniformis protease optimum pH 9–10 (alkaline laundry detergents). Substrate concentration: more collisions → faster, but active sites saturate → curve rises less steeply, never quite reaches a maximum.
  • ✓C1.1.9Variables: independent (deliberately varied: temp, [S], enzyme conc, pH), control (kept constant, monitored — fair test), dependent (measured → reaction rate/enzyme activity). Rate = change in amount ÷ time, e.g. mmol s⁻¹; two methods (fixed time vs run to completion); catalase experiment (yeast/liver/kidney/seeds; H₂O₂ → H₂O + O₂; oxygen in measuring cylinder); repeat for reliability; units: g, mg, dm³, cm³, mol dm⁻³, °C, pH, % absorbance.
  • ✓C1.1.10Activation energy: reactions pass through a transition state; Ea breaks substrate bonds, energy released forming new bonds; enzyme weakens substrate bonds at the active site → smaller Ea, net energy released UNCHANGED; rate typically increases by a factor of a million or more; interpret energy graphs (two peaks = the catalytic cycle's approach and product release).
  • ✓C1.1.11Extracellular enzymes (exoenzymes): made by ribosomes attached to the ER, work outside the cell — digestion in the gut; unicellular archaea/bacteria/fungi secrete exoenzymes (cell walls block endocytosis). Intracellular: made by free ribosomes — glycolysis in cytoplasm (hexokinase, first step), Krebs in mitochondrial matrix (fumarase: fumarate → malate); pancreas stores digestive enzymes in vesicles, secreted via duct.
  • ✓C1.1.12Heat generation: energy conversion never 100% efficient → extra energy becomes heat; birds/mammals maintain body temp with metabolic heat; excess dumped by sweating (evaporative cooling); shivering = involuntary muscle contractions; brown fat: many mitochondria, uncoupled respiration (heat without ATP); emperor penguins huddle to share neighbours' heat; compost heaps steam.
  • ✓C1.1.13Pathways: large transformations = sequences of small steps (word-game analogy); glycolysis: glucose → pyruvate, NINE reactions, nine enzymes; one reaction splits 6C → two 3C, so later steps run twice per glucose; branching common (network); cycles: every intermediate is product of one reaction and substrate of another — Calvin cycle, Krebs cycle, urea cycle.
  • ✓C1.1.14Allosteric site: second binding site where a different specific substance binds and unbinds; binding changes enzyme shape → alters active site (activates OR reversibly inhibits); substances binding there don't compete with substrates = non-competitive inhibitors; only specific substances fit; conformational change is the mechanism.
  • ✓C1.1.15Competitive inhibition: inhibitor structurally similar to substrate, binds active site reversibly, NOT converted → remains bound longer; first to arrive wins; more inhibitor → more inhibition; more substrate → inhibition shrinks (effectively uninhibited) — unlike non-competitive; statins = competitive inhibitors of HMG-CoA reductase (rate-limiting step of cholesterol synthesis); sarin = competitive inhibitor of acetylcholinesterase.
  • ✓C1.1.16Feedback inhibition: end product of the last reaction inhibits the FIRST reaction via its allosteric site = non-competitive + negative feedback; too much end product → pathway switched off, intermediates don't accumulate; too little → pathway open; example: threonine → isoleucine in 5 reactions, threonine deaminase is enzyme 1, isoleucine binds its allosteric site.
  • ✓C1.1.17Mechanism-based inhibition: irreversible — inhibitor forms a permanent covalent bond with the active site; heavy metals Hg/Pb bind irreversibly to cysteine –SH groups (non-specific, toxic); specific ones are substrate lookalikes converted into permanently-bound forms; penicillin vs transpeptidase (peptidoglycan cross-links fail → lysis; changed transpeptidase = resistance); Novichok agents vs acetylcholinesterase (lethal in minute quantities).
