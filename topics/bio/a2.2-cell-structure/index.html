A2.2.1

CELLS ARE THE BASIC UNIT OF LIFE

The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of all living organisms. Cell theory lets us make predictions.

The cell is the basic structural unit of all living organisms — every living thing is made of one or more cells, and every cell comes from a pre-existing cell (cell theory).

Nature of science: deductive reasoning lets us generate predictions from theories. Based on cell theory, a newly discovered organism can be predicted to consist of one or more cells.
A2.2.2

MICROSCOPY SKILLS

You should have practical experience of preparing and viewing cells, measuring them, and calculating actual size and magnification.

  • Make temporary mounts of cells/tissues and stain them.
  • Measure sizes using an eyepiece graticule (a scale in the eyepiece) calibrated against a stage micrometer.
  • Focus using coarse and fine adjustments.
  • Calculate actual size and magnification: actual size = measured size ÷ magnification.
  • Produce a scale bar and take photographs.
Nature of science: measuring with instruments is a form of quantitative observation.
A2.2.3

DEVELOPMENTS IN MICROSCOPY

Electron microscopy and modern techniques reveal far more detail than light microscopy.

TechniqueWhat it shows / advantage
Electron microscopy (EM)Much higher resolution (uses electrons, not light) → see organelles, membranes, ribosomes.
Freeze fractureSplits membranes and reveals internal membrane structure (e.g. proteins in the bilayer).
Cryogenic EMFreezes specimens to preserve near-native structure (no dehydration) → very high-resolution 3D views of proteins.
Fluorescent stainsSpecific molecules glow, letting you track them in living cells.
ImmunofluorescenceAntibodies tagged with fluorescent dyes bind specific targets → localise specific proteins.
A2.2.4

STRUCTURES COMMON TO ALL CELLS

All cells have DNA, cytoplasm (mostly water) and a plasma membrane (of lipids).

  • DNA — the genetic material.
  • Cytoplasm — composed mainly of water; the medium of reactions.
  • Plasma membrane — composed mainly of lipids; separates inside from outside and controls what enters/leaves.
A2.2.5

PROKARYOTIC CELL STRUCTURE

Prokaryotes (bacteria) have a cell wall, plasma membrane, cytoplasm, naked DNA in a loop and 70S ribosomes — but no nucleus or membrane-bound organelles.

The required example is Gram-positive eubacteria such as Bacillus and Staphylococcus. Key components:

  • Cell wall — rigid outer layer (of peptidoglycan).
  • Plasma membrane — inside the wall.
  • Cytoplasm.
  • Naked DNA in a loop — not bound to histones, not enclosed in a nucleus (the nucleoid region).
  • 70S ribosomes — smaller than eukaryote ribosomes.
Note: prokaryote structure varies, but you are not required to know details such as the lack of cell walls in phytoplasmas/mycoplasmas.
FIG 1 — A prokaryotic cell: cell wall, plasma membrane, cytoplasm, a naked DNA loop and 70S ribosomes — no nucleus.
A2.2.6

EUKARYOTIC CELL STRUCTURE

Eukaryotes have a nucleus (chromosomes of DNA + histones, in a double membrane with pores), 80S ribosomes, membrane-bound organelles and a cytoskeleton.

  • Plasma membrane enclosing a compartmentalized cytoplasm.
  • 80S ribosomes (larger than prokaryote 70S).
  • Nucleus — chromosomes of DNA bound to histones, in a double membrane with pores.
  • Membrane-bound organelles: mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum (rough + smooth), Golgi apparatus, and vesicles/vacuoles (including lysosomes).
  • Cytoskeleton — microtubules and microfilaments (shape + support).
FIG 2 — A eukaryotic cell: a nucleus with DNA bound to histones, membrane-bound organelles (mitochondria, ER, Golgi), 80S ribosomes and a cytoskeleton.
A2.2.7

PROCESSES OF LIFE IN UNICELLULAR ORGANISMS

A single cell carries out all the processes of life.

  • Homeostasis — keeping internal conditions stable.
  • Metabolism — all the chemical reactions.
  • Nutrition — obtaining nutrients.
  • Movement.
  • Excretion — removing waste.
  • Growth.
  • Response to stimuli.
  • Reproduction.

A2.2.9

ATYPICAL EUKARYOTE CELL STRUCTURE

Some eukaryote cells are atypical — often shown by unusual numbers of nuclei.

  • Aseptate fungal hyphae — no cross-walls, so many nuclei share one continuous cytoplasm.
  • Skeletal muscle fibres — multinucleate (many nuclei per fibre).
  • Red blood cells (mammalian) — no nucleus at all.
  • Phloem sieve tube elements — no nucleus (lost at maturity); kept alive by companion cells.
A2.2.10

IDENTIFYING CELLS IN MICROGRAPHS

You should be able to identify cells as prokaryote, plant or animal in light and electron micrographs, and name key structures.

In electron micrographs, you should be able to identify: nucleoid region, prokaryotic cell wall, nucleus, mitochondrion, chloroplast, sap vacuole, Golgi apparatus, rough and smooth ER, chromosomes, ribosomes, cell wall, plasma membrane and microvilli.

A2.2.11

DRAWING & ANNOTATING CELLS

You should be able to draw and annotate organelles from electron micrographs, including their functions.

Key organelles to draw and annotate with functions: nucleus, mitochondria, chloroplasts, sap vacuole, Golgi apparatus, rough and smooth ER, chromosomes; plus cell wall, plasma membrane, secretory vesicles and microvilli.

Annotations must include the function — a labelled but functionless diagram loses marks.
AHL · A2.2.12

ORIGIN OF EUKARYOTIC CELLS — ENDOSYMBIOSIS

Mitochondria (and in some cells chloroplasts) arose by endosymbiosis — an ancient cell engulfed a bacterium.

All eukaryotes evolved from a common unicellular ancestor that had a nucleus and reproduced sexually. Mitochondria evolved by endosymbiosis: a free-living bacterium was taken in and became an organelle. In some eukaryotes chloroplasts also arose this way.

  • Evidence: mitochondria and chloroplasts have their own 70S ribosomes, naked circular DNA, and the ability to replicate — like bacteria.
Nature of science: the strength of a theory comes from the observations it explains and the predictions it supports. Endosymbiosis accounts for a wide range of observations.
AHL · A2.2.13

CELL DIFFERENTIATION

Cells become specialised through different patterns of gene expression, often triggered by environment.

Specialised cells form by differentiation. The basis is different patterns of gene expression — cells have the same DNA but express different genes — often triggered by changes in the environment.

AHL · A2.2.14

EVOLUTION OF MULTICELLULARITY

Multicellularity evolved repeatedly and has advantages of larger size and cell specialisation.

Multicellularity has evolved repeatedly — many fungi, eukaryotic algae, and all plants and animals are multicellular. Its advantages: larger body size and cell specialization (different cells doing different jobs).
