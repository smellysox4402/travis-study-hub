D3.2.1

GAMETES & ZYGOTES — THE TRANSMISSION OF GENES

How traits actually get passed on: half your tickets from mum, half from dad, one full set each.

🎟️

Half from mum, half from dad

gametes · haploid · zygote · meiosis

In a sexual life cycle, parents pass genes to offspring in gametes — egg and sperm. Gametes are haploid (n): they contain one chromosome of each type, whether male or female. So both parents make an equal genetic contribution.

When the gametes fuse, the nuclei join and the chromosome number doubles: the zygote is diploid (2n), with two chromosomes of each type. The halving trick that makes this possible is meiosis — one diploid nucleus divides twice to make four haploid nuclei.

Figure 1 · The sexual life cycle: meiosis halves 2n → n in gametes; fertilisation restores 2n in the zygote. Equal tickets from both parents.
Memory: gametes are the haploid tickets (one of each chromosome), zygote is the diploid pair. If gametes weren't halved, the chromosome number would double every generation — the doubling trap.
THE HALVING

Meiosis

A diploid nucleus divides twice to produce four haploid nuclei — one copy of each gene per gamete.

THE DOUBLING

Fertilisation

Egg + sperm nuclei join → chromosome number doubles back to diploid. n + n = 2n. That's inheritance's whole trick.

D3.2.2

MENDEL'S EXPERIMENTS & LAWS

The controlled-cross method: how a monk in a garden figured out inheritance before DNA was even a word.

🧪

The original bouncer: Gregor Mendel

P generation · F1 · F2 · controlled crosses

Patterns of inheritance are investigated by crossing varieties — transferring pollen from the anthers of one plant (the male parent) onto the stigma of another (the female parent). A paintbrush works, or dab an anther directly on the stigma.

To keep the cross honest, all other pollen must be blocked: cut off the flower's own anthers before they mature, then enclose it in a paper bag so insects and wind can't sneak pollen in. Self-pollination (pollen from the same plant) would wreck the experiment.

The known parents are the P generation; offspring in the seeds are the F₁ (first filial); their offspring are F₂. Mendel crossed tall × dwarf peas, purple × white flowers, counted every offspring, and calculated ratios — unheard of in the 19th century.

Figure 2 · The controlled cross: block stray pollen, dust donor pollen on, let the seeds tell the story. Ratios are the whole game.
Self-pollination = self-fertilisation. It happens when pollen from a plant lands on its own stigma — no cross between varieties happens, and the experiment is void. Remove the anthers before they ripen and bag the flower.
D3.2.3 · D3.2.4 · D3.2.5

ALLELES, GENOTYPES & PHENOTYPES

Alleles, genotypes, phenotypes — and why dominant always wins at the door.

🎫

Two tickets per gene

alleles · genotype · homozygous · heterozygous · dominant · recessive

Alleles are different versions of a gene — they can differ by as little as one base or by large sections. New alleles are born by mutation. Diploid organisms carry two alleles of most genes, one inherited from each parent.

The combination of alleles is the genotype (DD, Dd, dd). If both alleles are the same → homozygous (all gametes carry that one allele). If different → heterozygous (50% of gametes carry each allele).

The phenotype is the observable outcome — structural (curly vs straight hair) or functional (seeing red vs green). Most phenotypes come from the interaction of genotype and environment.

Figure 3 · The classic monohybrid cross. Two heterozygotes → 3:1 phenotype, 1:2:1 genotype. The dwarf trait vanished in F₁ and reappeared in a quarter of F₂.
GENOTYPE

The tickets you hold

DD, Dd or dd — the allele combination. Homozygous = same pair, heterozygous = different pair.

PHENOTYPE

What shows

The observable trait. Genotype × environment → phenotype. Not everything written in the tickets gets expressed.

DOMINANT

The bouncer

One dominant allele is enough to show the trait. Recessive only shows when both tickets are recessive (tt).

Exam classic: Mendel crossed pure-breeding tall × dwarf. All F₁ were tall (the dwarf trait "disappeared"). Self-pollinate the F₁ and the dwarf trait reappears in 1/4 of F₂ — a 3:1 ratio. That reappearance is the evidence against blending inheritance.
D3.2.6

DOMINANT & RECESSIVE ALLELES

Phenotypic plasticity: the same genotype, dressed differently by the environment.

🦎

The shape-shifter's trick

phenotypic plasticity · gene expression · reversible

Organisms can respond to their environment by varying patterns of gene expression — and therefore their traits. This is phenotypic plasticity: genes are switched on or off, not changed into new alleles, so it's reversible.

Classic example: tanning. More sunlight → increased expression of the melanin gene → darker skin. Less sunlight → gene expression reverts → paler skin. Same genotype the whole time.

Sometimes plasticity is a one-way switch between alternative forms. Plants are champions: seedlings raised in the dark grow tall, pale and etiolated; in the light they're green and compact — same variety, same genotype.

Figure 4 · Phenotypic plasticity: environment flips gene expression, not the genes. Reversible adaptation to a changing world.
Exam trap: plasticity is NOT mutation and NOT Lamarckian inheritance. The genes stay identical — only their expression changes. Nothing is inherited by the next generation from this.
D3.2.7 · D3.2.8

PUNNETT GRIDS & MONOHYBRID CROSSES

PKU: the hidden-carrier disease, and the SNPs hiding in every gene pool.

🧬

Two carriers walk into a club…

carrier · recessive disease · phenylketonuria · SNP

Phenylketonuria (PKU) is a genetic disease caused by a recessive allele of the gene for the enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase (on chromosome 12 — an autosome). The enzyme converts phenylalanine → tyrosine.

A carrier (Aa) has one normal + one disease allele: they make enough functional enzyme to stay symptom-free, but can pass the disease allele on. Two carriers don't know they're carrying — until a child inherits both recessive alleles. Chance: 1 in 4 (25%).

With two disease alleles (aa), no functional enzyme is made → phenylalanine accumulates → brain damage, intellectual disability. Preventable: screen newborns, give a low-phenylalanine diet. Boys and girls have the same 25% chance — it's autosomal.

Figure 5 · The 1-in-4 trap: two symptom-free carriers, 25% risk per child. PKU is preventable if caught at birth — which is why heel-prick screening exists.

SNPs — the single-base ticket variations

A gene pool is all the genes of all individuals in a sexually reproducing population. Alleles differ by small variations in base sequence — often just one base. Positions where different bases can occur are single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), pronounced "snips".

Even within one gene there can be many SNP positions, so a gene pool can hold many different alleles of the same gene — that's multiple alleles. Next act: the ABO blood group is the classic three-allele system.

D3.2.9 · D3.2.10

MULTIPLE ALLELES — THE ABO BLOOD GROUP

Three alleles, four blood types, and the difference between codominance and incomplete dominance.

🩸

ABO: the VIP system with three tickets

multiple alleles · codominance · incomplete dominance · I^A I^B i

The ABO blood group is set by one gene with three alleles in the gene pool: Iᴬ, Iᴮ and i. Iᴬ codes for the A glycoprotein on red blood cells, Iᴮ for the B glycoprotein, and i for no glycoprotein.

Because you only carry two of the three: IᴬIᴬ / Iᴬi → type A · IᴮIᴮ / Iᴮi → type B · ii → type O · and IᴬIᴮ → type AB, where both glycoproteins show. That's codominance: both alleles are expressed at once, not a blend.

People make antibodies against the glycoproteins they lack — anti-A, anti-B — which is why blood transfusions must be compatible. Type AB accepts anything (universal recipient); type O can donate to anyone (universal donor).

Figure 6 · ABO blood groups: multiple alleles → 4 phenotypes. AB is codominant (both show); pink Mirabilis is incomplete dominance (a blend).
Memory: codominance = both flags fly (AB). Incomplete dominance = two buckets make a half-bucket (pink). Don't mix them — the exam loves this distinction.
D3.2.11

SEX CHROMOSOMES & THE X-FACTOR

Sex determination: it's not the mother's egg that picks the sex — it's which sperm gets in.

⚧

X marks the spot, Y makes the call

sex chromosomes · XX · XY · SRY · autosomes

Humans have 23 pairs of chromosomes; pair 23 is the sex chromosomes. The X is big, with ~900 genes essential in both sexes. The Y is small (~55 genes) with the SRY gene — the testis-determining factor (TDF).

Females are XX, males XY. All eggs carry one X. Sperm carry either an X or a Y — so the father's sperm decides the sex: X sperm → daughter (XX), Y sperm → son (XY). 50:50.

Proof it's the Y that matters: XXY (Klinefelter's) develops male — the Y is present. XO (Turner's) develops female — no Y. SRY → gonads become testes → testosterone → male development. No SRY → ovaries → oestradiol → female.

Figure 7 · Sex determination: XX × XY → 1:1. The Y chromosome's SRY gene flips development to male; no Y → female.
Key detail: X chromosomes carry ~900 genes essential in both sexes — everyone needs at least one X. The Y is small and mostly male-specific. That asymmetry is what makes sex-linked inheritance skip generations.
D3.2.12 · D3.2.13

SEX LINKAGE

Sex-linked disorders like haemophilia — why they mostly hit boys — and reading pedigree charts.

🩹

Haemophilia: the X-linked trap

sex linkage · X-linked recessive · Factor VIII · carrier

Haemophilia is a sex-linked genetic disorder: the gene for Factor VIII (a blood-clotting protein) lives on the X chromosome. The disease allele is recessive.

Males (XY) have only one X — so one disease allele is enough to get the disorder. Females (XX) need both X chromosomes to carry it; one healthy X makes them symptom-free carriers. That's why haemophilia is almost always in boys (~1 in 10,000) and girls are almost never affected (theoretical 1 in 100,000,000).

Carrier mother × healthy father: sons 50% affected, daughters 50% carriers. No male-to-male transmission — a son gets his Y from dad and his X from mum.

Figure 8 · Sex-linked recessive: the classic "why it's mostly boys" diagram. Sons inherit their X from mum — 50% chance of the disease allele.

Pedigree charts — reading the family album

Pedigree charts map a disorder through a family: squares = males, circles = females, filled = affected. Generations are Roman numerals down the side; individuals numbered within each generation.

  • Recessive signature: two unaffected parents produce an affected child → both parents must be carriers (Aa × Aa).
  • Dominant signature: every affected child has at least one affected parent — the trait doesn't skip generations.
  • X-linked recessive signature: mostly affected males; no father→son transmission.
  • Most of the 4,000+ identified genetic disorders are caused by very rare recessive alleles — you'd need to inherit the same rare allele from both parents. A typical person carries at least 5 rare recessive alleles that could cause a disorder.
Figure 9 · Pedigree logic: unaffected parents + affected child = recessive. The trait "skips" the carrier generation and reappears.
D3.2.16 · D3.2.17

THE DIHYBRID PUNNETT GRID

Two genes at once: segregation + independent assortment, and the famous 9:3:3:1.

🎲

Two genes, one grid

segregation · independent assortment · dihybrid cross · 9:3:3:1

Segregation: the two alleles of each gene separate into different gametes during meiosis — each gamete gets one allele per gene.

Independent assortment: for genes on different chromosomes, the allele pairs sort independently — the gamete's allele of gene 1 doesn't care which allele of gene 2 it's carrying. The result: a heterozygous dihybrid (RrYy) makes four equally common gametes: RY, Ry, rY, ry.

Cross two dihybrids (RrYy × RrYy) and the 16-box grid collapses to the famous 9:3:3:1 phenotype ratio — 9 round-yellow : 3 round-green : 3 wrinkled-yellow : 1 wrinkled-green. If the ratio is off, the genes aren't assorting independently — a hint of linkage (Act 11).

Figure 10 · The 16-box dihybrid grid and its 9:3:3:1 collapse. Genes on different chromosomes assort independently.
Exam trick: you rarely need all 16 boxes. Split it into two monohybrid crosses (Rr × Rr → 3:1, Yy × Yy → 3:1) and multiply: 3:1 × 3:1 = 9:3:3:1. Saves time and catches errors.
D3.2.14 · D3.2.15

VARIATION & PLASTICITY

Discrete vs continuous variation — and the box-and-whisker plot that displays the spread.

📊

Discrete club vs continuous dancefloor

polygenic inheritance · continuous variation · box-and-whisker

Discrete variation (ABO blood type, pea colour): a few genes, separate categories, no in-between. Continuous variation (height, skin colour, mass): many genes (polygenic inheritance) plus environmental influence → a smooth range from one extreme to the other.

Continuous data is summarised with a box-and-whisker plot: minimum, lower quartile (Q1), median, upper quartile (Q3), maximum. The box spans the interquartile range (IQR = Q3 − Q1) — the middle 50% of the data. Whiskers run to the extremes; points beyond 1.5 × IQR are plotted separately as outliers.

Figure 11 · Box-and-whisker anatomy + why traits come in two flavours: discrete (few genes) vs continuous (polygenic + environment).
DISCRETE

Separate clubs

Few genes, no intermediates. ABO blood type, seed shape/colour. Display as bar chart / counts.

CONTINUOUS

The full dancefloor

Polygenic inheritance + environmental effects. Height, skin colour, milk yield. Display as histogram / box-and-whisker.

D3.2.18 · D3.2.19 · D3.2.20 · D3.2.21 · AHL

LINKAGE & THE CHI-SQUARED TEST

Loci, gene linkage, recombinants, and the chi-squared test that tells you if ratios are lying.

🔗

When genes ride together

locus · linked genes · recombinants · chi-squared (χ²)

Each gene sits at a locus (plural loci) on a specific chromosome — humans have ~20,000 genes spread across the autosomes (1–22) and sex chromosomes. When two genes sit close together on the same chromosome, their alleles tend to be inherited together — that's autosomal gene linkage. They don't assort independently, so a dihybrid cross gives a skewed ratio, not 9:3:3:1.

Crossing over in meiosis can shuffle segments between non-sister chromatids, producing recombinants — new allele combinations. In a test cross of a double heterozygote: unlinked genes → 1:1:1:1; linked genes → parental types dominate, recombinants are rare (their frequency reflects how far apart the genes are).

The chi-squared (χ²) test decides if an observed ratio fits an expected one (3:1, 9:3:3:1, 1:1:1:1): χ² = Σ (observed − expected)² / expected. Compare with the critical value at p = 0.05 and degrees of freedom = classes − 1. If χ² is inside the critical region → reject the null hypothesis → the ratio doesn't fit.

Figure 12 · AHL: linkage skews ratios, crossing over makes recombinants, and χ² tells you whether the deviation is real or just sampling noise.

Corn numbers — the worked example

Test cross of CcNn × ccnn (coloured/shrunken corn seeds). If unlinked, expect 1:1:1:1. Observed: 638 CcNn · 21,379 Ccnn · 21,096 ccNn · 672 ccnn. The parental combos (Cn, cN) swamp the recombinants (CN, cn) — so C and N are linked on the same chromosome. Crossing over produced the rare recombinants.

Exam shortcut: in any test cross, parental types = most frequent, recombinants = least frequent. The recombination frequency ≈ distance between loci. And χ² always uses (observed − expected)² / expected, summed over every class.

✍ SELF-TEST

10 questions. Pick an answer — instant feedback. No mercy from the bouncer.

1. Gametes are…

✅ Gametes are haploid (n): one of each chromosome. Meiosis halves 2n → n; fertilisation restores 2n.
❌ Gametes are haploid — the halving that makes n + n = 2n possible. Meiosis does the halving.

2. Why bag the flower during a controlled cross?

✅ The bag keeps the cross honest: only your chosen donor pollen reaches the stigma.
❌ The bag blocks stray pollen. Anthers are cut off first so the flower can't self-pollinate.

3. A heterozygous individual has genotype…

✅ Heterozygous = Dd: two different alleles, split 50/50 into gametes.
❌ Same alleles = homozygous (DD or dd). And you only carry two alleles of most genes, not three.

4. Phenotypic plasticity means…

✅ Plasticity = gene expression changes with environment; the genes themselves stay identical and it's reversible.
❌ No mutation, no Lamarckian inheritance. Only gene expression changes — tanning is the classic example.

5. Two PKU carriers (Aa × Aa) have a…

✅ Aa × Aa → AA : Aa : Aa : aa = 25% affected. Both carriers are symptom-free, so it arrives as a surprise.
❌ The ratio is 1:2:1 — only the aa child (1 in 4) develops the disease.

6. Blood group AB is an example of…

✅ IᴬIᴮ shows both glycoproteins — codominance, not a blend. (Blending = incomplete dominance, like pink Mirabilis flowers.)
❌ AB is codominant: both alleles fully expressed at once. Incomplete dominance would give an intermediate phenotype.

7. What determines the sex of a human baby?

✅ Eggs always carry X; sperm carry X or Y. The sperm decides — and it's the PRESENCE of Y (SRY gene) that makes a male.
❌ Mother contributes X only. XXY (Klinefelter) is male despite two Xs — the Y is what matters.

8. Why is haemophilia far more common in boys?

✅ X-linked recessive: a boy's single X has no second copy to cover the disease allele. Girls with one healthy X are carriers.
❌ The gene is on the X (Factor VIII), and the allele is recessive — a boy's one X is exposed, so it shows.

9. RrYy × RrYy gives the phenotype ratio…

✅ Two unlinked heterozygous genes → 9:3:3:1. Independent assortment gives four equally likely gametes per parent.
❌ 9:3:3:1 is the signature of two independently assorting genes. (Mostly-parental = linkage.)

10. In a chi-squared test on 4 phenotype classes (df = 3), the critical value at p = 0.05 is…

✅ At 3 df, p = 0.05, critical χ² = 7.815. Above it → significant deviation (e.g. linkage). Below → keep the null hypothesis.
❌ 3.841 is the df = 1 value. For 4 classes, df = 4 − 1 = 3, critical value 7.815.
