Act 1 · C2.1.1

THE GUEST LIST 🎟

Only the right key opens the right door. That's the whole of cell signalling in one sentence.

HOW CELLS TALK

Cell signalling = the transmission of chemical signals between interacting cells. Your trillions of cells use it to coordinate — to hold homeostasis together, to grow, to fight, to think.

It also happens between unicellular organisms (bacteria gossip too — see Act 4).

  • The guest = the LIGAND — the signalling chemical, released by the communicating cell. (Syllabus word: always say ligand, not just "chemical".)
  • The door = the RECEPTOR — a protein with a specific binding site, on or inside the target cell.
  • Ligand binds → the receptor changes shape (conformational change) → triggers a cellular response.
  • Turning a chemical signal into a cellular response = signal transduction.

ONLY THE RIGHT KEY GETS IN 🥒

A hormone travels through your bloodstream and passes every single cell in your body. But only the cells with the matching receptor react. A cucumber key 🥒 does not open the front door — no matter how hard it tries.

That's why one chemical can do completely different jobs: the message is the same, the doors it fits are different.

LIGAND-BINDING SITE = THE KEYHOLE 🔑

Receptor–ligand binding is like enzyme–substrate specificity — with ONE crucial difference:

  • Same: binding happens at a specific site; the shape and chemical properties of the site match the ligand (rejecting everything else); neither enzyme nor receptor is changed by binding (induced fit is temporary).
  • Different: an enzyme converts its substrate into a product and releases it — binding is brief and the cycle repeats many times per second. A receptor catalyses nothing — the signalling chemical stays bound for a long time and is released unchanged.

Exam phrasing: "the signalling chemical binds selectively to the ligand-binding site of the receptor and is released unchanged."

WHAT THE RESPONSE CAN BE 🎭

A ligand can trigger (exam loves this list):

  • Change in gene expression → cell differentiation
  • Regulation of enzyme activity → metabolism changes
  • Change in cell size/shape → e.g. muscle contraction
  • Cell proliferation (growth) or cell death (apoptosis)

SPECIFICITY = THE LOCK & KEY 🔐

Ligand and receptor must be complementary in shape AND charge — like a key to a lock. Complementary shape alone isn't enough; the chemistry has to match too.

Remember: hormone passes every cell → only cells with the matching receptor respond.

DELIVERY METHODS 📦

Same club, four ways to send the invite — from whispering to a neighbour to mailing across the country.

HOW FAR DOES THE MESSAGE TRAVEL?

AUTOCRINE · SELF 📣

"Message to self"

A cell releases a chemical that stimulates itself.

Example: an activated T lymphocyte releases cytokines → stimulates that same T cell to proliferate.

PARACRINE · NEIGHBOUR 🤫

"Message next door"

A cell signals a nearby cell.

Example: a pre-synaptic neuron releases neurotransmitters → stimulate the post-synaptic neuron across the synapse.

ENDOCRINE · BLOODSTREAM 💌

"Message across the country"

A cell releases a chemical into the blood → acts on distant target cells.

Example: endocrine glands release hormones that travel to distant target tissue.

Also worth knowing: pheromones = chemicals between members of the same species (e.g. alarm trails); allelopathy = chemicals between different species (e.g. plants poisoning competitors).

THE FOUR DELIVERY TYPES 🎁

DeliveryHow it worksKey facts
💌 HORMONES Released by endocrine cells into the bloodstream → act on distant cells. Signal can persist for hours; often trigger changes in gene expression. Secreted by endocrine glands (into blood capillaries, no duct) not exocrine glands (duct to the outside). Hydrophilic (proteins, amines) OR hydrophobic (steroids — carried bound to transport proteins). Examples: insulin, thyroxin, testosterone.
🤫 NEUROTRANSMITTERS Released by nerve cells → diffuse across the synaptic cleft. Rapid; removed from the gap after firing — broken down in the cleft OR reabsorbed into the pre-synaptic neuron. Excitatory NTs stimulate impulses; inhibitory NTs block them. Include amino acids, amines, peptides, esters (acetylcholine!) and gases.
📡 CYTOKINES Signalling proteins released by cells to regulate immune activity (immunomodulatory). Act on the producing cell or nearby cells; can't enter cells → bind membrane receptors → cascades → gene expression. One cytokine can bind several receptor types → multiple effects. Roles: inflammation, immune responses, cell growth, embryo development (a cytokine storm = sepsis). Examples: interleukins, erythropoietin, interferon.
🔔 CALCIUM IONS A common intracellular signal (muscles, nerves). Stored in the sarcoplasmic reticulum (muscle): an impulse opens channels → Ca²⁺ binds the proteins that block contraction → they shift → contraction starts; no impulse → Ca²⁺ pumped back. In neurons: Ca²⁺ enters the pre-synaptic end → exocytosis of neurotransmitter → pumped back out. Also a second messenger.

THE MENU: CHEMICAL DIVERSITY 🧪

Why so many different chemicals? A signalling chemical must have a distinctive shape and chemical properties (so the receptor can tell it apart from everything else) and be small and soluble enough to be transported. Evolution just repurposed whatever molecules already existed.

HORMONES — 4 GROUPS
AMINEModified amino acid. Melatonin, thyroxin, adrenaline/epinephrine.
PEPTIDE< 50 amino acids. ADH, oxytocin.
PROTEIN≥ 50 amino acids. Insulin, glucagon.
STEROIDNon-polar lipid from cholesterol. Oestrogen, testosterone, cortisol.
NEUROTRANSMITTERS — 5 GROUPS
AMINO ACIDGlutamate, glycine.
AMINEDopamine, norepinephrine.
PEPTIDEEndorphin.
ESTERAcetylcholine (ACh).
GASNitric oxide (NO).
EXAM TRAP: peptide (<50 aa) vs protein (≥50 aa) hormone — examiners love the 50-amino-acid cutoff. And adrenaline is BOTH a hormone and a neurotransmitter — a classic "state the difference" answer.
NITRIC vs NITROUS: the official guide says nitric oxide (NO) is the gas neurotransmitter. Some sites say "nitrous oxide" — that's laughing gas (N₂O), a different molecule. In the exam, write nitric oxide.

🧪 Textbook grouping (Oxford 2023): hormones = amines · peptides · steroids (insulin sits under peptides there); neurotransmitters = amines · gases · amino acids · esters. The sharper exam answer keeps peptide (<50 aa) vs protein (≥50 aa) separate.

🐟 Fun fact from the textbook's own data question: nitric oxide even runs newt courtship — nitric oxide synthase activity in male newt brains spikes through each stage of the mating dance. NO: signalling gas, dating coach.

THE DOOR POLICY 🚪

Hydrophilic guests ring the doorbell. Hydrophobic VIPs walk straight through the wall. Meet the four bouncers.

THE QUEUE: RECEPTION → TRANSDUCTION → RESPONSE
HYDROPHILIC RINGS THE BELL · HYDROPHOBIC WALKS IN
NO KEY, NO ENTRY · ONE "FIRE!" EMPTIES THE STADIUM
VIPs GO STRAIGHT TO THE BOSS · NEGATIVE = THERMOSTAT, POSITIVE = SNOWBALL

THE 3-STAGE QUEUE 🎬

  1. RECEPTION — a ligand binds to a receptor, inside the cell or on the membrane.
  2. TRANSDUCTION — the receptor converts the chemical signal into a cellular signal (often a sequential cascade of reactions).
  3. RESPONSE — change in the cell: gene expression, metabolic activity, or shape/movement.
HYDROPHILIC LIGAND (can't cross the membrane)

→ TRANSMEMBRANE RECEPTOR 🔔

Ligand stays outside; it rings a receptor that spans the membrane.

Receptor chemistry: a band of hydrophobic amino acids on its surface — attracted to the apolar phospholipid tails in the core of the membrane — with hydrophilic amino acids on both sides, in contact with the aqueous fluids inside and outside the cell.

Peptide hormones (insulin, glucagon, leptin, ADH, oxytocin) all work this way.

HYDROPHOBIC LIGAND (crosses freely)

→ INTRACELLULAR RECEPTOR 💎

Ligand penetrates the cell and finds its receptor in the cytoplasm or nucleus.

Receptor chemistry: hydrophilic amino acids on its surface (so it stays dissolved in the aqueous cytoplasm or nucleus) + a non-polar binding site (for the fat-soluble ligand).

Steroid hormones (oestradiol, progesterone, testosterone) work this way. Oestradiol receptors even pair up: two hormone–receptor complexes jointly bind DNA as a dimer.

🚪

BOUNCER 1 — THE DOOR

LIGAND-GATED ION CHANNEL · neurotransmitter receptor · example: ACETYLCHOLINE (ACh) · C2.1.8

The bouncer is the door. ACh is released by the pre-synaptic neuron into the synaptic cleft and binds to a cholinergic receptor on the post-synaptic cell.

  1. ACh binds → conformational change in the channel protein.
  2. The channel opens → Na⁺ floods in by facilitated diffusion (down its gradient, through the open pore).
  3. The voltage across the membrane changes → LOCAL DEPOLARISATION → can trigger an action potential (full story in C2.2).
  4. This can trigger voltage-gated ion channels → nerve impulse propagates.
  5. ACh is then broken down (no continued stimulation) and the products are recycled by the pre-synaptic neuron.

Speed: milliseconds. ACh runs muscle contraction and brain pathways — memory, arousal, learning.

EXAM BIT: "changes the membrane potential" = Na⁺ floods in → depolarisation. Say the ions' name. Don't say "ions" — say sodium ions.
📻

BOUNCER 2 — THE WALKIE-TALKIE

G PROTEIN-COUPLED RECEPTOR (GPCR) · example: EPINEPHRINE (ADRENALINE) · C2.1.9 + C2.1.10

The bouncer doesn't open anything himself — he calls dispatch on the radio, and dispatch mobilises the whole club.

Structure: a GPCR is a single polypeptide that snakes through the membrane. The extracellular part forms the binding site; the intracellular part docks onto a G protein. Humans have hundreds of different GPCRs — one of the biggest receptor families we've got.

The G protein = three subunits: an α subunit bound to GDP + a βγ dimer. When resting, the α, β and γ subunits sit assembled on the receptor.

GPCRs are everywhere: their ligands include light-sensitive compounds, odours, pheromones, hormones and neurotransmitters — your eyes (rhodopsin) and nose (olfactory receptors) run on GPCRs. One of the biggest receptor families humans have.

  1. Ligand binds the GPCR → the G protein activates → GDP detaches from the α subunit.
  2. A molecule of GTP replaces the GDP.
  3. The activated G protein separates into its α, β and γ subunits and dissociates from the receptor — the subunits then carry the message to effectors.
  4. Both diffuse laterally along the membrane, interacting with other membrane proteins and second messengers.

Adrenaline's pathway (C2.1.10): adrenaline is a water-soluble amine hormone from the adrenal glands above the kidneys. It binds GPCRs found on a huge variety of cells → system-wide fight-or-flight:

  • G protein activates adenylyl cyclase (a membrane enzyme)
  • → converts ATP → cAMP (cyclic AMP, the SECOND MESSENGER)
  • → cAMP activates protein kinases → glycogen → glucose, heart rate up, etc.

⚡ AMPLIFICATION: one adrenaline molecule → many cAMP → cascade. ONE "FIRE!" EMPTIES THE STADIUM.

Fight-or-flight effects to know: heart rate ↑, muscle contraction, metabolism ↑ (fuel release), ventilation ↑, pupils dilate.

Speed check (textbook): liver cells break down glycogen and release glucose into the blood within seconds of an epinephrine signal.

NOS — NAMING CONVENTIONS: "adrenaline" (Latin: ad = at, ren = kidney) and "epinephrine" (Greek: epi = above, nephros = kidney) both name the same hormone — produced at the adrenal gland, above the kidney. Most of the world says adrenaline; North America says epinephrine; the IUPAC chemical name is (R)-1-(3,4-dihydroxyphenyl)-2-methylaminoethanol. Shared naming conventions are international cooperation in science — though "epinephrine" risks confusion with the stimulant drug ephedrine, which is why some prefer "adrenaline". Use either in the exam — both accepted.

BOUNCER 3 — THE PHOTOCOPIER

RECEPTOR TYROSINE KINASE (RTK) · example: INSULIN · C2.1.11

The bouncer who photocopies the message and hands copies to everyone in the club.

What an RTK is: a transmembrane kinase — an enzyme that adds a phosphate group from ATP to a molecule (that's phosphorylation). This one phosphorylates the amino acid tyrosine in proteins. Adding phosphate changes a protein's shape and activity.

  1. Insulin binds its receptor on the plasma membrane.
  2. The receptor has two intracellular tails — each one a tyrosine kinase enzyme — that connect to form a dimer on binding.
  3. Each tail phosphorylates tyrosine residues on the other tail (cross-phosphorylation).
  4. This recruits relay proteins — they act as second messengers and activate several signal transduction pathways.
  5. One response: vesicles containing glucose transporters (GLUT4) move to the plasma membrane and fuse with it → the transporters are channel proteins → glucose enters by facilitated diffusion → used in cell respiration.

🔓 NO INSULIN = NO GLUT4 AT THE DOOR = GLUCOSE LOCKED OUTSIDE = DIABETES.

EXAM BIT: the endpoint of the insulin pathway is vesicle movement to the membrane, not the glucose transport itself. End your answer there.
💎

BOUNCER 4 — THE VIP LOUNGE

INTRACELLULAR RECEPTOR · example: STEROIDS (oestradiol, progesterone, testosterone) · C2.1.12

Steroids don't queue. They're fat-soluble, so they diffuse straight through the plasma membrane (in the blood they ride bound to transport proteins).

  1. Steroid diffuses into the cell → binds its receptor in the cytoplasm or nucleus.
  2. The activated hormone–receptor complex = a TRANSCRIPTION FACTOR.
  3. It binds to specific DNA sequences and helps RNA polymerase attach to the promoter.
  4. Gene transcription switches on → new proteins → cellular change.

Speed: slow (minutes to hours) but the effect lasts. Testosterone → male sex characteristics + muscle growth (textbook example: the androgen–receptor complex boosts the FADS1 gene → more fats in prostate cells); oestradiol → female characteristics + menstrual cycle; progesterone → pregnancy (implantation, milk production).

💎 VIPs GO STRAIGHT TO THE BOSS — no bouncers, no second messengers, no cascade. Direct line to the DNA.

SECOND MESSENGERS GALLERY 📨

When the first messenger (the ligand) can't get inside, the receptor sends second messengers in instead:

cAMP

Made from ATP by adenylyl cyclase. Adrenaline's messenger.

Ca²⁺

Calcium ions — from outside or the SR.

Nitric oxide (NO)

Gas, diffuses freely.

Protein kinases

Enzymes that phosphorylate the next protein in line.

Second messengers = amplification: each step activates more molecules than the last.

Act 4 · C2.1.2

THE PARTY 🦑

Bacteria don't have a club — so they build one. When the room gets full enough, the lights come on.

QUORUM SENSING

Quorum sensing = bacteria regulating their behaviour based on population density. A quorum = the fixed minimum number of individuals needed for a meeting to go ahead (like the UN Security Council's two-thirds rule). For bacteria: enough cells to switch on a group activity.

  1. Each bacterium releases a little ligand called an autoinducer.
  2. Lone bacterium: autoinducer diffuses away, concentration stays low → nothing happens.
  3. Bacteria multiply → more autoinducer in the local environment.
  4. When the threshold concentration is hit → synchronised change across the whole population.

LIGHTS ON WHEN THE ROOM'S FULL. 🎉

VIBRIO FISCHERI → BIOLUMINESCENCE ✨

  • Vibrio fischeri releases autoinducers that bind to an intracellular receptor called LuxR in the cytoplasm (nice callback to Act 3!).
  • The LuxR–autoinducer complex binds DNA → transcription switched on → the enzyme luciferase is made.
  • Luciferase catalyses an oxidation reaction — over 80% of its energy comes out as greenish-blue light. Free-living bacteria don't glow: no quorum, no function, no wasted energy.

THE BOBBTAIL SQUID DEAL 🤝

Vibrio fischeri lives inside the bobtail squid — a mutualism (both win):

  • Squid gets: camouflage — the glow lights up its underside so it's invisible from below against the bright surface.
  • Bacteria get: organic compounds produced by the squid's metabolism.

🦷 Textbook extra: quorum sensing builds biofilms too — bacteria on teeth secrete glue-like chemicals, stick to the tooth surface and form dental plaque. Quorum sensing is interaction (signals pass cell-to-cell); the group behaviours it triggers are interdependence (they only work if many cells take part).

Act 5 · C2.1.13 · C2.1.14

VOLUME CONTROL 🎚

Two hormones with very specific jobs, and the DJ's two volume knobs: thermostat (calm it down) and snowball (amp it up).

OESTRADIOL → THE HYPOTHALAMUS 🧠

Oestradiol (an oestrogen, made by ovarian follicular cells) travels in the blood to cells of the hypothalamus.

  1. Binds its receptor in the cytoplasm of the hypothalamus cell → complex moves to the nucleus = transcription factor.
  2. Enhances transcription of GnRH mRNA (gonadotropin-releasing hormone) — just before and during ovulation.
  3. GnRH → anterior pituitary → releases FSH and LH.
  4. FSH/LH control the menstrual cycle, acting on cells in the uterus.

🔥 THE CLEVER BIT: at different stages of the menstrual cycle oestradiol can INHIBIT or PROMOTE GnRH — so one hormone drives negative OR positive feedback. One hormone, both knobs.

PROGESTERONE → THE ENDOMETRIUM 🏠

Progesterone binds its intracellular receptor in the endometrial cells (the uterine lining).

  1. Complex = transcription factor → activates a specific gene: insulin-like growth factor.
  2. Growth factor promotes cell proliferation.
  3. Result: the endometrial lining thickens — ready for implantation during the menstrual cycle and pregnancy.

Progesterone also coordinates milk production by the mammary glands.

NEGATIVE FEEDBACK = THERMOSTAT 🌡

The response reverses the change — pushes the system back toward equilibrium. The most common type (homeostasis runs on it).

  • Testosterone (textbook example): hypothalamus → GnRH → anterior pituitary → LH → Leydig cells in the testes make testosterone. Rising testosterone → less LH from the pituitary AND less GnRH from the hypothalamus — the end-product shuts off its own production.
  • Thermoregulation: body temp off → thyroxin drives changes that restore it.
  • Blood sugar: glucose high → insulin lowers it; glucose low → glucagon raises it.
  • Osmoregulation: dehydrated → ADH secreted → water retained; hydrated → ADH inhibited.

POSITIVE FEEDBACK = SNOWBALL ⛄

The response reinforces the change — amplifies it until the signal stops. Rare, dramatic, one-way.

  • Childbirth: oxytocin → contractions → more oxytocin → baby out. 🍼
  • Calcium-induced calcium release (textbook example): IP₃ binds an IP₃ receptor on the ER → a little Ca²⁺ escapes → that Ca²⁺ activates a neighbouring calcium channel → even more Ca²⁺ floods out. The product amplifies its own production.
  • Blood clotting: platelets release clotting factors → more platelets aggregate at the injury.
  • Cell division: CDKs trigger production of more cyclins → cycle accelerates.

Practice

SELF-TEST ✍

Click an answer. Instant feedback. No mercy.

1. Name the four bouncers in order of speed, fastest first.

✅ Correct — millisecond channel, then the cascades, then the slow-but-lasting VIPs.
❌ The door (ion channel) is millisecond-fast; VIP steroids are the slow ones (minutes–hours).

2. Order the G protein cycle: a) GTP replaces GDP, b) α + βγ dissociate, c) ligand binds the GPCR, d) GDP leaves the α subunit.

✅ Ligand binds → GDP out → GTP in → split and deliver. "GDP OUT, GTP IN, SPLIT."
❌ GDP must leave BEFORE GTP can arrive. LIGAND BINDS → GDP OUT → GTP IN → SPLIT.

3. When ACh binds its channel, what exactly happens to the post-synaptic membrane?

✅ Na⁺ in → depolarisation → may open voltage-gated channels → impulse propagates.
❌ It's Na⁺ in → depolarisation. (Ca²⁺ is a different story — that's exocytosis at the synapse.)

4. A T lymphocyte releases cytokines that stimulate the SAME T cell to divide. What signalling type is this?

✅ Autocrine = self-signalling. "Auto" = self. That's literally the whole trick.
❌ Autocrine = self. Paracrine = neighbour. Endocrine = bloodstream. This one is SELF → autocrine.

5. Where's the line between a peptide hormone and a protein hormone?

✅ <50 = peptide (ADH, oxytocin) · ≥50 = protein (insulin, glucagon).
❌ The cutoff is 50 amino acids. Peptide < 50 (ADH, oxytocin); protein ≥ 50 (insulin, glucagon).

6. What enzyme makes Vibrio fischeri glow in the squid?

✅ Luciferase — switched on when quorum is reached.
❌ It's luciferase. (Adenylyl cyclase makes cAMP in the adrenaline pathway — different party.)

7. ADH is secreted when you're dehydrated and inhibited when you're hydrated. Which feedback type?

✅ Negative (osmoregulation). Dehydrated → ADH → retain water → back to normal → ADH off.
❌ Negative — it counteracts dehydration. Positive would mean dehydration → MORE water loss. Chaos.

8. Why can oestradiol drive BOTH negative AND positive feedback?

✅ It regulates GnRH both ways — switch the knob up (positive, e.g. LH surge) or down (negative, e.g. after ovulation).
❌ Oestradiol can inhibit or promote GnRH — so the same hormone can push the loop either direction.

9. What does a receptor do that an enzyme doesn't?

✅ Receptors don't catalyse — the signalling chemical stays bound, then leaves unchanged. Enzymes convert substrates into products and release them.
❌ Receptors hold and release the ligand unchanged — no catalysis. Converting substrate to product is what enzymes do.

10. IP₃ opens a calcium channel on the ER; the escaping Ca²⁺ opens a NEIGHBOURING channel; more Ca²⁺ floods out. Which feedback type?

✅ Positive — calcium-induced calcium release. A snowball: each bit of Ca²⁺ recruits more.
❌ Positive. The end-product (Ca²⁺) amplifies the starting point (more Ca²⁺ release) — that's the textbook definition of positive feedback.
