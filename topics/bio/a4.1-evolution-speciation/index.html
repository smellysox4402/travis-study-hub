A4.1 EVOLUTION & SPECIATION

What evolution is, the evidence for it (sequences, selective breeding, homologous structures, convergent evolution), and how new species form by speciation.

A4.1.1

WHAT EVOLUTION IS

Evolution is a change in the heritable characteristics of a population — this distinguishes Darwinian evolution from Lamarckism.

Evolution = change in the heritable characteristics of a population over generations — driven by changes in allele frequencies. Crucially, it is the population that evolves, not an individual.

Lamarckism vs Darwin: Lamarck thought characteristics (acquired traits) were inherited; Darwinian evolution is change in the heritable characteristics of a population (via variation + selection).
A4.1.2

EVIDENCE FROM BASE & AMINO ACID SEQUENCES

Base sequences in DNA/RNA and amino acid sequences in proteins give powerful evidence of common ancestry.

Organisms that share many base sequences (or amino acid sequences) are more closely related — the more similar their sequences, the more recent their common ancestor. Sequence data is strong, objective evidence for evolution.

A4.1.3

EVIDENCE FROM SELECTIVE BREEDING

Selective breeding of domesticated animals and crop plants shows how humans have changed heritable traits over generations.

By breeding only the individuals with desired traits, humans have dramatically changed domesticated animals (e.g. dogs, cattle) and crop plants (e.g. wheat, corn). This is evolution in action — an artificial example of change in heritable characteristics.

A4.1.4

EVIDENCE FROM HOMOLOGOUS STRUCTURES

Homologous structures share a common evolutionary origin, e.g. the pentadactyl limb of vertebrates.

Homologous structures have the same underlying (evolutionary) origin even if used differently. The pentadactyl limb (five-digit limb) in humans, cats, whales and bats is the classic example — all share the same bones from a common ancestor, adapted for different functions.

FIG 1 — The pentadactyl limb: the same skeletal plan (homologous) from a common ancestor, used for grasping, flying, swimming and more.
A4.1.5

CONVERGENT EVOLUTION & ANALOGOUS STRUCTURES

Analogous structures have the same function but different evolutionary origins — the result of convergent evolution.

Convergent evolution produces analogous structures — features with the same function but different evolutionary origins. For example, the wings of a bird and the wings of an insect both provide flight but evolved independently.

Homologous vs analogous: homologous = same origin, may differ in function; analogous = same function, different origin.
A4.1.6

SPECIATION

New species form by the splitting of pre-existing species — the only way new species appear, increasing biodiversity.

Speciation = the splitting of one species into two or more new ones. It is the only way new species have appeared, and it increases biodiversity.

A4.1.7

REPRODUCTIVE ISOLATION & DIFFERENTIAL SELECTION

Reproductive isolation and differential selection drive speciation. Geographical isolation can achieve reproductive isolation.

For speciation to occur, populations must become reproductively isolated (no longer interbreeding) and face differential selection in different environments. Geographical isolation (a physical barrier) is one way to achieve reproductive isolation.

Example: bonobos and common chimpanzees were separated by the Congo River; isolated from each other, they diverged and became separate species.

AHL · A4.1.8

SYMPATRIC vs ALLOPATRIC SPECIATION

Reproductive isolation separates populations; in allopatric speciation it is geographic, in sympatric it is not (e.g. by polyploidy or habitat differences).

AHL · A4.1.9

ADAPTIVE RADIATION

Adaptive radiation lets closely related species coexist without competing, increasing biodiversity.

Adaptive radiation = one ancestral species rapidly diversifies into many species, each adapted to a different niche (e.g. Darwin's finches on the Galápagos, each with a different beak). Because they exploit different resources, they can coexist without competing — increasing biodiversity.

AHL · A4.1.10

BARRIERS TO HYBRIDIZATION & STERILE HYBRIDS

Barriers that prevent hybridization and the sterility of hybrids stop allele mixing between species.

  • Courtship behaviour often prevents hybridization in animals — individuals only mate with a partner performing the correct display.
  • Where hybridization does occur, the offspring are often sterile (e.g. a mule), so genes cannot flow back between the species.
  • These mechanisms keep the gene pools of different species separate.
AHL · A4.1.11

ABRUPT SPECIATION IN PLANTS — HYBRIDIZATION & POLYPLOIDY

In plants, hybridization followed by polyploidy can create a new species abruptly.

If two plants hybridize and the hybrid undergoes polyploidy (a doubling of the chromosome number), it is reproductively isolated from both parents — because it can no longer interbreed with them. This creates a new species in a single step (abrupt speciation).

Example: the genus Persicaria (knotweed or smartweed) contains many species that have arisen this way, by hybridization and polyploidy.
