A1.2.1

DNA AS THE GENETIC MATERIAL

DNA is the genetic material of all living organisms. Some viruses use RNA instead, but viruses are not considered living.

Every living organism stores its genetic information in DNA — the instructions for building and running the body. The only exception is a group of viruses that use RNA as their genetic material, but because viruses cannot reproduce on their own and lack cell structure, they are not classified as living.

  • DNA = the hereditary molecule in all living organisms.
  • Some viruses use RNA — but viruses are not living, so DNA is still the genetic material of life.
A1.2.2

COMPONENTS OF A NUCLEOTIDE

A nucleotide is made of a phosphate group, a pentose (5-carbon) sugar and a nitrogenous base. Draw phosphate as a circle, the sugar as a pentagon and the base as a rectangle.

DNA and RNA are polymers of nucleotides. Each nucleotide has three parts:

  • Phosphate group (−PO₄) — drawn as a circle.
  • Pentose sugar (5 carbons) — drawn as a pentagon. In DNA it is deoxyribose; in RNA it is ribose.
  • Nitrogenous base — drawn as a rectangle. One or two rings of carbon and nitrogen.
FIG 1 — A nucleotide: phosphate (circle), pentose sugar (pentagon), nitrogenous base (rectangle).
A1.2.3

THE SUGAR-PHOSPHATE BACKBONE

Sugar–phosphate bonding forms a continuous, strong chain of covalently bonded atoms along each strand — the 'backbone' of DNA and RNA.

In a chain of nucleotides, the phosphate group of one nucleotide bonds to the sugar of the next. This creates a long, continuous chain of covalently bonded sugar–phosphate–sugar–phosphate units.

The sugar–phosphate backbone is very strong because the bonds are covalent. The bases stick out from the backbone and carry the genetic code.

A1.2.4

THE NITROGENOUS BASES

The bases form the code. You need to know their names for both DNA and RNA.

The four bases in DNA are adenine, thymine, guanine and cytosine (A, T, G, C). In RNA, thymine is replaced by uracil (U), giving A, U, G, C.

  • Purines (two rings): adenine (A) and guanine (G).
  • Pyrimidines (one ring): thymine (T), cytosine (C) and uracil (U).
A1.2.5

RNA — A POLYMER OF NUCLEOTIDES

RNA is a polymer formed by condensation reactions linking nucleotide monomers. You should be able to draw and recognise single nucleotides and RNA polymers.

RNA is built by condensation of nucleotides — each bond forms with the loss of a water molecule. It is usually single-stranded and much shorter than DNA. RNA's sugar is ribose.

A1.2.6

THE DNA DOUBLE HELIX

DNA is a double helix of two antiparallel strands linked by hydrogen bonds between complementary base pairs (A–T, G–C). Draw the strands antiparallel.

DNA is double-stranded. The two strands run in antiparallel directions and are held together by hydrogen bonds between complementary bases: A pairs with T and G pairs with C.

  • You do NOT need to draw the helical twist — a straight ladder arrangement showing the two antiparallel strands is enough.
  • You do NOT need the relative lengths of purines/pyrimidines or the exact number of hydrogen bonds.
FIG 2 — DNA as two antiparallel strands. A pairs with T and G pairs with C, linked by hydrogen bonds (the base-pair "rungs").

A1.2.8

COMPLEMENTARY BASE PAIRING

Complementary base pairing (A–T, G–C) — based on hydrogen bonding — lets genetic information be replicated and expressed.

Because A always pairs with T and G always pairs with C, knowing one strand lets you know the other. This complementarity is what makes it possible to replicate DNA (each strand is a template for a new one) and to transcribe it into RNA (a complementary copy).

A1.2.9

THE CAPACITY OF DNA FOR INFORMATION

Because a DNA molecule can be any length with any base sequence, DNA can store an enormous amount of information with great economy.

The number of possible sequences is practically limitless. A sequence of n bases has 4ⁿ possible arrangements — so even a moderately long molecule can encode an astronomical amount of information in a tiny space.

A1.2.10

A UNIVERSAL GENETIC CODE

The genetic code is essentially the same across all life forms, which is evidence of universal common ancestry.

The same codon → amino acid rules apply in bacteria, plants and animals. This universality is strong evidence that all life shares a common ancestor. (You are not required to memorise specific examples.)

AHL · A1.2.11

DIRECTIONALITY — 5′ TO 3′

DNA and RNA are built with 5′ to 3′ linkages in the sugar-phosphate backbone, which matters for replication, transcription and translation.

Each nucleotide has a 5′ end (where the phosphate attaches to the sugar's carbon 5) and a 3′ end (where there is a free −OH on carbon 3). New nucleotides are always added to the 3′ end of the growing strand.

  • The two DNA strands are antiparallel: one runs 5′→3′, the other 3′→5′.
  • DNA polymerase reads the template 3′→5′ and builds the new strand 5′→3′.
  • RNA is transcribed and read 5′→3′, and at the ribosome mRNA is read 5′→3′.
FIG 4 — Antiparallel strands: one runs 5′→3′, the other 3′→5′. New nucleotides are added at the 3′ end.
AHL · A1.2.12

PURINE-PYRIMIDINE BONDING & HELIX STABILITY

A–T and G–C pairs have equal length, so the DNA helix has the same three-dimensional structure whatever the base sequence.

A purine (A or G) always pairs with a pyrimidine (T or C). Because a purine+tiny pyrimidine pair has the same width as every other pair, the two backbones stay a constant distance apart — keeping the helix a uniform shape regardless of the sequence.

Exam point: if a purine paired with a purine, the helix would bulge; if a pyrimidine paired with a pyrimidine, it would pinch. The purine-pyrimidine rule keeps it even-width.
AHL · A1.2.13

THE NUCLEOSOME

A nucleosome is DNA wrapped around a core of eight histone proteins, with a linker histone holding the strands together.

In eukaryotes, DNA is packaged by wrapping around proteins called histones. A nucleosome = DNA coiled around a core of eight histones (an octamer), plus an additional histone (H1) attached to the linker DNA that connects neighbouring nucleosomes.

FIG 5 — A nucleosome: DNA wrapped around a core of eight histones, with an H1 histone on the linker DNA.
AHL · A1.2.14

THE HERSHEY-CHASE EXPERIMENT

Hershey and Chase showed DNA (not protein) is the genetic material by using radioisotopes to label the two parts of a bacteriophage.

They grew bacteriophages (viruses that infect bacteria) in media containing radioisotopes: ³²P labels DNA (phosphorus is in DNA, not protein) and ³⁵S labels protein (sulfur is in protein, not DNA).

  • Infected bacteria were then separated from the empty viral shells.
  • The ³²P (DNA) was found inside the bacteria — confirming that DNA enters and carries the genetic instructions.
  • The ³⁵S (protein) stayed in the shells outside.
  • Conclusion: DNA is the genetic material, not protein.
Nature of science: the experiment became possible only once radioisotopes were available as research tools — technology can open up new experiments.
AHL · A1.2.15

CHARGAF'S DATA & FALSIFICATION

Chargaff found the amounts of adenine and thymine (and of guanine and cytosine) are roughly equal across diverse organisms. This falsified the tetranucleotide hypothesis.

Across many species, A ≈ T and G ≈ C. This is exactly what you'd expect from complementary pairing, and it helped Watson and Crick build the correct model.

Chargaff's data also falsified the earlier tetranucleotide hypothesis (which claimed DNA was a repeating sequence of the same four bases). The idea of falsification — a hypothesis is accepted until evidence disproves it — is central to the nature of science.

Nature of science: the "problem of induction" (no amount of confirming data proves a theory) is addressed by the "certainty of falsification" — one decisive contradictory result can reject a hypothesis.
