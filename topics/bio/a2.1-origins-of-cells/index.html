A2.1 ORIGINS OF CELLS

A2.1.1

CONDITIONS ON EARLY EARTH

The early Earth lacked free oxygen and ozone, had higher CO₂ and methane, and so was hotter with more UV — conditions that may have made carbon compounds form spontaneously.

Around 4 billion years ago Earth was very different: no free oxygen (so no ozone layer), high levels of carbon dioxide and methane, which produced a greenhouse effect, and intense ultraviolet light reaching the surface because there was no ozone shield.

  • No O₂ → no ozone → more UV reaches the surface.
  • High CO₂ + methane → higher temperatures.
  • These conditions may have caused a variety of carbon compounds to form spontaneously by chemical processes that no longer occur today.
A2.1.2

CELLS — THE SMALLEST UNITS OF SELF-SUSTAINING LIFE

A cell is the smallest unit that can sustain itself. You should be able to distinguish living from non-living, and explain why viruses are considered non-living.

The cell is the smallest unit of life that can maintain itself — carry out metabolism, grow, respond and reproduce independently. Viruses are not cells and are considered non-living because they:

  • cannot reproduce on their own (they hijack a host's machinery);
  • have no metabolism — no energy production or chemical reactions of their own;
  • are not made of cells, and cannot maintain their own internal environment.
A2.1.3

THE CHALLENGE OF EXPLAINING THE ORIGIN OF CELLS

Cells are highly complex and today can only be produced by division of pre-existing cells. Explaining a spontaneous origin is hard.

Cells are so complex that today they form only by division of existing cells. To explain how the very first cells arose, four things had to emerge:

  • Catalysis — speeding up the needed reactions.
  • Self-replication — copying the genetic material.
  • Self-assembly — molecules coming together on their own.
  • Compartmentalization — a membrane enclosing the reactions.
Nature of science: science claims must be testable. Here the hypotheses are very hard to test — we cannot replicate the exact pre-biotic conditions, and the first protocells did not fossilise.
A2.1.4

EVIDENCE — THE MILLER-UREY EXPERIMENT

Miller and Urey simulated early-Earth conditions and produced organic (carbon) compounds — evidence that pre-biotic carbon compounds could form spontaneously.

In 1953, Stanley Miller and Harold Urey set up a closed apparatus containing water, and gases thought to be in the early atmosphere (methane, ammonia, hydrogen), and passed electric sparks (simulating lightning) through it.

  • Result: within days, a variety of organic compounds — including amino acids — had formed in the water.
  • Evaluation: it showed organic molecules CAN form spontaneously under simulated early-Earth conditions; BUT it used the specific gas mixture the scientists guessed, which may not have matched the real atmosphere, and it only made small molecules — not living cells.
FIG 1 — The Miller–Urey apparatus: water + simple gases + electric sparks produced amino acids, showing organic compounds can form under simulated early-Earth conditions.
A2.1.5

FORMATION OF VESICLES

Fatty acids can spontaneously coalesce into spherical bilayers — membrane-bound vesicles. A compartment is needed so internal chemistry differs from outside.

Some molecules (like fatty acids) have a hydrophilic head and a hydrophobic tail. In water they spontaneously form bilayers that enclose a space — a vesicle or protocell.

  • This gives a membrane-bound compartment.
  • Inside, the chemistry can become different from the outside — concentrations can build up, and reactions can be kept separated and controlled.
  • This compartmentalization is a key step toward a living cell.
FIG 2 — Fatty acid molecules coalesce into a spherical bilayer (vesicle). The membrane compartment allows the inside chemistry to differ from the outside.
A2.1.6

RNA AS THE FIRST GENETIC MATERIAL

RNA can both store information and catalyse reactions, so it may have been the first genetic material AND the first enzyme.

RNA is special because it can replicate (template copying) and also has catalytic activity. So in the earliest cells, RNA may have acted as both the genetic material and the enzymes.

  • Ribozymes are RNA molecules that catalyse reactions.
  • Even today, the ribosome uses RNA (a ribozyme) to catalyse peptide bond formation during protein synthesis — a relic of the RNA world.
A2.1.7

EVIDENCE FOR A LAST UNIVERSAL COMMON ANCESTOR

The universal genetic code and shared genes are evidence that all life descends from a last universal common ancestor (LUCA).

If all life shares a last universal common ancestor (LUCA), then we should see shared features across all organisms — and we do:

  • The genetic code is essentially universal (same codon → amino acid rules).
  • Many core genes are shared across all domains of life (e.g. basic metabolic and ribosome genes).
  • It is likely other forms of life evolved but went extinct, outcompeted by LUCA and its descendants.
A2.1.8

ESTIMATING WHEN LIFE BEGAN

Scientists use different approaches to date the first cells and LUCA, and the times involved are immense.

Dates are estimated using the fossil record (oldest microfossils ~3.5 billion years), radiometric dating of rocks, and molecular clocks (comparing DNA/protein sequences between species). These point to: first living cells ~3.5–3.8 billion years ago, and Earth itself ~4.5 billion years old.

Takeaway: life has been evolving on Earth for an immense length of time — around 3.5 billion years.
A2.1.9

LUCA & HYDROTHERMAL VENTS

Evidence suggests LUCA evolved near hydrothermal vents on ancient seafloors.

Two lines of evidence support a hydrothermal vent origin for LUCA:

  • Fossil evidence: fossilised life has been found in ancient seafloor hydrothermal vent precipitates (mineral-rich deposits).
  • Genomic evidence: conserved sequences from genomic analysis point to an organism adapted to hot, mineral-rich, oxygen-free environments — matching vents.
FIG 3 — A hydrothermal vent. Fossilised life in ancient vent precipitates and conserved gene sequences suggest LUCA evolved in these hot, mineral-rich, oxygen-free conditions.
