D3.1.1 – D3.1.2

ASEXUAL VS SEXUAL REPRODUCTION

Asexual reproduction = the same track played on repeat (continuity, identical clones). Sexual reproduction = produces variation every generation and needs meiosis AND gamete fusion to keep the chromosome number stable.

🔄

Identical clones, or genetic variation?

asexual vs sexual reproduction · change vs continuity · meiosis + fusion cycle

Asexual reproduction has a single parent: offspring form by mitosis alone, so every offspring is genetically identical to the parent (and to each other) — clones. No gametes fuse, no variation is generated, and the parent's exact genome is passed on. It works brilliantly in an unchanging environment, where the winning formula doesn't need to change.

Sexual reproduction needs two parents and involves meiosis (halving the chromosome number when gametes form) followed by gamete fusion (doubling it back at fertilization). Offspring are genetically different from both parents and from each other — variation every generation. That variety is the species' insurance when the environment changes.

FIG 1 — Asexual reproduction: mitosis only, clones, no variation. Sexual = meiosis halves 2n → n gametes, fusion doubles n → 2n zygote, variation every generation.
Exam gold: "explain the role of meiosis in a sexual life cycle" questions want the halving argument: if gametes were formed by mitosis, each generation would double the chromosome number. Meiosis produces haploid gametes (n); fusion restores the diploid number (2n) in the zygote — the cycle keeps the number constant and shuffles alleles into new combinations.
D3.1.2 – D3.1.3

SEXUAL REPRODUCTION & VARIATION

Gametes are the half-price tickets to the next generation: each carries one set of chromosomes (haploid, n). Male and female gametes are built completely differently — that difference is anisogamy.

🎟️

Two ticket types, two jobs

anisogamy · male vs female gametes · haploid vs diploid

Anisogamy is the difference in size and behaviour between the two gamete types. The male gamete is small and motile — it travels to the female gamete. The female gamete is large, sessile (it stays put) and packed with food reserves that nourish the early embryo. Because building a big food-stocked gamete is expensive, females produce far fewer gametes than males.

Gametes are haploid (n) — half the chromosome number of the parent's body cells, which are diploid (2n). All plants and animals are anisogamous: two distinct gamete types is the rule across kingdoms.

FIG 2 — Anisogamy: the male gamete is a cheap fast courier ticket (small, motile, mass-produced); the female gamete is a loaded lounge pass (large, sessile, stocked with food for the embryo). Both are haploid (n).

Remember: anisogamy is any difference between gamete types — humans have it, mosses have it, even algae have it. The motile/sessile + reserves split explains everything else (numbers, size, who travels).
D3.1.4

THE MALE REPRODUCTIVE SYSTEM

The male reproductive system is a production line for sperm: made in the testis (kept cool in the scrotum), stored in the epididymis, mixed into semen by the accessory glands, and delivered through the urethra by the penis.

🎧

Who makes, stores and delivers the gametes

testis · scrotum · epididymis · sperm duct · seminal vesicle · prostate · urethra · penis

The testis produces both sperm and the hormone testosterone. It hangs in the scrotum, which holds the testes below core body temperature — sperm need it cooler. Epididymis stores sperm until ejaculation; the sperm duct (vas deferens) transfers them during ejaculation.

The seminal vesicle and prostate gland secrete an alkaline fluid with proteins and fructose — mixed with sperm this forms semen. The urethra carries semen during ejaculation (and urine at other times) through the penis, which penetrates the vagina and ejaculates near the cervix to give sperm the shortest trip to the egg.

FIG 3 — Male reproductive system chain: testis (sperm + testosterone) → scrotum (cooler room) → epididymis (storage) → sperm duct (transfer) → seminal vesicle + prostate (alkali + proteins + fructose = semen) → urethra + penis (delivery near the cervix).
Don't mix up: the sperm duct carries sperm only, the urethra carries semen and urine. And it's the seminal vesicle + prostate that add the fructose fuel and alkali — sperm alone are just swimmers without provisions.
D3.1.4

MALE HORMONE CONTROL

The female reproductive system: the ovary releases the egg, the oviduct hosts fertilization, the uterus hosts the pregnancy, and the cervix, vagina and vulva guard the entrances.

🎤

The reproductive organs, from ovary to vagina

ovary · oviduct · uterus · cervix · vagina · vulva

The ovary produces eggs plus the hormones oestradiol and progesterone. The oviduct collects the egg at ovulation, is the site of fertilization, and moves the early embryo to the uterus. The uterus provides for the embryo and then the foetus during pregnancy.

The cervix protects the developing foetus and dilates to form the birth canal. The vagina stimulates the penis and ejaculation, and also serves as the birth canal. The vulva protects the internal parts of the female reproductive system.

FIG 4 — Female reproductive system: ovary (eggs + oestradiol + progesterone) → oviduct (collects egg, fertilization HERE) → uterus (hosts embryo then foetus) → cervix (protects, dilates) → vagina (stimulates ejaculation, birth canal) → vulva (protects the entrance).
Exam gold: anatomy questions love "state the function of…" — memorise one crisp function per structure. The two most-tested: the oviduct is the site of fertilization, and the uterus lining (endometrium) is where the embryo implants (Act 14).
D3.1.5

THE FEMALE REPRODUCTIVE SYSTEM

The menstrual cycle is a monthly cycle (~28 days): the ovarian cycle books a follicle and releases an egg at Day 14 (ovulation), while the uterine cycle rebuilds the endometrium and, if no embryo arrives, sheds it as menstruation.

📅

Two calendars, one booking system

ovarian cycle · uterine cycle · FSH · LH · oestradiol · progesterone · ovulation Day 14

The ovarian cycle: the follicular phase develops follicles (one egg matures), then around Day 14 the follicle bursts and the egg is released — ovulation. The follicle wall becomes the corpus luteum (luteal phase), which breaks down if fertilization doesn't happen. The uterine cycle runs in parallel: oestradiol repairs and thickens the endometrium, progesterone maintains it for implantation; with no embryo it breaks down and is shed as menstruation (Day 1).

FSH (pituitary) stimulates follicle development and oestradiol secretion. LH (pituitary) has a sharp mid-cycle peak that completes meiosis in the oocyte, triggers ovulation and promotes the corpus luteum. Oestradiol from the follicle wall thickens the endometrium, boosts FSH receptors (positive feedback) then, at high levels, inhibits FSH and stimulates LH. Progesterone from the corpus luteum maintains the endometrium and inhibits FSH and LH.

FIG 5 — The monthly booking: LH's sharp Day-14 spike fires ovulation; FSH grows the follicle; oestradiol thickens the endometrium (positive feedback first, then negative); progesterone from the corpus luteum holds the lining — until it breaks down and the show resets at Day 1.

Remember: "oestradiol runs the first half, progesterone runs the second half." Oestradiol peaks just before Day 14 (that rising level is what triggers the LH surge); progesterone only appears after ovulation, from the corpus luteum.
D3.1.6

THE MENSTRUAL CYCLE

Fertilization is a meeting at the door: sperm swim toward chemicals released by the egg, one sperm breaks through the zona pellucida, and the door slams shut behind it so the zygote stays diploid.

🚪

One sperm, one egg, one fusion

zona pellucida · glycoprotein layer · sperm receptors · diploid zygote

Sperm detect chemicals released by the egg and swim toward it — directional swimming. The egg is surrounded by follicle cells and a glycoprotein layer, the zona pellucida. Sperm digest through the glycoproteins and bind with plasma membrane proteins of the egg.

The first sperm to penetrate the zona fuses with the egg membrane and its nucleus enters. The zona pellucida hardens immediately, blocking any more sperm — so the fertilized egg is a diploid zygote. The sperm tail and most mitochondria stay outside or are destroyed: the offspring's mitochondria come from the mother.

FIG 6 — Fertilization: sperm swim toward egg chemicals, digest through the zona pellucida, and the first to fuse wins — the zona hardens, the door slams, and the zygote stays diploid (2n).
Don't mix up: fertilization happens in the oviduct, not the uterus. And the zona pellucida hardening you meet here is the same "cortical reaction" you'll meet in detail at AHL (Act 13) — the door-slam that blocks polyspermy.
D3.1.7

GAMETES & FERTILISATION

IVF is reproduction in the lab: the natural cycle is muted, many follicles are forced to grow with FSH, hCG triggers final maturation, and fertilization happens in a dish before the embryo is placed in the uterus.

🧪

The protocol, beat by beat

down-regulation · FSH · hCG · egg collection · incubation · embryo transfer

Down-regulation (~2 weeks): a drug stops the pituitary secreting FSH and LH, so oestradiol and progesterone fall and the woman's cycle is suspended — the doctors now control the timing. Then daily FSH injections for 7–12 days make many follicles develop at once (8–15 is ideal, each around 18 mm).

When follicles reach ~18 mm, an hCG injection (LH-like) triggers final maturation. Eggs are collected 34–35 hours later with a micropipette guided by ultrasound. Each egg is mixed with 50,000–100,000 sperm in a dish and incubated at 37 °C. Embryos about 48 hours old are placed in the uterus, and extra progesterone (tablet in the vagina) maintains the lining.

FIG 7 — The IVF protocol: mute the cycle (down-regulation) → FSH for many follicles → hCG trigger → collect eggs 34–35 h later → mix with 50,000–100,000 sperm at 37 °C → transfer ~48 h embryos with progesterone support.
Exam gold: the "why down-regulation?" question — doctors suspend the natural cycle so they control when follicles mature. The "why hCG?" question — hCG mimics LH, giving the sharp LH-like surge that completes final maturation before collection.
D3.1.8 – D3.1.9

IVF — IN VITRO FERTILISATION

Flowers are insect-pollinated: the stamen (anther + filament) produces pollen, the carpel (stigma + style + ovary) catches it, and insect-pollinated flowers bribe couriers with deep nectar so they brush past the pollen machinery.

🌸

Structure of the flower, and how insects pollinate

stamen · carpel · pollen · ovule · pollination · insect-pollinated features

The stamen = anther (produces pollen by meiosis) held up by the filament. The carpel = stigma (catches pollen), style (connects to ovary), and ovary containing ovules. The egg is one of 8 haploid nuclei produced by meiosis plus three mitotic divisions. A pollen grain carries 3 haploid nuclei, two of which are the male gametes. Pollination is pollen transfer anther → stigma; the pollen tube grows down the style to the ovule, fertilization follows, and the zygote becomes an embryo with root, shoot and cotyledons.

Insect-pollinated flowers advertise: large bright petals (bright coloration + a landing platform), scent from petals, large spiky pollen grains that stick to insects (protein-rich food for them), a large sticky stigma, and nectaries that secrete nectar deep inside — insects must brush past anthers and stigma to reach it.

FIG 8 — The insect-pollinated flower: stamen (anther + filament) makes pollen by meiosis; carpel (stigma + style + ovary) catches it. Big petals + scent advertise; deep nectaries force insects to brush past anthers and stigma on the way to the prize.
Remember: pollen = 3 haploid nuclei, two are the male gametes; the egg = one of 8 haploid nuclei (meiosis + 3 mitotic divisions). Pollination (anther → stigma) is just delivery — fertilization happens later, down in the ovule.
D3.1.10 – D3.1.11

FERTILISATION — SITE & PROCESS

Cross-pollination is the courier service between plants — it delivers variation and hybrid vigour. Self-pollination is extreme inbreeding, so plants evolved timing tricks and a genetic rejection system (self-incompatibility) to stop it.

🚚

Pollen transfer between flowers — and self-incompatibility

cross-pollination · hybrid vigour · inbreeding depression · protandry · protogyny · S-alleles

Cross-pollination (pollen from the anther of one plant to the stigma of another) promotes genetic variation and produces offspring with hybrid vigour. Self-pollination is extreme inbreeding, which causes inbreeding depression: premature death, failure to thrive and infertility, as rare recessive alleles meet up.

Mechanisms preventing self-pollination: separate male and female flowers on the same plant (e.g. corn/maize); male and female flowers on different plants (ginkgo, stinging nettle); anthers and stigmas maturing at different times — protandry (anthers first, e.g. foxglove) or protogyny (stigma first, e.g. sacred lotus). Self-incompatibility is genetic: if pollen carries the same S-alleles as the plant, the pollen fails to germinate or its tube stops growing — the converse of the immune system, which rejects non-self while self-incompatibility rejects self.

FIG 9 — Cross-pollination between plants = variation + hybrid vigour; same-plant pollen is rejected by self-incompatibility (matching S-alleles stop the tube). Protandry, protogyny and dioecy physically separate the sexes in time or space.
Don't mix up: self-incompatibility is the converse of immunity — the immune system attacks non-self and tolerates self; self-incompatibility accepts non-self pollen (other S-alleles) and rejects self (matching S-alleles).
D3.1.12

SPERM TRANSPORT & CAPACITATION

Seeds take the tour bus out of town: dispersal moves seeds from the parent to a new germination site — reducing competition and spreading the species — via wind, animal coats, animal guts or explosive pods.

🚌

Dispersal ≠ pollination — and here's why it matters

seed dispersal · wind · animals · explosion · germination

Pollination moves pollen from anther to stigma; seed dispersal moves seeds from the parent plant to where they will germinate. Two separate processes, two separate journeys. Dispersal reduces competition between offspring and parent (and between siblings) and spreads the species to new territory.

Fruit types match the transport: dry + explosive pods (gorse, exploding cucumber, jewelweed); fleshy + attractive fruits eaten by animals, seeds passing through the gut; feathery/winged seeds for wind (maple samaras, dandelion); and hooks that catch animal coats (burrs). Germination starts from the seed's embryo: embryo root, embryo shoot and cotyledons.

FIG 10 — The tour bus: wind (maple samaras, dandelion), animal coats (burrs), animal guts (fleshy fruits) and explosion (gorse, jewelweed) — each reducing competition and spreading the species. Dispersal is a separate journey from pollination.
Exam gold: "why do plants disperse seeds?" → reduce competition between offspring and parent, and spread the species to new habitats. "Why is dispersal separate from pollination?" → pollination is delivery of pollen; dispersal is the relocation of seeds — different structures, different agents, different outcomes.
AHL · D3.1.13

HORMONES OF PREGNANCY

Puberty is the debut night: the hypothalamus starts with pulses of GnRH, the pituitary answers with FSH and LH, and the gonads' sex steroids remodel the body into its adult form.

🌙

The flower, the pollen tube and fertilisation

GnRH · hypothalamus · FSH · LH · testosterone · oestradiol · secondary sexual characteristics

GnRH (gonadotropin-releasing hormone) is a 10-amino-acid peptide made by a few hundred hypothalamic neurons and sent down a blood vessel straight to the pituitary. It is secreted in pulses (peaks at least hourly): lower-frequency pulses → FSH, higher-frequency → LH.

In males: FSH → testis growth; LH → testosterone from Leydig cells; testosterone drives penis growth, pubic hair and voice deepening (larynx growth). In females: FSH → follicle development; the follicle wall makes oestradiol; LH → corpus luteum after ovulation, which makes oestradiol + progesterone; oestradiol drives uterus enlargement, breast development and pubic/underarm hair; progesterone develops the mammary glands for lactation. GnRH secretion starts ~10 weeks after fertilization, stops at 4–6 months old, and resumes at puberty.

FIG 11 — Debut night chain: hypothalamus → GnRH pulses (slow = FSH, fast = LH) → pituitary → FSH/LH → gonads → sex steroids → secondary sexual characteristics. GnRH switches off between ~4–6 months and puberty.
Exam gold: the pulse-frequency detail is the AHL favourite: same hormone (GnRH), two messages (frequency) — slower pulses favour FSH, faster pulses favour LH. And "what restarts puberty?" is answered by the hypothalamus re-starting GnRH secretion.
AHL · D3.1.14

SPERMATOGENESIS VS OOGENESIS

Spermatogenesis is a factory — endless mitosis, millions of sperm per day, 4 per meiosis. Oogenesis is a one-off — started before birth, one egg per month, 1 per meiosis, with all the cytoplasm going to the egg.

🏭

Two production lines, one per sex

spermatogenesis · seminiferous tubules · Sertoli · Leydig · oogenesis · polar bodies

Spermatogenesis (testes, seminiferous tubules): germinal epithelium cells divide endlessly by mitosis, grow into primary spermatocytes (2n), then meiosis I → secondary spermatocytes (n), meiosis II → spermatids, which differentiate into sperm (tail, minimal cytoplasm) with Sertoli cell support. Leydig cells (interstitial) secrete testosterone. Output: millions per day, from puberty onwards, ~75 days per batch, 4 sperm per meiosis.

Oogenesis (ovaries): begins in the foetus — about 400,000 primary follicles at birth, and no more are ever made. Each cycle a few follicles develop; usually one matures. The oocyte completes meiosis I at ovulation and meiosis II after fertilization. Result: 1 egg per meiosis (polar bodies are discarded), 1 per month from puberty to menopause, and the egg carries more cytoplasm than any other human cell — the food reserves for the embryo.

FIG 12 — The factory (spermatogenesis: 4 sperm per meiosis, millions/day, Sertoli + Leydig support) vs the one-off (oogenesis: started before birth, 1 egg per meiosis, polar bodies discarded, the biggest cytoplasm in the body).
Remember: both gamete types come from the same four stages (mitosis → growth → meiosis ×2 → differentiation) — but the factory runs continuously from puberty, while the one-off started in the foetus and pays out one egg at a time.
AHL · D3.1.15

AIDS/HIV & SEXUAL HEALTH

Polyspermy is harmful — two sperm in one egg makes a triploid zygote that dies. The acrosome reaction digs the first sperm in; the cortical reaction slams the door behind it.

🔒

One sperm per egg, enforced by two reactions

acrosome reaction · cortical reaction · zona pellucida · polyspermy · triploid

Acrosome reaction: the sperm binds specific glycoproteins in the zona pellucida, and its acrosome (an enzyme sac in the sperm head) releases enzymes that digest the glycoproteins. The sperm's tail force then pushes it through to the egg's plasma membrane.

Cortical reaction: once the first sperm's nucleus enters, the egg activates and its cortical granules (enzyme vesicles near the egg membrane) move to the membrane and undergo exocytosis. The enzymes toughen the zona pellucida and change the glycoproteins that sperm bind to — no more sperm can penetrate. Without it, a second sperm would fuse and create a triploid (3n) zygote, which is non-viable and dies.

FIG 13 — Preventing polyspermy: acrosome enzymes dig sperm 1 through the zona; the cortical reaction then hardens the zona and changes its binding glycoproteins — sperm 2 bounces off. Two sperm in = triploid zygote = death.
Don't mix up: the acrosome reaction is the sperm's own enzyme attack (digging in); the cortical reaction is the egg's response (locking down). First dig, then slam — the door only needs to slam once.
AHL · D3.1.16

PREGNANCY & IMPLANTATION

The zygote becomes a blastocyst — a hollow ball of ~250 cells that rides down the oviduct, breaks out of the zona pellucida and implants in the endometrium to start the residency.

🏠

From single cell to hollow ball, then check-in

cleavage · blastocyst · inner cell mass · trophoblast · blastocoele · implantation

DNA replication happens ~6 hours after fertilization and the first mitosis ~30 hours later. Cleavage is rapid — cell cycles about every 18 hours with no cell growth, so cells get smaller. The solid ball becomes a hollow ball (blastocyst) at ~6–7 days: ~250 cells, ~200 μm, with an inner cell mass (→ embryo body), the trophoblast outer layer (→ placenta) and a fluid-filled cavity, the blastocoele.

The blastocyst is wafted down the oviduct by cilia, the zona pellucida breaks down, and it implants into the endometrium — finger-like projections grow into the lining and exchange materials. By 8 weeks it's a foetus with bone tissue, recognizably human.

FIG 14 — Moving in: cleavage (no growth, shrinking cells) → hollow blastocyst (inner cell mass → embryo, trophoblast → placenta, blastocoele) → cilia waft it down the oviduct → zona breaks down → implantation into the endometrium.
Exam gold: "describe the blastocyst" needs the three parts and their fates: inner cell mass → embryo body, trophoblast → placenta, blastocoele → fluid cavity. And the numbers (250 cells, 200 μm, 6–7 days) are the ones examiners reward.
AHL · D3.1.17

THE PLACENTA

The trophoblast sends hCG — the tenant's "I'm alive, keep the room" signal — to the corpus luteum. hCG keeps progesterone flowing, and its presence in urine is what pregnancy tests detect with monoclonal antibodies.

🧾

How the embryo pays the rent — and how the test reads it

hCG · corpus luteum · progesterone · monoclonal antibodies · pregnancy test

From the blastocyst stage, the trophoblast secretes hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin, a medium-sized protein). hCG keeps the corpus luteum alive, which keeps secreting progesterone, which maintains the endometrium — preventing miscarriage. At ~8–12 weeks the placenta takes over progesterone secretion and the corpus luteum degenerates.

hCG appears in urine, so pregnancy tests use monoclonal antibodies: one mobile antibody carries a blue dye and binds hCG; a second, immobilised antibody traps the hCG-antibody complex, forming a blue band. A control band shows the test worked.

FIG 15 — The rent check: hCG from the trophoblast keeps the corpus luteum alive → progesterone → endometrium maintained. The test stick uses a mobile antibody + dye and an immobilised antibody — two blue bands (test + control) = pregnant.
Remember: hCG's job is to rescue the corpus luteum from its normal Day-28 breakdown. The placenta only starts making progesterone itself at ~8–12 weeks — until then, hCG is the bridge.
AHL · D3.1.18

LABOUR & BIRTH

The placenta is the catering corridor between two blood supplies: foetal villi bathe in maternal blood, separated by a ~5 μm selectively permeable barrier that feeds the foetus and hauls away its waste.

🍽️

Two kitchens, one 5-micrometre hatch

placental villi · intervillous spaces · placental barrier · umbilical cord · exchange

The placenta is made of foetal tissues in intimate contact with maternal tissues. Its functional unit is the placental villus — finger-like foetal tissue bathed in maternal blood in the intervillous spaces. Foetal blood runs through capillaries close to the villus surface, and the placental barrier (the cells separating the two bloods) is only about 5 μm thick and selectively permeable.

Exchange: O₂, glucose, amino acids, water and antibodies move to the foetus by diffusion, facilitated diffusion, osmosis and endocytosis; CO₂ and urea move to the mother. The umbilical cord carries one vein + two arteries (the vein brings oxygenated blood in). The placenta lets the foetus stay in the uterus far longer than marsupials or monotremes can manage (surface-area-to-volume argument), and it takes over hormone secretion after the corpus luteum degenerates.

FIG 16 — The catering corridor: villi (foetal) bathed in maternal blood; the ~5 μm barrier lets O₂, glucose, amino acids, water and antibodies in, and CO₂ + urea out. The cord carries the foetal vein + arteries.
Exam gold: the "why can placental mammals gestate so long?" question wants the surface-area-to-volume argument: the villus system gives a huge exchange surface for a small barrier thickness — so the foetus can keep growing inside the uterus far longer than marsupial or monotreme young, which are born tiny and feed externally.
AHL · D3.1.19

HORMONAL CONTRACEPTION

Childbirth is a positive feedback loop: oxytocin drives contractions, stretch receptors demand more oxytocin, contractions get stronger — a rare positive feedback loop that ends the pregnancy.

👶

The positive feedback loop

oxytocin · myometrium · stretch receptors · positive feedback · progesterone

During pregnancy, progesterone inhibits oxytocin secretion and myometrium (uterine muscle) contractions. At the end of pregnancy, foetal hormones signal the placenta to stop producing progesterone — and oxytocin is secreted. Oxytocin stimulates myometrium contractions; the contractions trigger stretch receptors which stimulate more oxytocin secretion → stronger, more frequent contractions. That's positive feedback — rare in human physiology.

Its advantage: a gradual increase in contraction intensity until the minimum intensity needed to birth the baby. The cervix relaxes and dilates, the amniotic sac bursts, contractions push the baby through cervix and vagina, the umbilical cord is broken, and the baby takes its first breath.

FIG 17 — The grand finale loop: oxytocin → contraction → stretch receptors → more oxytocin → stronger contraction. Progesterone is the mute switch during pregnancy; foetal hormones turn it off so contractions can begin.
Don't mix up: most body systems use negative feedback (bring the variable back to set point). Childbirth is the classic positive exception — the output amplifies itself until the job is done. Its rarity is exactly what makes it examinable.
AHL · D3.1.20

CHILD & MATERNAL HEALTH

Hormone replacement therapy (HRT) is the remastered album: it relieves menopause symptoms and helps prevent osteoporosis — but early observational studies and later randomised trials told different stories about heart risk.

💊

Two trials, two verdicts, one lesson

HRT · menopause · oestrogen + progesterone · observational vs RCT · correlation ≠ causation

HRT supplements oestrogen and progesterone after menopause, when the natural decrease causes hot flushes, night sweats, mood swings, vaginal dryness and reduced sex drive. It also helps prevent osteoporosis.

Early observational studies suggested HRT lowered the risk of CHD (coronary heart disease). Later randomised controlled trials showed a slightly elevated risk of CHD, breast cancer and stroke — and HRT use declined from the early 2000s. Why the discrepancy? Observational studies are confounded: women who chose HRT were often affluent with better healthcare, so their better health came from wealth, not the drugs — correlation ≠ causation. Trials also compared women in early menopause with women years post-menopause.

FIG 18 — The remaster: observational studies looked heart-safe but were confounded (affluent women, better healthcare); RCTs showed slightly elevated CHD, breast cancer and stroke risk. HRT still helps menopause symptoms and osteoporosis — but the evidence lesson is correlation ≠ causation.
Exam gold: the "why did observational studies mislead?" answer has three planks: confounding (affluent, health-conscious women self-selected HRT), timing (early vs late post-menopause), and design (observational correlation can't prove causation — only randomised trials can).

Fifteen one-liners. If you can say them out loud, you know the show.

🎧 Sexual = variation, asexual = clones — meiosis halves, fusion doubles, every generation.
🎫 Gametes are half-price tickets — male: small, motile, mass-produced · female: large, sessile, stocked.
🗓 Day 1 = period, Day 14 = ovulation — FSH grows follicles, LH fires the egg, oestradiol + progesterone run the room.
🚪 First sperm through the door wins — zona pellucida hardens behind it (cortical reaction).

🐝 Cross-pollination = variation + hybrid vigour — self-incompatibility rejects same-S-allele pollen.
🚌 Seeds take the tour bus — wind, animal coats, guts, or explosive pods; dispersal ≠ pollination.
🌙 Puberty = debut night — GnRH pulses (slow = FSH, fast = LH) → sex steroids → the body changes.
🏭 Factory vs one-off — spermatogenesis: millions/day, 4 per meiosis; oogenesis: 1/month, 1 per meiosis, started before birth.
🔒 One sperm per egg — acrosome reaction digs in, cortical reaction slams the door (triploids die).
📦 hCG = rent check — trophoblast signal keeps the corpus luteum + progesterone up; 2 blue bands = pregnant.
🍽 Placenta = catering corridor — 5 μm barrier, O₂ + glucose in, CO₂ + urea out.

Test yourself

✍ SELF-TEST

10 questions. Pick an answer and you'll instantly see why.

1. What is the key difference between sexual and asexual reproduction?

✅ Sexual = two parents, meiosis + fusion, genetically different offspring. Asexual = clones (one parent, mitosis only, identical clones). Variation vs continuity is the fundamental contrast.
❌ Speed and numbers aren't the defining difference. The key contrast is genetic: sexual reproduction produces variation, asexual reproduction produces identical clones.

2. What would happen if a sexual life cycle had NO meiosis?

✅ Meiosis halves 2n → n in gamete formation; fusion doubles n → 2n in the zygote. Without the halving, each generation would add a set of chromosomes — doubling forever.
❌ Meiosis is the halving step of the cycle. Without it, fusion would double the chromosome number every generation — the halving + doubling pair keeps it constant.

3. Which gamete carries the food reserves for the embryo?

✅ The female gamete is large and stocked with food reserves that nourish the early embryo — that's why so few are made. The male gamete is small, motile and mass-produced (anisogamy).
❌ Anisogamy means the gametes differ: the female egg carries the food reserves. The male gamete is a minimal, motile courier with few or no reserves.

4. What triggers ovulation, and when does it happen?

✅ LH has a sudden sharp peak near Day 14: it completes meiosis in the oocyte, digests the follicle wall and releases the egg (ovulation), then promotes the corpus luteum.
❌ Ovulation is the Day-14 LH spike. FSH drives follicle development earlier, and progesterone is the luteal-phase hormone that comes after ovulation.

5. What hardens the zona pellucida after the first sperm enters the egg?

✅ The cortical reaction is the egg's lock-down: cortical granules exocytose enzymes that toughen the zona pellucida and change the glycoproteins sperm bind to — no more sperm get in.
❌ The acrosome reaction is the sperm's digging-in (releasing enzymes to break through), not the hardening. The hardening is the egg's cortical reaction — and it must happen, or a triploid zygote forms and dies.

6. What is the correct order of IVF steps?

✅ First mute the cycle (down-regulation, ~2 weeks), grow many follicles with FSH (7–12 days), trigger final maturation with hCG, collect eggs 34–35 h later, fertilize in the dish at 37 °C, then transfer ~48 h embryos with progesterone support.
❌ Down-regulation must come FIRST — doctors suspend the natural cycle before stimulating follicles, so they control the timing. The order is: mute → FSH → hCG → collect → dish → transfer.

7. Which is a feature of an insect-pollinated flower?

✅ Insect-pollinated flowers advertise: bright petals (landing platform), scent, large spiky pollen that sticks to insects, a large sticky stigma, and deep nectaries that force insects to brush past anthers and stigma.
❌ That's the wind-pollinated design (small dull flowers, huge pollen output, feathery stigma). Insect-pollinated flowers invest in advertising: bright petals, scent, sticky surfaces and deep nectar.

8. How does self-incompatibility reject self-pollen?

✅ Self-incompatibility is genetic (one or more genes, multiple S-alleles): matching S-alleles stop germination or the pollen tube. It's the converse of immunity — immunity rejects non-self, self-incompatibility rejects self.
❌ It's selective, not blanket: pollen with DIFFERENT S-alleles is accepted. And it's a genetic recognition system, not the immune system — the immune system rejects non-self, this rejects self.

9. How many gametes result from one meiosis in each sex?

✅ Spermatogenesis gives 4 sperm per meiosis (millions per day); oogenesis gives 1 egg per meiosis — the polar bodies are discarded, and all the cytoplasm goes to the egg.
❌ The sexes differ: the factory produces 4 sperm per meiosis, the one-off produces a single egg — that's why the egg can be the biggest cell in the human body, stocked for the embryo.

10. Childbirth contractions are driven by…

✅ Childbirth is the textbook positive-feedback loop: oxytocin contracts the myometrium, stretch receptors detect it and trigger more oxytocin, contractions get stronger and more frequent until the baby is born. Rare in human physiology — and that's why it's examinable.
❌ It's positive feedback, not negative: the signal AMPLIFIES itself (oxytocin → contraction → stretch → more oxytocin). Negative feedback would dampen contractions, and the uterus is smooth muscle — not under voluntary control.

  • ✓D3.1.1-2Sexual vs asexual reproduction: asexual = one parent, mitosis throughout, identical offspring, no variation, suited to unchanging environments; sexual = two parents, meiosis once per generation + gamete fusion, genetically different offspring, variation, suited to changing environments. Sexual life cycle needs BOTH meiosis (halving 2n → n) and fusion (doubling n → 2n) — without meiosis the chromosome number doubles every generation.
  • ✓D3.1.3Anisogamy: male gametes small, motile, few/no food reserves, produced in huge numbers; female gametes large, sessile, food reserves for the embryo, few in number. Gametes haploid (n), body cells diploid (2n). All plants and animals are anisogamous.
  • ✓D3.1.4Male reproductive system: testis (sperm + testosterone), scrotum (below core temp), epididymis (stores sperm until ejaculation), sperm duct (transfers sperm), seminal vesicle + prostate (alkali + proteins + fructose → semen), urethra (semen during ejaculation + urine), penis (penetrates vagina, ejaculates near cervix).
  • ✓D3.1.4Female reproductive system: ovary (eggs + oestradiol + progesterone), oviduct (collects egg at ovulation, site of fertilization, moves embryo to uterus), uterus (provides for embryo then foetus), cervix (protects foetus, dilates → birth canal), vagina (stimulates penis/ejaculation + birth canal), vulva (protects internal parts).
  • ✓D3.1.5Menstrual cycle (~28 days): ovarian cycle (follicular phase → ovulation Day 14 → luteal phase, corpus luteum) + uterine cycle (endometrium thickens for implantation; without embryo, breaks down → menstruation Day 1). Hormones: FSH (follicles + oestradiol), LH (sharp peak → completes meiosis, ovulation, corpus luteum), oestradiol (thickens endometrium; positive feedback on FSH receptors, then negative feedback inhibits FSH + stimulates LH), progesterone (maintains endometrium; inhibits FSH/LH).
  • ✓D3.1.6Human fertilization: sperm swim directionally toward egg chemicals; egg has follicle cells + zona pellucida (glycoprotein layer); sperm digest through, bind plasma membrane proteins; first sperm to penetrate fuses, nucleus enters; zona pellucida hardens → blocks more sperm → diploid zygote; sperm tail + most mitochondria stay outside/destroyed.
  • ✓D3.1.7IVF: down-regulation (~2 weeks, drug stops pituitary FSH/LH, cycle suspended); daily FSH injections 7–12 days → many follicles (8–15, ~18 mm); hCG injection at ~18 mm → final maturation; eggs collected 34–35 h later; each egg + 50,000–100,000 sperm in a dish at 37 °C; ~48 h embryos placed in uterus; extra progesterone (vaginal tablet) maintains the lining.
  • ✓D3.1.8-9Flower structure: stamen (anther makes pollen by meiosis + filament), carpel (stigma, style, ovary with ovules); egg = one of 8 haploid nuclei (meiosis + 3 mitotic divisions); pollen grain = 3 haploid nuclei, 2 are male gametes; pollination = anther → stigma; pollen tube grows down style to ovule; zygote → embryo (root, shoot, cotyledons). Insect-pollinated: large bright petals (advertise + landing platform), scent, large spiky protein-rich pollen, large sticky stigma, nectaries deep inside so insects brush past anthers + stigma.
  • ✓D3.1.10-11Cross-pollination (anther of one plant → stigma of another) promotes genetic variation + hybrid vigour; self-pollination = extreme inbreeding → inbreeding depression (premature death, failure to thrive, infertility; rare recessive alleles meet). Prevention: separate male/female flowers on same plant (corn), separate plants (ginkgo, nettle), protandry (anthers first, foxglove), protogyny (stigma first, sacred lotus). Self-incompatibility: same S-alleles → pollen fails to germinate / tube stops — converse of immune rejection.
  • ✓D3.1.12Seed dispersal vs pollination (separate processes: pollen anther → stigma; seeds parent → germination site). Dispersal reduces competition, spreads the species. Fruit types: dry explosive (gorse, exploding cucumber, jewelweed), fleshy eaten by animals (seeds through gut), feathery/winged wind seeds (maple samaras, dandelion), hooked burrs on animal coats. Germination: embryo root, embryo shoot, cotyledons.
  • ✓D3.1.13Puberty (AHL): GnRH (10-amino-acid peptide) from a few hundred hypothalamic neurons → pituitary via a blood vessel; secreted in pulses (peaks at least hourly) — lower frequency → FSH, higher frequency → LH. Males: FSH → testis growth, LH → testosterone (Leydig cells) → penis growth, pubic hair, voice (larynx). Females: FSH → follicles, follicle wall → oestradiol, LH → corpus luteum (oestradiol + progesterone); oestradiol → uterus, breasts, pubic/underarm hair; progesterone → mammary glands. GnRH starts ~10 weeks after fertilization, stops at 4–6 months, resumes at puberty.
  • ✓D3.1.14Gametogenesis (AHL): spermatogenesis (seminiferous tubules) — germinal epithelium mitosis endlessly → primary spermatocytes (2n) → meiosis → spermatids → sperm; Sertoli cells support, Leydig cells make testosterone; millions/day, ~75 days per batch, 4 sperm per meiosis, from puberty. Oogenesis — starts in foetus (~400,000 primary follicles at birth, no more ever made); meiosis I completed at ovulation, meiosis II after fertilization; 1 egg per meiosis (polar bodies discarded), 1/month, Day 14, puberty → menopause; egg has more cytoplasm than any other human cell (food reserves).
  • ✓D3.1.15Polyspermy prevention (AHL): acrosome reaction — sperm binds specific zona pellucida glycoproteins, acrosome enzymes digest them, tail force pushes through to the egg membrane. Cortical reaction — first nucleus enters → egg activates → cortical granules exocytose → enzymes toughen zona + change sperm-binding glycoproteins → no more sperm penetrate. Without it: triploid (3n) zygote, non-viable, dies.
  • ✓D3.1.16Blastocyst + implantation (AHL): DNA replication ~6 h after fertilization, first mitosis ~30 h; rapid cycles ~every 18 h with no cell growth (cells shrink) → solid ball → hollow blastocyst ~6–7 days (~250 cells, ~200 μm): inner cell mass → embryo body, trophoblast → placenta, blastocoele (fluid). Wafted down oviduct by cilia; zona pellucida breaks down; implants into endometrium (finger-like projections exchange materials); by 8 weeks → foetus (bone tissue, recognizably human).
  • ✓D3.1.17hCG + pregnancy testing (AHL): trophoblast secretes hCG (medium-sized protein) from blastocyst stage; hCG keeps corpus luteum alive → progesterone → endometrium maintained (prevents miscarriage); ~8–12 weeks placenta takes over progesterone, corpus luteum degenerates. Urine tests use monoclonal antibodies: mobile anti-hCG antibody + blue dye, then immobilised antibody traps the complex → blue band; control band proves the test worked.
  • ✓D3.1.18-20Placenta (AHL): foetal villi bathed in maternal blood (intervillous spaces); barrier ~5 μm, selectively permeable; O₂, glucose, amino acids, water, antibodies → foetus (diffusion, facilitated diffusion, osmosis, endocytosis); CO₂, urea → mother; umbilical vein + artery; allows longer gestation than marsupials/monotremes (surface-area-to-volume); secretes hormones after corpus luteum degenerates. Childbirth: progesterone inhibits oxytocin + myometrium; foetal hormones → placenta stops progesterone → oxytocin → contractions → stretch receptors → more oxytocin (POSITIVE feedback, rare; gradual intensity). HRT: oestrogen + progesterone after menopause (relieves hot flushes, night sweats, mood swings, vaginal dryness, low sex drive; prevents osteoporosis); observational studies suggested lower CHD risk, RCTs showed slightly elevated CHD/breast cancer/stroke risk → use declined; discrepancy = confounding (affluent women, healthcare), early vs late menopause, correlation ≠ causation.
