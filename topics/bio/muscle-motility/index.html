B3.3.1

MUSCLE CONTRACTION & THE SARCOMERE

Movement is a universal feature of life. The question is what kind — and how fast.

🕺

Two kinds of movement

There are two very different ways organisms move.

Movement within the body happens in ALL living things — peristalsis squeezing food down your gut, ventilation inflating your lungs, cytoplasm streaming inside a single cell. Even a coral that never leaves its seat is moving stuff around inside.

Locomotion = moving the whole organism from place to place. This is optional. Some organisms have it, some don't.

  • Motile — moves around under its own power. Most animals. Speed demons example: bar-tailed godwits fly 10,400 km from eastern Siberia to New Zealand in 7–8 days non-stop, doubling their body weight in fat reserves first.
  • Sessile — stays fixed in one place. Coral polyps, adult barnacles, most plants and fungi. Note the trick: a barnacle's larvae swim (motile), but the adult glues itself down forever.
Exam angle: sessile ≠ still. Corals still have internal movement — they pump water, extend tentacles, shift cytoplasm. The statement says movement is universal, locomotion is not.

Why bother moving at all? Motile organisms burn more energy → higher metabolic rate → need more food. The payoff: escaping danger, finding food and mates. Sessile organisms trade that for a life of staying put — but they can't run from a predator.

THE SLIDING-FILAMENT MECHANISM

Strip a muscle down and you find repeating dance floors called sarcomeres. Contraction isn't a muscle getting shorter like a spring — it's two sets of filaments sliding past each other.

💃

The anatomy of the floor

Muscle fibre → myofibrils (parallel rods) → sarcomeres (linked end-to-end at Z-discs)

Each sarcomere is the unit of contraction. Under the microscope it shows light bands at the ends (named I bands) and a dark band in the middle (the A band).

  • Actin — thin filaments, anchored to the Z-discs at each end.
  • Myosin — thick filaments, sitting in the centre, interlocking with actin like clasped fingers.
  • Each myosin filament is surrounded by six actins and forms cross-bridges to them at regular intervals.

The whole contraction story in one line: myosin heads grab actin, swivel, pull — and the Z-discs get dragged toward each other. The filaments themselves never shrink; the overlap just increases.

Classic exam trap: during contraction the light bands shorten (actin-only zones get smaller as actin slides into the myosin zone) but the dark band stays the same length (myosin filaments don't change). Memorise that pair and you've beaten half the paper.
READ THE SARCOMERE: same myosin zone (dark band) in both panels — only the Z-discs move in and the light bands shrink. The gold zigzags are titin: giant elastic springs tethering each myosin filament to the Z-discs (B3.3.3).
⚡

The power stroke — the ratchet that pulls

B3.3.2 · the molecular choreography
  1. Cross-bridge forms: a myosin head binds a binding site on an actin filament nearby.
  2. Power stroke: the head swivels, shoving the actin filament ~8–10 nm toward the centre of the sarcomere. This is the actual "pull".
  3. ATP binds the head → the head releases its grip on actin (without ATP, the head stays locked — that's rigor mortis).
  4. ATP hydrolyses → head re-cocks and binds the next site further along the actin. Repeat.

That's a ratchet: one head pulling 8 nm sounds pathetic, but thousands of heads on every myosin filament, firing asynchronously (never all at once), produce a smooth, powerful slide. The dark band staying constant is direct evidence — the thick filaments aren't shrinking.

How it works: the myosin heads are like ratchets — each one binds actin, pulls itself along, lets go, and re-attaches to the next actin site. The floor (sarcomere) shortens because everyone keeps pulling inwards.
THE RATCHET: 1 bind → 2 swivel & pull → 3 ATP unlocks → 4 re-cock & grab the next site. The dark band (myosin zone) never shrinks — only the overlap grows.
🪢

Titin — the bungee cord (B3.3.3)

The largest polypeptide ever found: 34,350 amino acids in humans (35,213 in mice!)

Titin is an elastic molecular spring tethering each myosin filament to the Z-discs. It's the biggest single protein chain in your body — and it has three jobs:

  • Centring: holds each myosin filament perfectly in the middle of its ring of six actins.
  • Bounce-back: stops the sarcomere being overstretched, and recoils like a released spring, adding force to contraction.
  • Storage: when a muscle is stretched, titin stretches and stores potential energy — free energy for the relaxation phase.
Why muscles need pairs: a muscle can only exert force when contracting — it can never push itself back to full length. Lengthening needs energy from outside, and that energy comes from the antagonist: as the antagonist contracts, it stretches the first muscle (and its titin), which then recoils. Biceps/triceps, external/internal intercostals — every joint runs on this push–pull buddy system.
B3.3.4

MOTOR UNITS & NEUROMUSCULAR JUNCTIONS

Sarcomeres don't just contract on their own — they need a signal from a motor neuron. One neuron, many muscle fibres, all contracting together.

🎧

One motor neuron controls all the muscle fibres of a motor unit

Motor unit = one motor neuron + ALL the muscle fibres its axon branches to

Each motor neuron sends an axon that branches to many skeletal muscle fibres. The junction where a branch meets a fibre is a neuromuscular junction — a synapse that uses the neurotransmitter acetylcholine (ACh).

  • A single unit usually controls hundreds of fibres — and the fibres of one unit are mingled with fibres of other units (not clumped together), so activation is spread out, not lumpy.
  • When the neuron fires, every fibre in its unit contracts together — all-or-nothing per fibre.
  • Graded strength: you don't fire one unit "harder" — you recruit more units. Lifting a pencil fires a handful; a deadlift fires nearly everything.
Exam favourite: "explain how muscle contraction force is graded" → recruitment of additional motor units, not bigger individual fibre twitches. Each fibre is all-or-nothing.
ONE UNIT, ONE BEAT: every green dot is a neuromuscular junction where ACh crosses the gap. One impulse = the whole unit fires as one. Fibres of different motor units are intermingled so contraction is even across the muscle.

BONES, JOINTS & THE SKELETON

Muscles pull, but they need something to pull against — and a way to turn a short pull into a big movement. That's the skeleton and the joints.

🏗️

Skeletons: anchorage + levers (B3.3.5)

Exoskeleton (arthropods — chitin plates) vs endoskeleton (vertebrates — bone)

The skeleton does two mechanical jobs: it's anchorage for muscles, and it's a set of levers that multiply or redirect muscle force.

  • Origin = where the muscle attaches to the fixed bone (the frame).
  • Insertion = where it attaches to the bone that moves.
  • Example: the masseter — origin on the cheekbone (zygomatic arch), insertion on the jawbone (mandible). Contract it and the jaw slams shut.

In a lever, the fulcrum = the joint, the effort = the muscle (applied via tendons), and the load is whatever's being moved. Where the effort sits relative to the fulcrum changes the trade-off:

Effort FAR from fulcrum

FORCE LEVER

Small pull becomes a big force, but the load travels a short distance. Great for crushing (a nutcracker jaw).

Effort NEAR fulcrum

SPEED LEVER

The load moves faster and further, but you lose force. Great for quick strikes (a whip arm).

Evolution picks the mix: a mole's short, wide forelimb bones = force lever for digging; a cheetah's long, narrow limbs = speed lever for sprinting.

LEVER LINGO: fulcrum = joint · effort = muscle via tendon · load = what moves. The masseter's origin is the cheekbone (fixed), insertion the mandible (moving).
🦴

Synovial joints — the freely movable joints (B3.3.6)

Where bones meet and actually move — the hip is the best example

Some joints are fixed (skull sutures — no movement, just fused bone). Movable ones are mostly synovial joints, and they come with a full support crew:

PartRole
BonesThe frame itself — anchorage + guide the movement path.
CartilageSmooth coating over bone ends — cuts friction, absorbs shock (the dance-floor padding).
Synovial fluidSlippery liquid filling the cavity — lubricates like a well-oiled hinge.
LigamentsTough collagen cords strapping bone to bone — stop dislocation; the joint capsule seals the whole thing.
MusclesThe force — span the joint to move it.
TendonsCollagen cables attaching muscle to bone — high tensile strength, transmit the pull.

The hip is a ball-and-socket: the rounded head of the femur (ball) sits in the cup-shaped socket of the pelvis. That's the whole name game — femur + pelvis, ball + socket.

Range of motion (B3.3.7): the joint's shape dictates how far it can move. Hinge joints (elbow, knee) allow flexion/extension in one plane. Ball-and-socket (hip) allows movement in three planes: protraction/retraction, abduction/adduction, and rotation. Scientists measure the angle with a goniometer or by computer analysis of images.
EVERY PART, ONE PICTURE: cartilage + synovial fluid = the no-friction zone; ligaments + capsule = the anti-dislocation straps; muscle + tendon = the engine and its drive shaft.
B3.3.8

INTERCOSTAL MUSCLES & VENTILATION

The same pair of muscles, two layers, opposite jobs — the perfect demonstration of antagonistic pairs and titin's stored energy.

🌬️

External vs internal — opposite surfaces

Antagonistic pair that runs your breathing

Between your ribs sit external intercostal muscles and internal intercostal muscles, their fibres running at different angles. Because of that orientation, contracting each layer moves the ribcage in the opposite direction:

EXTERNAL contract → INHALATION

🎈

Ribs swing up and out → chest volume ↑ → air rushes in. This stretches the internal layer — storing potential energy in its titin.

INTERNAL contract → EXHALATION

🪂

Ribs swing down and in → chest volume ↓ → air pushed out. This stretches the external layer in turn.

Each contraction stretches the other layer's titin, banking elastic potential energy — so the rebound/recoil helps drive the next phase. It's the textbook proof of the titin story from Act 2: muscles can only pull, but the pair working together gives you push and pull.

Memory hook: External = Expand (in). Internal = Inward (out). The letters literally point the way.
E vs I: the cyan fibres pull the ribs up and out; the pink fibres (same pair of muscles!) pull them down and in. Same muscles, opposite fibre angles, opposite results.
B3.3.9

LOCOMOTION & WHY WE MOVE

Locomotion costs energy — lots of it. So there must be a payoff. There are four big ones.

🍽️ Foraging

EATING

Move to find food. Bees fly flower to flower collecting nectar; predators sprint to catch prey; herbivores trek between feeding patches.

🛟 Escaping danger

SURVIVAL

Run from predators or hostile rivals. Strong selective pressure for speed and stamina — the fastest and most agile live to reproduce.

💘 Finding a mate

REPRODUCTION

Travel to reach the opposite sex — avoids inbreeding in dispersed populations. Young male lions leave the pride and roam; male moon moths fly kilometres upwind to a female's pheromones.

🗺️ Migration

SEASONS

Move between habitats as conditions change. Snow geese fly from the Arctic to the Atlantic coast of the US each autumn; salmon make a once-in-a-lifetime migration to their breeding grounds.

Exam shape: "explain why an organism moves" → name the reason (foraging / escape / mate / migration) and the energy cost. Every movement is a trade: calories burned vs survival or reproduction gained.
B3.3.10

SWIMMING IN DOLPHINS & AQUATIC LIFE

Water is ~1000× denser than air. Marine mammals are a masterclass in drag reduction — every shape choice is an adaptation.

🐬

Body-shape adaptations for life in water

Streamlining: the teardrop rule
  • Streamlined body — widest near the front, tapering toward the tail; teardrop cross-section. Smooth skin with no hair, no hind limbs, no ear flaps — anything sticking out creates drag.
  • Flippers (front limbs) — for steering.
  • Flukes (tail lobes) — the engine: beat up and down for thrust (unlike fish, which wiggle side to side).
  • Dorsal fin — stability: stops the body rolling in the water.
  • Blubber — thick fat layer: buoyancy (float without paddling) plus insulation in cold water.
  • Blowhole — nostrils moved to the top of the head: the larynx connects to the blowhole, not the mouth — so they can breathe at the surface without ever swallowing water.
Exam angle: they're mammals that went back to the sea. The question loves "how is the dolphin adapted to swimming?" — the answer is the whole list above, especially up-down fluke beats (they're mammals — vertebral column flexes vertically) and the mouth–lung separation.
EVERY BUMP HAS A JOB: flukes power (up-down!), flippers steer, dorsal fin stabilises, blowhole breathes without the mouth — and the whole silhouette is a teardrop because water is brutally dense.

Test yourself

✍ SELF-TEST

Ten questions, instant feedback. No peeking at the cheat sheet.

1. During muscle contraction, which band gets shorter?

✅ Light bands shrink — actin slides INTO the myosin zone, so the actin-only zones get smaller. The dark band (myosin zone) never changes length.
❌ The dark band is the myosin zone and it stays constant. Only the light bands (actin-only) shorten as actin slides over myosin.

2. What actually produces the pull in a sarcomere?

✅ The myosin heads bind actin, swivel ~8–10 nm, release, and re-cock. Titin only helps with centring and recoil.
❌ The pull is the myosin power stroke. Filaments never shrink, and titin is the elastic spring that holds myosin centred — it stores, not pulls.

3. Titin is famous for being…

✅ Titin holds the size record — a single chain of 34,350 amino acids (mice: 35,213). It's an elastic spring, not a motor.
❌ Titin's claim to fame is SIZE — the biggest polypeptide known. It's an elastic spring that centres myosin and stores stretch energy.

4. Why can't a muscle lengthen itself after contracting?

✅ Muscles pull, they never push. The antagonist's contraction stretches the first muscle (banking energy in titin) so it returns to length.
❌ Muscles can only exert force when contracting. Returning to full length takes the antagonist contracting — that's why they always come in pairs.

5. How does the body make a contraction stronger?

✅ Individual fibres are all-or-nothing — the way to scale up is to fire more motor units (each unit = one neuron + its fibres).
❌ A fibre either contracts fully or not at all. Strength comes from recruitment — activating more motor units.

6. In a bone acting as a lever, what is the fulcrum?

✅ The joint is the pivot point. Muscle = effort, joint = fulcrum, whatever moves = load.
❌ The fulcrum is the joint. Muscle effort and the tendon are how the force gets in — the load is what gets moved.

7. The hip joint is a ball-and-socket. Name the two bones.

✅ The femur's rounded head is the ball; the pelvis provides the socket. Ball-and-socket = movement in three planes.
❌ Hip = femur + pelvis. (Humerus + scapula is the shoulder — also ball-and-socket, but the syllabus names the hip.)

8. Compared to a hinge joint, a ball-and-socket joint allows…

✅ Hinge = one plane (flexion/extension). Ball-and-socket = protraction/retraction, abduction/adduction AND rotation.
❌ Ball-and-socket moves in three planes — that's its whole advantage over the hinge (elbow/knee).

9. Which intercostal muscles contract to inhale?

✅ External = Expand. They lift the ribcage up and out, increasing volume so air rushes in. Internal fibres do the reverse for exhalation.
❌ External contracts for inhalation (up and out). Internal contracts for exhalation (down and in) — opposite fibre angles, opposite jobs.

10. A dolphin's flukes move…

✅ Up and down! Whales and dolphins are mammals — their vertebral column bends vertically, so the flukes beat up-down, unlike fish tails.
❌ Mammals' spines flex vertically → up-down fluke beats. Side-to-side wiggling is the fish way.
