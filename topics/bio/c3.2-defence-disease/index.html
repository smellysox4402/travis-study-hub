C3.2.1

PATHOGENS & INFECTIOUS DISEASE

A broad range of disease-causing organisms (pathogens) can infect humans.

A pathogen is a disease-causing organism. A broad range can infect humans — bacteria, viruses, fungi, protists. They cause infectious (communicable) diseases.

C3.2.2

SKIN & MUCOUS MEMBRANES — PRIMARY DEFENCE

The skin acts as a physical and chemical barrier to pathogens, as do mucous membranes.

  • Physical barrier — the skin and mucous membranes block entry. (You are not required to draw/label the skin.)
  • Chemical barrier — sweat and secretions contain antimicrobial chemicals; the stomach's acid and lysozyme in tears/mucus destroy pathogens.
C3.2.3

BLOOD CLOTTING SEALS CUTS

Platelets release clotting factors that trigger a cascade, forming a clot rapidly to seal a cut.

When a cut occurs, platelets release clotting factors which trigger a cascade pathway. This converts soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin threads, which form a clot to seal the wound and stop pathogen entry + blood loss.

C3.2.4

INNATE vs ADAPTIVE IMMUNITY

The innate system responds to broad pathogen categories and doesn't change with repeated exposure; the adaptive system is specific and improves.

FeatureInnateAdaptive
Specificitybroad (categories)specific (to a pathogen)
Change with exposurenoyes (memory)
Speedfastslower first time, fast later
Examples: innate = skin, phagocytes; adaptive = lymphocytes (B & T cells), antibodies.
C3.2.5

PHAGOCYTES

Phagocytes move to infection sites, recognise pathogens, engulf them by endocytosis and digest them using lysosomes.

Phagocytes move by amoeboid movement from the blood to infection sites. They recognise the pathogen, engulf it by endocytosis, and digest it using lysosomal enzymes.

C3.2.6

LYMPHOCYTES

Lymphocytes are the cells of the adaptive immune system that cooperate to produce antibodies.

Lymphocytes are the key adaptive cells. They circulate in the blood and are also contained in lymph nodes and the spleen. They cooperate (helper T-cells activate B-cells) to produce antibodies.

C3.2.7

ANTIGENS

Antigens are recognition molecules (usually glycoproteins or other proteins) that trigger antibody production.

An antigen is a molecule (often a glycoprotein or other protein) that the immune system recognises. It is usually on the surface of a pathogen (or an infected cell). When detected, it triggers antibody production.

C3.2.8

ACTIVATION OF B-CELLS BY HELPER T-CELLS

There are antigen-specific B-cells and helper T-cells; helper T-cells activate B-cells.

There are antigen-specific B-cells and helper T-cells. A helper T-cell that recognises the antigen activates the matching B-cell. B-cells then produce antibodies and become memory + plasma cells.

C3.2.9

B-CELLS MULTIPLY INTO PLASMA CELLS

Activated B-lymphocytes multiply to form clones of antibody-secreting plasma cells.

Only small numbers of B-cells respond to a specific antigen. The activated B-cell multiplies (clonal expansion) to form many plasma cells, which secrete large amounts of antibodies specific to that antigen.

C3.2.10

IMMUNITY FROM MEMORY CELLS

Immunity is the ability to eliminate an infection, due to retained memory cells.

Immunity is the ability to eliminate an infectious disease from the body. It results from the retention of memory cells after an infection — on a second exposure, the response is faster and stronger.

C3.2.11

HIV TRANSMISSION

HIV is transmitted in body fluids.

HIV is transmitted in body fluids such as blood, semen, vaginal fluids and breast milk. Modes: unprotected sex, sharing needles, mother-to-child (during birth/breastfeeding), and blood transfusion.

C3.2.12

HIV INFECTS LYMPHOCYTES → AIDS

HIV infects and kills certain lymphocytes; the resulting lymphocyte reduction leads to AIDS.

HIV infects and kills helper T-lymphocytes (CD4+ cells). As these are destroyed, the immune system weakens. When the reduction in lymphocytes becomes severe, the person has AIDS — vulnerable to opportunistic infections.

C3.2.13

ANTIBIOTICS

Antibiotics block processes that occur in bacteria but not in eukaryotic cells — so they fail against viruses.

Antibiotics block bacterial processes (e.g. cell-wall synthesis, protein synthesis) that are different from eukaryotic ones — so they kill bacteria but not human cells. Because viruses don't have these bacterial processes, antibiotics are ineffective against viral infections.

C3.2.14

EVOLUTION OF ANTIBIOTIC RESISTANCE

Bacteria can evolve resistance to several antibiotics; careful antibiotic use slows this.

Random mutations can make some bacteria resistant to an antibiotic. In the presence of the antibiotic, these survive and multiply, so the population becomes resistant — e.g. MRSA. Careful/appropriate use of antibiotics slows the emergence of multi-resistant strains.

C3.2.15

ZOONOSES

Zoonoses are infectious diseases that transfer from other species to humans.

A zoonosis is an infectious disease that can transfer from other species to humans (e.g. COVID-19, rabies, influenza). Zoonoses are a large fraction of human infectious diseases and have varied modes of transmission.

C3.2.16

VACCINES & IMMUNIZATION

Vaccines contain antigens (or nucleic acids coding for antigens) that stimulate the immune system to produce memory cells.

A vaccine contains antigens (or nucleic acids — DNA or RNA — with sequences coding for antigens). It stimulates the immune system to produce memory cells and antibodies — giving immunity without the disease.

C3.2.17

HERD IMMUNITY

If a sufficient percentage of a population is immune, the pathogen cannot spread effectively — protecting the whole population.

Herd immunity occurs when a sufficient percentage of a population is immune, so transmission is interrupted and epidemics are prevented — including for unvaccinated individuals (who are protected indirectly). Members of a population are interdependent in building herd immunity.

FIG 1 — Herd immunity: when enough people are immune, the pathogen can't spread, protecting even the non-immune.
C3.2.18

EVALUATING COVID-19 DATA

You should calculate percentage difference and percentage change from COVID-19 (and other) data.

  • Percentage change = (new − original) ÷ original × 100.
  • Percentage difference = (difference between two values) ÷ reference × 100.
  • Use these to compare case numbers, deaths, vaccination rates, etc. and evaluate the data.
