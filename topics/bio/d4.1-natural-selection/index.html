D4.1.1

NATURAL SELECTION

Natural selection is the mechanism driving evolutionary change.

Natural selection is the mechanism of evolutionary change: individuals with advantageous heritable traits survive and reproduce more, so those traits increase in frequency in the population over generations.

D4.1.2

VARIATION — MUTATION & SEXUAL REPRODUCTION

Mutation and sexual reproduction generate the variation on which natural selection acts.

Mutation is the original source of variation; sexual reproduction (recombination, crossing over, independent assortment) shuffles it into new combinations. Together they provide the differing traits that selection acts on.

D4.1.3

OVERPRODUCTION & COMPETITION

Overproduction of offspring and competition for resources promote natural selection.

Organisms produce more offspring than can survive, so there is competition for limited resources. Only some survive and reproduce — this differential survival drives natural selection.

D4.1.4

ABIOTIC SELECTION PRESSURES

Abiotic factors (density-independent, e.g. temperature) act as selection pressures.

Abiotic factors such as temperature extremes, low water or salinity are selection pressures. They are often density-independent — affecting a population regardless of its size.

D4.1.5

DIFFERENCES IN ADAPTATION & SURVIVAL

Differences between individuals in adaptation, survival and reproduction are the basis of natural selection.

Because individuals differ in their adaptations, they differ in survival and reproduction. The better-adapted individuals pass on more genes — this differential is the basis of natural selection.

D4.1.6

TRAITS MUST BE HERITABLE

Only heritable characteristics can change a population by evolution.

For evolutionary change to occur, a trait must be heritable (determined by genes). Acquired characteristics (not inherited) cannot drive evolutionary change.

D4.1.7

SEXUAL SELECTION

Sexual selection is a selection pressure in animal species, based on differences in physical/behavioural traits.

Sexual selection acts on differences in physical and behavioural traits that improve mating success (e.g. a peacock's tail, courtship displays). It can lead to traits that help obtain mates even if they reduce survival.

D4.1.8

MODELLING SELECTION

Model sexual and natural selection by experimentally controlling selection pressures.

You can model selection by controlling a selection pressure experimentally — e.g. varying a predator or mate-choice factor, and measuring how things like colour or size change in the population (e.g. counting survivors of different variants).

AHL · D4.1.9

THE GENE POOL

A gene pool is all the genes and their different alleles present in a population.

The gene pool is the sum of all genes and alleles in a population. Its composition is described by allele frequencies.

AHL · D4.1.10

ALLELE FREQUENCIES OF ISOLATED POPULATIONS

Use databases to compare allele frequencies of geographically isolated populations, and identify conditions that change them.

Geographically isolated populations of the same species can have different allele frequencies (from genetic drift, selection, mutation, migration). You should use databases to compare these and identify the conditions that change allele frequencies.

AHL · D4.1.11

CHANGES IN ALLELE FREQUENCY

Natural selection changes allele frequency in the gene pool according to survival and reproduction.

Because individuals with an advantageous allele survive and reproduce more, that allele's frequency increases in the gene pool over generations — this is evolution at the population-genetics level.

AHL · D4.1.12

AHL · D4.1.13

THE HARDY–WEINBERG EQUATION

Use p and q for two alleles: p + q = 1, and (in equilibrium) p² + 2pq + q² = 1.

For two alleles (A and a) with frequencies p and q: p + q = 1. Under Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, genotype frequencies are p² (AA) + 2pq (Aa) + q² (aa) = 1. Use these to calculate allele/genotype frequencies.

AHL · D4.1.14

HARDY–WEINBERG CONDITIONS

A population is in genetic equilibrium only if certain conditions are maintained.

  • No mutation, no natural selection, no migration (gene flow), random mating, and a large population (no genetic drift).
  • If any of these is violated, allele frequencies change (evolution occurs).
AHL · D4.1.15

ARTIFICIAL SELECTION

Artificial selection deliberately chooses traits — in crop plants and domesticated animals.

Artificial selection is when humans deliberately choose which organisms reproduce to select for desired traits — carried out in crop plants and domesticated animals (e.g. selective breeding of dogs, wheat).
