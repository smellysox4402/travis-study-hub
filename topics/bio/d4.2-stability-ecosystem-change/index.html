D4.2.1

STABILITY OF ECOSYSTEMS

Stability is a property of natural ecosystems — illustrated with evidence from forests, deserts, etc.

Ecosystems tend towards stability (a fairly constant community over time). Evidence from forests, deserts and other ecosystems shows that communities can remain relatively stable unless disturbed.

D4.2.2

REQUIREMENTS FOR STABILITY

Stability needs a supply of energy, recycling of nutrients, genetic diversity and stable climate.

  • Supply of energy (from the sun).
  • Recycling of nutrients (matter cycles).
  • Genetic diversity (so species can adapt).
  • A stable climate / predictable conditions.
D4.2.3

THE AMAZON — A TIPPING POINT

Deforestation of the Amazon rainforest is a possible tipping point in ecosystem stability.

Widespread deforestation of the Amazon may push it to a tipping point — beyond which large areas flip from rainforest to savannah, losing biodiversity and carbon stores. Large-scale deforestation changes the local water cycle and rainfall.

D4.2.4

USING MODELS

Use a model (e.g. mesocosms) to investigate the effect of variables on ecosystem stability.

Mesocosms (enclosed tanks or experimental systems) can be used to manipulate variables (e.g. adding/removing a species, changing nutrients) and observe the effect on stability.

D4.2.5

KEYSTONE SPECIES

Keystone species have a disproportionate impact on community structure and stability.

A keystone species has a disproportionately large effect on its community relative to its abundance (e.g. a top predator, a pollinator, the beaver). If it's removed, the ecosystem can change drastically.

D4.2.6

SUSTAINABILITY OF RESOURCE HARVESTING

Sustainability depends on the rate of harvesting not exceeding the rate of replenishment.

Sustainable harvesting means the harvest rate must not exceed the rate of replenishment (growth). If harvesting exceeds renewal, the resource is depleted.

D4.2.7

SUSTAINABILITY OF AGRICULTURE

Ecosystems — consider soil erosion, leaching of nutrients and other factors.

  • Soil erosion — loses fertile topsoil.
  • Leaching of nutrients — fertilisers washed away, polluting water.
  • Over-use of water, monoculture (low diversity), and loss of soil fertility reduce sustainability.
D4.2.8

EUTROPHICATION

Leaching of nutrients causes eutrophication of aquatic and marine ecosystems.

Eutrophication: leached nutrients (e.g. nitrates from fertiliser) enter water → algal bloom → algae die and are decomposed → oxygen is used up → fish and invertebrates die. This can lead to an anoxic "dead zone".

FIG 1 — Eutrophication: excess nutrients → algal bloom → die-off → decomposers consume oxygen → fish die.
D4.2.9

BIOMAGNIFICATION

Pollutants accumulate and are concentrated up food chains (biomagnification).

Biomagnification: persistent toxins (e.g. DDT, mercury) are not broken down, so they accumulate in the body and become more concentrated at each trophic level — reaching harmful levels in top predators.

D4.2.10

MICROPLASTIC & MACROPLASTIC POLLUTION

Plastics are persistent in the oceans and cause problems at both macro and micro scales.

  • Macroplastics — large items; entangle and are ingested by marine animals.
  • Microplastics — tiny fragments; entered the food web, ingested by plankton/fish, and contain/adsorb toxins.
  • Plastics are persistent (barely degrade) and accumulate in the ocean.
D4.2.11

REWILDING

Restore natural processes in ecosystems by rewilding — e.g. reintroducing apex predators.

Rewilding restores natural ecosystem processes. Methods include reintroducing apex predators (which control herbivores and restore trophic cascades) and allowing natural regeneration.

AHL · D4.2.12

ECOLOGICAL SUCCESSION

Succession is the change in a community over time, triggered by abiotic and biotic changes.

Ecological succession is the gradual change in a community as species replace one another over time. It can be triggered by both abiotic and biotic changes.

AHL · D4.2.13

PRIMARY SUCCESSION

Primary succession starts on bare substrate with no soil.

On bare rock/soil-free ground, pioneer species (e.g. lichens, mosses) colonise first, build soil, then grasses → shrubs → trees follow. Biomass and species diversity increase over time (e.g. after a volcanic eruption).

AHL · D4.2.14

CYCLICAL SUCCESSION

In some ecosystems, succession is cyclical — a cycle of community change.

In some ecosystems the community goes through a cycle rather than reaching a final state — e.g. the cycle of regeneration in some species/communities that repeatedly return to an earlier stage due to periodic disturbance or a fixed sequence.

AHL · D4.2.15

CLIMAX COMMUNITIES & ARRESTED SUCCESSION

Under stable conditions, succession tends toward a climax community; it may be arrested.

  • Climax community — the stable final stage determined by the environmental conditions.
  • Arrested succession — where a stable community is maintained short of the climax by a factor (e.g. grazing, fire, waterlogging) that prevents change.
