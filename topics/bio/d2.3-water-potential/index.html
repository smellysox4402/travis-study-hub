D2.3.1

SOLVATION — WATER AS A SOLVENT

Water dissolves solutes by hydrogen bonding and ion-dipole attractions.

Solvation = dissolving a solute in water. It involves hydrogen bond formation between solute and water, and attractions between positive and negative ions and the polar water molecules (ions are surrounded/hydrated).

D2.3.2

WATER MOVEMENT & CONCENTRATION

Water moves from a less concentrated to a more concentrated solution — express this in solute concentration.

Water moves from a less concentrated (more dilute) solution to a more concentrated one. Express the direction in terms of solute concentration (water moves toward the higher solute concentration), not water concentration.

D2.3.3

OSMOSIS INTO & OUT OF CELLS

Predict the net movement of water if a cell's environment is hypotonic or hypertonic.

  • Hypotonic environment (lower solute outside) — water moves into the cell.
  • Hypertonic environment (higher solute outside) — water moves out of the cell.
  • Isotonic — no net movement.
D2.3.4

WATER MOVEMENT IN PLANT TISSUE

Plant tissue bathed in hypotonic vs hypertonic solutions changes mass and length — measure and analyse.

  • Hypotonic — water enters, tissue gains mass and length (becomes turgid).
  • Hypertonic — water leaves, tissue loses mass and length (becomes flaccid/plasmolysed).
  • This is used to estimate solute potential or analyse water relations.
D2.3.5

CELLS WITHOUT A CELL WALL

Without a wall, cells swell and burst in hypotonic, and shrivel (crenate) in hypertonic.

  • Hypotonic — water enters → the cell swells and may burst (lyse).
  • Hypertonic — water leaves → the cell shrivels (crenation in red blood cells).
  • Osmotic regulation is needed to prevent this (e.g. unicellular organisms controlling internal solute).
D2.3.6

CELLS WITH A CELL WALL

With a wall, cells develop turgor pressure in hypotonic and plasmolysis in hypertonic.

  • Hypotonic — water enters, the wall prevents bursting → turgor pressure develops (turgid).
  • Hypertonic — water leaves → the protoplast shrinks away from the wall → plasmolysis.
FIG 1 — Plant cells become turgid in hypotonic conditions and plasmolysed in hypertonic; animal cells lyse or crenate because they have no wall.
D2.3.7

MEDICAL APPLICATIONS OF ISOTONIC SOLUTIONS

Isotonic solutions are used as IV fluids and to bathe organs for transplantation.

  • Intravenous (IV) fluids — given as part of medical treatment (must be isotonic with blood to avoid damaging red blood cells).
  • Bathing organs — organs ready for transplantation are kept in isotonic solution to prevent cell damage.
AHL · D2.3.8

WATER POTENTIAL

Water potential is the potential energy of water per unit volume; only its difference can be measured.

Water potential (ψ) is the potential energy of water per unit volume. It is impossible to measure its absolute value — only differences in water potential matter (water moves from high to low potential).

AHL · D2.3.9

WATER MOVES FROM HIGH TO LOW POTENTIAL

Water moves from higher to lower water potential because of potential energy.

Water moves from higher to lower water potential, because water naturally flows from regions where its potential energy is higher to regions where it is lower (down the potential-energy gradient).

AHL · D2.3.10

SOLUTE & PRESSURE POTENTIAL

ψw = ψs + ψp — water potential is the sum of solute potential and pressure potential.

For cells with a wall: ψw = ψs + ψp, where ψs (solute potential) is always negative (lowered by solutes) and ψp (pressure potential) is usually positive (from turgor pressure).

AHL · D2.3.11

WATER POTENTIAL IN PLANT TISSUE

Explain water movements in plant tissue in terms of solute and pressure potentials.

  • In a hypotonic solution, water enters → ψp increases (turgor) → ψw rises until equilibrium.
  • In a hypertonic solution, water leaves → ψp falls, the protoplast shrinks (plasmolysis).
  • Water moves until the water potentials inside and outside are equal.
