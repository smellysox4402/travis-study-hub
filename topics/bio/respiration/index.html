C1.2.1 · SL

THE ENERGY CURRENCY — ATP

ATP is the energy currency — the molecule that carries energy to wherever it is needed. It's a nucleotide with exactly the right properties to be the universal energy carrier.

🪙

The same ATP molecule powers every process

ATP = adenosine triphosphate · nucleotide · energy currency

ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is a nucleotide: a nitrogen-containing base (adenine), a five-carbon sugar (ribose) and a chain of three phosphate groups, each negatively charged. Like any currency, it's used for temporary storage of energy and transfer of energy between processes and between parts of the cell.

Four properties make it perfect for the job: it's soluble in water (moves freely through the cytoplasm), stable at neutral pH (like the cytoplasm), cannot pass through the phospholipid bilayer (so it can't leak out of cells and its movement between organelles stays controlled), and its third phosphate can be easily removed and reattached by hydrolysis and condensation.

FIG 1 — The ATP token: adenine + ribose + 3 phosphates = a nucleotide. Small energy release per hydrolysis means no wasteful excess — if each token dumped huge energy, most would be lost as heat.
Exam gold: the four properties question is a classic: soluble in water · stable at neutral pH · can't cross the phospholipid bilayer · third phosphate easily removed/reattached by hydrolysis/condensation. Say all four and link each to a consequence (moves freely · survives in cytoplasm · stays inside the cell · releases a small amount of energy on demand).
C1.2.2 · SL

ATP AT WORK IN CELLS

Respiration keeps energy available only if ATP is continually regenerated. ATP supplies energy to three main types of activity: building macromolecules, pumping against gradients, and movement.

🏗️

Three rooms where the currency gets cashed in

anabolism · active transport · movement · ATP→ADP + Pi

1. Synthesizing macromolecules: anabolic reactions that link monomers into polymers are endothermic — they only happen when coupled to ATP → ADP. Every time a monomer joins a growing polymer, one or more ATP molecules are spent: DNA during replication, RNA in transcription, proteins in translation.

2. Active transport: pumping ions against their concentration gradient. ATP drives reversible changes in the conformation (shape) of the pump protein — one shape lets the particle enter, the other lets it exit. ATP pays for the change from the stable to the less stable shape.

3. Movement: chromosomes dragged to the poles during mitosis, vesicles shuttling cargo, whole cells changing shape during cytokinesis, phagocytes crawling to infection sites, and muscle fibres contracting as actin and myosin filaments slide across each other.

FIG 2 — Three spending rooms: anabolism (DNA/RNA/protein, ATP per monomer), active transport (pump protein conformational change), movement (mitosis chromosomes, cytokinesis, phagocytes, actin-myosin sliding).
Don't mix up: ATP does not store energy long-term — that's glycogen and starch. ATP is the immediate transfer token; storage molecules are the savings account, ATP is the cash in your pocket.
C1.2.3 · SL

ATP TURNOVER & RECYCLING

ATP and ADP are constantly interconverted: hydrolysis of ATP releases energy, and rebuilding ATP from ADP + phosphate needs energy from somewhere. The turnover is relentless.

🔄

The same token, spent and reminted hundreds of times a day

ATP ⇌ ADP + Pi · hydrolysis vs condensation · ~120 mol/day

ATP → ADP + Pi releases energy — ATP holds more chemical potential energy than ADP. The amount released is small but sufficient for many cell processes. Sometimes the phosphate is first tacked onto another molecule (like a pump protein or a metabolic substrate); when it detaches, the energy is released and drives a change — a conformational change in a pump, or a chemical change of substrate into product.

ADP + Pi → ATP needs energy, supplied by cell respiration (oxidizing carbohydrates, fats or proteins), photosynthesis (light → chemical energy) or chemosynthesis (oxidizing inorganic substances such as sulfides). The whole cycle is not 100% efficient — some energy escapes as heat.

The numbers are wild: a human uses about 120 moles of ATP per day, but only about 0.2 moles exist in the body at any moment — so each ATP molecule is converted to ADP and back roughly 600 times per day.

FIG 3 — The recycling loop: hydrolysis spends the token (small, sufficient energy), condensation remints it with energy from respiration, photosynthesis or chemosynthesis. 120 mol/day used vs 0.2 mol present = ~600 turnovers per molecule per day.
Remember: ATP runs out fast if regeneration stops — neurons stop firing, muscle cells cramp, and cells start degrading within minutes. Continual regeneration of ATP from ADP + Pi is what keeps a supply of energy available.
C1.2.4 · SL

RESPIRATION: INTRODUCING THE STAGES

Cell respiration is the cell's energy system: it oxidises carbon compounds and uses the released energy to produce ATP. It needs gas exchange to keep the air moving — the two are interdependent.

⚡

Every living cell runs its own generator

oxidation of carbon compounds · glucose + fatty acids · gas exchange

Cell respiration is performed by all living cells: carbon compounds are oxidised to release energy, and that energy is used to produce ATP. A wide range of carbon compounds can serve as respiratory substrates, but glucose and fatty acids are the main ones in most cells. Humans get their substrates from food; plants use carbohydrates or lipids made by photosynthesis.

Gas exchange ≠ respiration — but they're interdependent. O₂ diffuses into cells and CO₂ diffuses out, each across the plasma membrane independently (no one-for-one swap). Without gas exchange, respiration stops (oxygen runs low, CO₂ builds up); without respiration, gas exchange stops (the concentration gradients that drive diffusion vanish).

FIG 4 — The power system: oxidise carbon compounds (glucose, fatty acids) → energy → ATP. Gas exchange and respiration are different processes but interdependent: respiration makes the gradients, the gradients drive diffusion.
Exam gold: "distinguish between cell respiration and gas exchange" — respiration = chemical process releasing energy from carbon compounds to make ATP (happens in every cell); gas exchange = physical diffusion of O₂ in and CO₂ out across the plasma membrane. Then add the interdependence sentence for full marks.
C1.2.5 · SL

AEROBIC VS ANAEROBIC

Aerobic respiration is the main pathway — high yield, needs oxygen, uses mitochondria. Anaerobic respiration is the emergency backup generator — 2 ATP per glucose, cytoplasm only, no mitochondria required.

⚔️

Two sets of word equations, one process

aerobic vs anaerobic · oxygen debt · lactate · sprint power

Aerobic (humans, most animals, plants): glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water, with a yield of more than 30 ATP per glucose. Reactions start in the cytoplasm but continue in mitochondria, where oxygen is used.

Anaerobic (humans, some bacteria): glucose → lactate, only 2 ATP per glucose. Everything happens in the cytoplasm; mitochondria are not required. Only carbohydrates can be used, and no water is produced.

Anaerobic respiration trades yield for speed — it supplies ATP very rapidly for short bursts of maximal power: weightlifters during a lift, sprinters up to 400 m, sprint finishes. Lactate tolerance limits how long this can last — that's why we can only sprint about 400 m. After vigorous contractions the lactate must be broken down, which needs oxygen — the extra demand is the oxygen debt.

FIG 5 — Aerobic: glucose + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O, >30 ATP, mitochondria. Anaerobic: glucose → lactate, 2 ATP, cytoplasm only. Backup wins on speed, loses on yield — and leaves an oxygen-debt bill.

Remember: anaerobic = fast but broke (2 ATP), aerobic = slow but rich (>30 ATP). The oxygen debt is the O₂ needed afterwards to clear the lactate — not the lactate itself.
C1.2.6 · SL

MEASURING RESPIRATION RATE

How fast is the cell respiring? The rate of cell respiration can be measured three ways — oxygen uptake, CO₂ production, or substrate consumption — and a heap of variables can change it.

📊

Ways to clock the burn rate

oxygen uptake · CO₂ production · substrate use · rate = amount ÷ time

Three measurements can give the rate of cell respiration: oxygen uptake, carbon dioxide production, or consumption of glucose (or another respiratory substrate). Oxygen uptake is the usual choice for aerobic respiration — but you can't measure it from how much air is breathed in, because air contains other gases and only a small fraction of each breath is absorbed.

A rate is an amount per unit time: if 50 mm³ of oxygen is used in 10 minutes, the rate is 5 mm³ of oxygen per minute. The calculated rate is the dependent variable; the variable you deliberately change (temperature, organism, activity level) is the independent variable.

Variables that matter: temperature, the type of organism, active vs inactive organisms — any experiment changes one variable while keeping all others constant. Temperature and pressure must be controlled because volume, pressure and temperature interact: a rise in temperature expands the air and skews the reading.

FIG 6 — The rate meter: three dials (O₂ uptake, CO₂ production, substrate consumption), rate = amount ÷ time, and a controlled experiment: one independent variable changed, everything else constant.
Don't mix up: the rate (amount per unit time, the dependent variable) with the variable you change (the independent variable). Temperature and pressure are controlled variables — they must not drift, or the volume readings lie.
C1.2.6 · SL

GLYCOLYSIS

The respirometer is a sealed chamber where CO₂ is absorbed by alkali, so any movement of fluid in the capillary tube is caused by oxygen uptake alone.

🧪

How the fuel gauge works

sealed container · potassium hydroxide · capillary fluid · water bath

A respirometer has three essential parts: a sealed glass or plastic container holding the organism or tissue with enough air, a base (alkali) such as potassium hydroxide (KOH) that absorbs the CO₂ produced, and a capillary tube containing fluid connected to the container to measure volume changes.

Normally, CO₂ production adds volume while O₂ intake reduces it. With the CO₂ absorbed by the KOH, any volume change must be due to oxygen intake alone — so fluid movement in the capillary = oxygen consumption. Convert distance moved to volume if the tube's internal diameter is known.

Control the variables: keep temperature constant with a thermostatically controlled water bath, and watch outside air pressure (it can move the fluid without any O₂ uptake). A second identical tube without respiring tissue corrects for pressure and temperature changes.

FIG 7 — Respirometer: KOH absorbs CO₂ → capillary fluid movement = O₂ uptake; thermostatic water bath controls temperature; identical flask without tissue corrects pressure/temperature drift.
Exam gold: "why does the respirometer need KOH?" — without it, CO₂ production would add volume and mask the O₂ uptake. "Why a thermostatically controlled water bath?" — volume, pressure and temperature interact; constant temperature keeps readings honest. "Why a control tube?" — it corrects for changes in outside air pressure that would move the fluid regardless of respiration.
C1.2.7 · AHL

STRUCTURE OF THE MITOCHONDRION

Oxidation and reduction always happen together — and in respiration, oxidation means removing hydrogen. NAD is the carrier that takes up the hydrogen (and its electrons).

🧤

Redox: one molecule loses electrons, another gains them

oxidation = loss of electrons · reduction = gain · NAD = nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide

Oxidation is the loss of electrons from a substance; reduction is the gain of electrons. They always occur together because electrons are transferred from one substance to another. The lab classic: in the Benedict's test, blue Cu²⁺ ions gain electrons from sugar molecules and become insoluble copper atoms — a red/orange precipitate. The sugar is oxidised (it lost electrons), the copper is reduced.

NAD (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) is the main electron carrier in respiration — two nucleotides joined (one with a nicotinamide base, one with an adenine base). Substrates are oxidised by removal of two hydrogen atoms (dehydrogenation); each hydrogen is an electron plus a proton. NAD⁺ accepts two electrons and one proton, becoming NADH, and the other proton (H⁺) is released.

FIG 8 — Oxidation by dehydrogenation: substrate loses 2 H (2 e⁻ + 2 H⁺), NAD⁺ takes 2 e⁻ + 1 H⁺ → NADH + H⁺. Benedict's test shows the same redox in a test tube: Cu²⁺ blue → Cu red.
Don't mix up: hydrogen atoms are removed, not hydrogen ions. Removing H⁺ alone would take no electrons, so the substrate wouldn't be oxidised. And NAD⁺ becomes reduced NAD by gaining electrons — reduced means electron-rich, not "smaller".
C1.2.8 · AHL · stages 1–2

THE LINK REACTION & KREBS CYCLE

Glycolysis is the fast street-level generator: no oxygen needed, runs in the cytoplasm, and converts glucose into two pyruvates in four stages. First, the doors open: phosphorylation and lysis.

🚪

Stage 1 · Phosphorylation — spend tokens to prime the fuel

glycolysis · cytoplasm · 2 ATP in · fructose-1,6-bisphosphate

Stage 1 (phosphorylation): two ATP molecules are invested to add phosphate groups to glucose — first to glucose-6-phosphate and fructose-6-phosphate, then a second phosphorylation converts fructose-6-phosphate to fructose-1,6-bisphosphate. The phosphorylated sugar is trapped inside the cell and primed for splitting.

Stage 2 (lysis): fructose-1,6-bisphosphate is split into two molecules of triose phosphate (3C each). Now the whole pathway deals with two identical three-carbon halves.

Every step is catalysed by a different enzyme — a whole chain of bouncers, each with one specific door.

FIG 9 — Doors open: phosphorylation spends 2 ATP to make fructose-1,6-bisphosphate; lysis splits it into 2 triose phosphates (3C). Cytoplasm, no O₂, different enzyme per step.
Remember: the 2 ATP spent in stage 1 are an investment — they're repaid 4-fold in stage 4. Net yield = 4 − 2 = 2 ATP per glucose.
C1.2.8 · AHL · stages 3–4

THE KREBS CYCLE — FULL DETAIL

Now the generator pays out: oxidation hands hydrogen to NAD, and ATP formation banks four tokens per glucose. The net result of glycolysis: 2 pyruvate, 2 reduced NAD and a net 2 ATP.

🔋

Stage 3 · Oxidation — hydrogen is removed and carried by NAD

oxidation · reduced NAD · bisphosphoglycerate · 4 ATP out

Stage 3 (oxidation): each triose phosphate is oxidised by removal of hydrogen, accepted by NAD → reduced NAD. (Hydrogen atoms, not ions — removing H⁺ alone would lose no electrons.) Oxidising the sugar produces an organic acid; the energy released lets a second phosphate attach, giving bisphosphoglycerate.

Stage 4 (ATP formation): the two phosphates of bisphosphoglycerate are transferred to ADP — twice, because there are two bisphosphoglycerates per glucose. Four ATP are produced per glucose, converting glycerate into pyruvate, the end product.

Overall per glucose: one 6C glucose → two 3C pyruvates; 2 NAD → 2 reduced NAD; net 2 ATP (2 used, 4 produced). Small yield — but it needs no oxygen, so it's the lifeline when O₂ runs short.

FIG 10 — Full power: oxidation (hydrogen → NAD → reduced NAD, bisphosphoglycerate) then ATP formation (4 ATP out). Net per glucose: 2 pyruvate + 2 reduced NAD + 2 ATP, oxygen-free.
Exam gold: the four stages in order — phosphorylation (2 ATP in), lysis (splits to 2 triose phosphate), oxidation (hydrogen removed → reduced NAD), ATP formation (4 ATP out). Net yield 2 ATP + 2 reduced NAD; 2 pyruvate. You don't need intermediate names, but you do need the net numbers and that each step has its own enzyme.
C1.2.9 · AHL

THE ELECTRON TRANSPORT CHAIN

When oxygen runs out, the generator can't dump its hydrogen anywhere — unless pyruvate steps in. Converting pyruvate to lactate regenerates NAD, and glycolysis keeps spinning.

🚨

Pyruvate accepts the hydrogen so NAD is regenerated

pyruvate → lactate · NAD regeneration · lactic fermentation · oxygen debt

Glycolysis needs a constant supply of glucose, ADP and NAD. NAD runs out unless it's regenerated by oxidising reduced NAD — two hydrogen atoms (two protons and two electrons) must be transferred to another molecule.

In human (and some animal and bacterial) cells, hydrogen is transferred from reduced NAD to pyruvate, converting pyruvate into lactate — in the cytoplasm. Two pyruvates are made per glucose, and each regenerates one NAD, so all the NAD used in glycolysis is recovered. As long as glucose is available and lactate doesn't build up too high, anaerobic respiration can continue indefinitely.

This is lactic fermentation — and it's a food-preservation trick: accumulating lactic acid lowers the pH and stops decomposition by bacteria and fungi. Yoghurt, kimchi, sauerkraut and silage are all made this way.

FIG 11 — Emergency backup: pyruvate + reduced NAD → lactate + NAD (cytoplasm). NAD is regenerated, glycolysis continues at 2 ATP/glucose. Lactic fermentation = yoghurt, kimchi, sauerkraut, silage.
Remember: the point of making lactate is NOT to get rid of pyruvate — it's to regenerate NAD so glycolysis can keep producing its 2 ATP. Lactate is a by-product of recycling, not a waste product with a job.
C1.2.10 · AHL

OXIDATIVE PHOSPHORYLATION

Yeast runs the same backup generator differently: it turns pyruvate into ethanol and CO₂ in two stages. That CO₂ raises bread; that ethanol brews beer and wine.

🍞

Same glycolysis, different way to recycle NAD

ethanol fermentation · ethanal · decarboxylation · facultative anaerobe

Yeast (a unicellular fungus, and a facultative anaerobe — it can respire with or without oxygen) regenerates NAD by converting pyruvate to ethanol + CO₂, in two stages. Stage 1: decarboxylation — CO₂ is removed from pyruvate, leaving ethanal. Stage 2: two hydrogens transfer from reduced NAD to ethanal, making ethanol and regenerating NAD. Same number of NADs regenerated per glucose as in human cells, so the 2-ATP-per-glucose pathway continues as long as glucose holds out and ethanol doesn't get too high.

Baking: yeast in dough respires — aerobically at first, then anaerobically once the dough's oxygen is gone. The viscous dough traps the CO₂ bubbles, making it swell and rise; the ethanol evaporates during baking.

Brewing: the goal is ethanol. Wine ferments grape juice; beer uses barley starch first converted to sugar by amylase (yeast can't metabolise starch). In big tanks oxygen is limited, so yeast switches anaerobic; fermentation ends when the sugar runs out or the ethanol becomes toxic to the yeast — about 15% by volume.

FIG 12 — Yeast fermentation: decarboxylation → ethanal (+CO₂), then reduced NAD → ethanol (+NAD). Bread: trapped CO₂ rises the dough, ethanol evaporates. Brewing: ethanol accumulates until ~15% by volume kills the yeast.
Don't mix up: humans regenerate NAD by making lactate; yeast regenerates NAD by making ethanol + CO₂. Both give 2 ATP per glucose, both run in the cytoplasm — only the final product differs.
C1.2.11 · AHL

ANAEROBIC PATHWAYS IN MUSCLE

If oxygen is available, pyruvate moves into the mitochondrial matrix and docks at the link reaction: a three-enzyme complex that strips CO₂, oxidises the remnant, and loads it onto coenzyme A.

📦

Pyruvate in, acetyl CoA out — the handoff to the turbine

link reaction · matrix · 3-enzyme complex · acetyl coenzyme A

Glycolysis leaves pyruvate in the cytoplasm, but the link reaction and Krebs cycle happen in the matrix of the mitochondrion. A transporter protein in the outer membrane moves pyruvate across. This conversion links glycolysis to the Krebs cycle — hence the name.

A complex of three enzymes carries out three processes: 1 · decarboxylation — removal of CO₂ changes 3C pyruvate into a 2C molecule; 2 · oxidation — two electrons are removed and accepted by NAD → reduced NAD; 3 · binding — the acetyl group attaches to coenzyme A, forming acetyl coenzyme A (acetyl CoA).

Per glucose: two pyruvates → 2 acetyl CoA + 2 CO₂ + 2 reduced NAD. The acetyl group (2C) is now fuelled up for the turbine loop.

FIG 13 — Delivery dock: pyruvate (3C) in the matrix is decarboxylated (−CO₂), oxidised (→ reduced NAD) and bound to coenzyme A → acetyl CoA (2C). Three-enzyme complex, matrix, aerobic only.
Remember: the link reaction is where the aerobic flag drops. Without oxygen, pyruvate gets diverted to lactate or ethanol instead; with oxygen, it's loaded onto CoA and sent to the turbine.
C1.2.12 · AHL

LACTATE FERMENTATION

The Krebs cycle is a turbine loop that never stops turning: acetyl groups are fed in, citrate (6C) forms, and two decarboxylations plus four oxidations spin it back to oxaloacetate (4C) — paying out reduced NAD, reduced FAD and ATP each turn.

🔁

One acetyl group in, a stream of energy carriers out

Krebs cycle · matrix · citrate 6C · oxaloacetate 4C

Acetyl groups from the link reaction are fed into the cycle by transfer from coenzyme A to oxaloacetate (4C), producing citrate (6C) — named in honour of Hans Krebs, who won a Nobel Prize for discovering the cycle. Citrate is converted back to oxaloacetate by a series of enzyme-catalysed reactions: two decarboxylations remove carbon as CO₂, and four oxidations (dehydrogenations) release energy carried off by electron carriers.

The electrons removed in the oxidations are transferred to NAD or FAD — both electron carriers, both hydrogen carriers. Reduced NAD and reduced FAD carry the energy to the electron transport chain in the inner mitochondrial membrane.

Net per turn of the cycle: one acetyl group consumed · 3 reduced NAD + 1 reduced FAD + 1 ATP produced · 2 CO₂ released. All the carbon in sugar or fat is removed by decarboxylation here or in the link reaction.

FIG 14 — The turbine loop: acetyl (2C) + oxaloacetate (4C) → citrate (6C) → back to oxaloacetate via 2 decarboxylations + 4 oxidations. Per turn: 3 reduced NAD + 1 reduced FAD + 1 ATP + 2 CO₂.
Exam gold: you only need to name citrate (6C) and oxaloacetate (4C) — and the per-turn yield (3 reduced NAD, 1 reduced FAD, 1 ATP, 2 CO₂). The four oxidations are dehydrogenations: hydrogen removed, electrons carried away by NAD or FAD.
C1.2.13 + C1.2.14 · AHL

THE RESPIRATORY SUBSTRATES

Reduced NAD and reduced FAD hand their electron pairs to the electron transport chain — a conveyor belt of carriers in the inner mitochondrial membrane. The energy of the electron flow pumps protons, building the gradient that powers everything next.

🎢

Electrons in, protons pumped — energy stored as a gradient

ETC · cristae · proton pumps · 10 H⁺ vs 6 H⁺

C1.2.13 — handing over the energy: groups of proteins in the inner mitochondrial membrane act as electron carriers, each accepting and passing on pairs of electrons — the electron transport chain (ETC). The first carrier accepts a pair from reduced NAD, converting it back to NAD and gaining chemical energy. Reduced NAD arrives from glycolysis (entering the mitochondrion), the link reaction and the Krebs cycle. Reduced FAD also transfers a pair — but with less energy, so it enters part way along the chain, at a carrier with higher electron affinity. Cristae (infoldings) maximise the membrane area and the number of ETCs.

C1.2.14 — building the proton gradient: energy is released at every step as electrons pass from carrier to carrier. The three main carriers each act as a proton pump: the first two pump 4 protons each per pair of electrons, the third pumps 2 — a total of 10 protons from reduced NAD, but only 6 from reduced FAD (fed in after the first carrier).

Protons are pumped from the matrix to the intermembrane space — against the concentration gradient, so it costs energy, which is stored as the gradient itself: high [H⁺] in the intermembrane space, low [H⁺] in the matrix. That stored energy will make ATP in Act 16.

FIG 15 — The electron transport chain: carriers accept electron pairs (reduced NAD at the start, reduced FAD partway), three main carriers pump 4+4+2 = 10 H⁺ (reduced NAD) or 6 H⁺ (reduced FAD) into the intermembrane space → high [H⁺] outside, low [H⁺] in the matrix.
Don't mix up: reduced NAD delivers at the start of the chain (10 H⁺ pumped), reduced FAD enters partway (6 H⁺) — because its electrons carry less energy. And the protons are pumped from matrix to intermembrane space, not the other way round.
C1.2.15 · AHL

THE LINK REACTION — ACETYL COA

Chemiosmosis is the water-wheel: protons flow back down their gradient through ATP synthase, spinning a rotor that cranks ADP + phosphate into ATP.

💧

The proton waterfall turns the ATP factory

chemiosmosis · ATP synthase · rotor (10 c subunits) · 2.5 / 1.5 ATP

ATP synthase is a large complex protein that phosphorylates ADP → ATP — an endergonic reaction, so it needs energy. In chemiosmosis, protons move down their concentration gradient from the high concentration in the intermembrane space to the lower concentration in the matrix, and the released energy links a phosphate to ADP. (In plain osmosis the energy of water moving down a gradient isn't harnessed; chemiosmosis is the version that captures it.)

The machine: a drum of 10 c subunits in the membrane, each with a proton binding site; two misaligned half-channels (a) force the drum to rotate for protons to pass. The drum is tightly connected to a stalk (γ) — together the rotor. As the stalk spins inside a ring of α and β subunits (3 β subunits, each with an active site), the conformational changes first bind ADP + phosphate, then link them into ATP, then release it. A rotor arm (b₂ + δ) stops the ring from spinning.

The maths: one proton per c subunit → 10 protons per full rotation; 3 β subunits each make one ATP per turn → 3 ATP per rotation. Since reduced NAD drives 10 pumped protons and reduced FAD only 6: 2.5 ATP per reduced NAD, 1.5 ATP per reduced FAD.

FIG 16 — Chemiosmosis: protons fall down their gradient through ATP synthase; the rotor (10 c subunits, 1 H⁺ each) spins the γ stalk, which turns the 3 β subunits — one ATP each per rotation. 10 H⁺ → 3 ATP → 2.5 per reduced NAD, 1.5 per reduced FAD.
Exam gold: the mechanism chain: proton gradient → protons through ATP synthase → rotor (10 c subunits) turns γ stalk → conformational changes in β subunits → ADP + Pi bind, link, ATP released. Numbers to quote: 2.5 ATP per reduced NAD, 1.5 per reduced FAD.
C1.2.16 · AHL

RESPIRATION & ENERGY YIELD

Oxygen is the final electron acceptor at the end of the electron transport chain: it has the strongest affinity for electrons, so it accepts the spent electrons — plus protons from the matrix — and forms water.

🅾️

Without the final customer, the whole belt jams

terminal electron acceptor · metabolic water · electron backup

Each electron carrier in the ETC has a stronger affinity for electrons than the previous one — so only a substance with a very strong affinity can remove electrons from the last carrier. Most organisms use molecular oxygen (O₂): the terminal electron acceptor. O₂ accepts electrons from the final carrier and hydrogen ions from the matrix, producing water — about half a litre per day in humans. Making water also helps maintain the proton gradient.

Everything except glycolysis depends on oxygen. If O₂ runs out: electrons aren't removed from the end, all the carriers become reduced, no electrons are accepted from reduced NAD at the start, and reduced NAD accumulates. Once all the NAD is reduced, the oxidations of the link reaction and Krebs cycle can't happen — they stop. Anaerobic respiration keeps glycolysis going in the cytoplasm, but the yield crashes from 32 ATP to just 2 ATP per glucose.

FIG 17 — Oxygen: terminal electron acceptor with the strongest affinity. It takes spent electrons + protons from the matrix → water (~0.5 L/day). No O₂ = chain backs up, link + Krebs stop, only glycolysis (2 ATP vs 32).
Remember: oxygen's job is to keep the conveyor belt moving — by taking electrons off the end, it lets carriers accept new ones from reduced NAD. No final customer, no flow, no ATP beyond glycolysis.
C1.2.17 · AHL

LIPIDS VS CARBOHYDRATES AS FUEL

Lipids and carbohydrates are both respiratory substrates — but they burn differently. Lipids pack nearly twice the energy per gram, and only carbohydrates can power anaerobic respiration.

⛽

Two fuels, two energy densities, two metabolic paths

17 kJ/g vs 37 kJ/g · glycolysis first vs fatty acids first

Carbohydrates (glucose, fructose): the first stage is glycolysis, which makes some ATP without oxygen — so anaerobic respiration is possible. Pyruvate then converts to acetyl groups via the link reaction for the Krebs cycle (oxygen needed). Energy yield: 17 kJ per gram — more than 50% of the mass is oxygen, and oxygen doesn't yield energy when oxidised.

Lipids (fats, oils): the first stage is breakdown of fatty acids to acetyl groups in the matrix of the mitochondrion, which then feed the Krebs cycle — both stages need oxygen, so anaerobic respiration is impossible with lipids. Energy yield: 37 kJ per gram — nearly twice the carbohydrates, because almost 90% of lipid mass is carbon and hydrogen, both of which yield energy when oxidised.

Both fuels are oxidised in respiration — which is why both work as energy-yielding foods and as energy stores. The energy yield per gram and the pathway taken are where they differ.

FIG 18 — Fuel mix: carbs 17 kJ/g, glycolysis first, anaerobic possible. Lipids 37 kJ/g (≈2×), fatty acids → acetyl groups in the matrix, aerobic only. Energy density comes from C + H content.
Exam gold: the "why do lipids give more energy per gram?" answer: lipids are nearly 90% carbon and hydrogen (both yield energy on oxidation), while carbohydrates are >50% oxygen by mass — and oxygen is already oxidised, so it yields no energy. And remember: no glycolysis, no anaerobic respiration — so lipids can't run the backup generator.

Quick recap

⚡ CHEAT

Fifteen one-liners. If you can say them out loud, you know the show.

🪙 ATP = nucleotide — adenine + ribose + 3 phosphates; soluble, stable at neutral pH, can't cross the bilayer, third phosphate pops off by hydrolysis.
🏗️ Tokens pay for three things — macromolecule synthesis (DNA/RNA/protein), active transport (pump conformation), movement (chromosomes, cytokinesis, phagocytes, actin-myosin).
🔄 ATP → ADP + Pi releases energy — rebuilt by respiration, photosynthesis or chemosynthesis; ~120 mol/day used, only ~0.2 mol present → ~600 turnovers each.
⚙️ Respiration oxidises carbon compounds — glucose and fatty acids are the main substrates; gas exchange and respiration are interdependent (gradients ↔ diffusion).
🎪 Aerobic: glucose + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O — >30 ATP, mitochondria required.
🚨 Anaerobic human: glucose → lactate — 2 ATP, cytoplasm only, no mitochondria; lactate limit ≈ 400 m sprint.
🧾 Oxygen debt = the bill — the O₂ needed afterwards to break down lactate from vigorous exercise.
📊 Respirometer: KOH absorbs CO₂ — capillary fluid movement = O₂ uptake; thermostatic water bath + control tube keep it honest.
🧤 NAD⁺ + 2e⁻ + 1H⁺ → NADH + H⁺ — oxidation = loss of electrons (Benedict's: Cu²⁺ blue → Cu red).
🏭 Glycolysis (cytoplasm): 2 ATP in, 4 out — 2 reduced NAD, 2 pyruvate (3C); net 2 ATP, no O₂; each step its own enzyme.
🍺 Fermentation regenerates NAD — pyruvate → lactate (humans: yoghurt, kimchi, sauerkraut, silage) or ethanal → ethanol + CO₂ (yeast: bread rises, brewing stops ~15%).
📦 Link reaction (matrix) — pyruvate −CO₂, oxidised (−2e⁻ → reduced NAD), + CoA → acetyl CoA; 3-enzyme complex.
🔁 Krebs (matrix) — acetyl (2C) + oxaloacetate (4C) → citrate (6C) → back via 2 decarboxylations + 4 oxidations: 3 reduced NAD + 1 reduced FAD + 1 ATP + 2 CO₂ per turn.
🎢 ETC + chemiosmosis — 10 H⁺ pumped per reduced NAD (6 per reduced FAD); protons spin the ATP synthase rotor (10 c) → 2.5 ATP/reduced NAD, 1.5/reduced FAD.
🅾️ Oxygen = terminal electron acceptor — takes e⁻ + H⁺ → water (~0.5 L/day); lipids 37 kJ/g vs carbs 17 kJ/g, and no anaerobic respiration on lipids.
Test yourself

✍ SELF-TEST

10 questions. Pick an answer and you'll instantly see why.

1. What exactly is ATP?

✅ ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is a nucleotide: adenine + ribose + three phosphates, each negatively charged. It's the energy currency, not a storage molecule and not an enzyme.
❌ ATP is a nucleotide (base + 5-carbon sugar + phosphate chain). Long-term storage is glycogen/starch's job, and enzymes are proteins that catalyse — ATP is the energy token itself.

2. Which property makes ATP suitable as the energy currency?

✅ All four properties matter: soluble in water, stable at neutral pH, can't cross the bilayer (controlled movement), and hydrolysis releases a small amount of energy — enough for processes, without wasteful excess lost as heat.
❌ The release is deliberately SMALL (not huge) — excess energy would be wasted as heat. And ATP is stable at NEUTRAL pH, not acidic. It's the bilayer-blocking + controlled hydrolysis combo that makes it the ideal token.

3. Humans use about 120 moles of ATP per day, yet only ~0.2 moles exist at any moment. What does that mean?

✅ The pool is tiny (0.2 mol) but the turnover is enormous (~120 mol/day) — so each molecule is recycled ~600×/day. If regeneration stops, neurons fail and muscle cramps — cells degrade within minutes.
❌ ATP is NOT stored in bulk — only ~0.2 moles exist at any time. The whole system runs on rapid, continuous recycling of the small pool: hydrolysis (spend) and condensation (remint) ~600 times per molecule per day.

4. Energy to rebuild ATP from ADP + phosphate can come from…

✅ Three energy sources rebuild ATP: cell respiration (oxidising carbs/fats/proteins), photosynthesis (light → chemical energy), and chemosynthesis (oxidising inorganic substances such as sulfides).
❌ All three supply the energy: respiration, photosynthesis, and chemosynthesis. Photosynthesis isn't the only source, and respiration isn't the only source — the cell takes energy from whichever is available.

5. Where does anaerobic respiration in humans happen, and what does it produce?

✅ Anaerobic respiration in humans: glucose → lactate, 2 ATP per glucose, all in the cytoplasm; mitochondria are not required. Only carbohydrates can be used, and no water is produced.
❌ The cytoplasm does the whole anaerobic job (no mitochondria needed). Ethanol + CO₂ is the YEAST version — humans make lactate. And the yield is 2 ATP, not 30+ (that's aerobic).

6. In a respirometer, why is potassium hydroxide (KOH) essential?

✅ CO₂ production normally adds volume while O₂ uptake reduces it. KOH absorbs all the CO₂, so the movement of fluid in the capillary measures oxygen consumption alone. Temperature is controlled separately, with a thermostatic water bath.
❌ KOH's job is chemical: absorb CO₂. The seal comes from the apparatus design, and temperature control comes from the thermostatically controlled water bath — both separate parts of the respirometer.

7. When NAD⁺ is reduced to NADH during respiration, it accepts…

✅ NAD⁺ accepts 2 electrons + 1 proton, becoming NADH; the second proton (H⁺) is released. Substrates are oxidised by removal of hydrogen atoms (each = electron + proton) — dehydrogenation.
❌ NAD⁺ takes two electrons and one proton → NADH, with the other H⁺ released. If it took two whole hydrogen atoms, electrons wouldn't be transferred — and oxidation (electron loss) wouldn't happen.

8. What is the net yield of glycolysis per molecule of glucose?

✅ Per glucose: 2 ATP invested in phosphorylation, 4 produced in ATP formation → net 2 ATP; plus 2 reduced NAD and 2 pyruvate (3C). No oxygen is needed — it's the street-level generator.
❌ Watch the investment: 2 ATP are USED in stage 1, 4 are MADE in stage 4 → net 2 ATP, with 2 reduced NAD and 2 pyruvate. 32 ATP is the full aerobic total, not glycolysis.

9. What is the yield of ONE turn of the Krebs cycle?

✅ Per turn: 3 reduced NAD, 1 reduced FAD, 1 ATP, 2 CO₂. Acetyl (2C) + oxaloacetate (4C) → citrate (6C) → back to oxaloacetate via 2 decarboxylations and 4 oxidations.
❌ One turn gives 3 reduced NAD + 1 reduced FAD + 1 ATP + 2 CO₂. The 4 oxidations are what load up the electron carriers — the cycle is an energy-capture machine, not just a CO₂ factory.

10. Chemiosmosis: how much ATP does one reduced NAD deliver, and why?

✅ Reduced NAD → 10 protons pumped → one full rotation of the ATP synthase rotor (10 c subunits) → 3 ATP made at the β subunits — quoted as 2.5 ATP per reduced NAD. Reduced FAD (6 protons) gives 1.5.
❌ It's reduced FAD that enters partway (6 protons → 1.5 ATP). Reduced NAD starts at the first carrier, drives 10 protons, and yields 2.5 ATP — and reduced NAD definitely reaches the ETC; it's the chain's main fuel.

  • ✓C1.2.1ATP = nucleotide (adenine + ribose + 3 phosphates in a chain, each negatively charged). Energy currency properties: soluble in water (moves freely in cytoplasm), stable at neutral pH, cannot pass through the phospholipid bilayer (can't diffuse out; movement between organelles controlled), third phosphate easily removed/reattached by hydrolysis/condensation. ATP + H₂O → ADP + phosphate + energy; the small energy release avoids wasteful excess lost as heat.
  • ✓C1.2.2ATP supplies energy to: (1) synthesis of macromolecules — anabolism, ATP per monomer linked: DNA replication, RNA transcription, protein translation; (2) active transport — reversible conformational changes of pump proteins (stable → less stable shape costs ATP), particle enters in one shape, exits in the other; (3) movement — chromosomes to poles in mitosis, vesicle transport, cytokinesis shape change, phagocyte locomotion, muscle actin-myosin filament sliding.
  • ✓C1.2.3ATP → ADP + Pi releases a small but sufficient amount of energy (phosphate may be transferred to another molecule first; energy released when it detaches). Regeneration of ATP needs energy from cell respiration (oxidising carbs/fats/proteins), photosynthesis, or chemosynthesis (oxidising inorganic substances such as sulfides). Human body uses ~120 moles ATP/day but only ~0.2 moles exist at any time → each molecule recycled ~600×/day; without ATP neurons fail, muscle cramps, cells degrade within minutes. Interconversions are not 100% efficient — some energy becomes heat.
  • ✓C1.2.4Cell respiration: all living cells oxidise carbon compounds to release energy used to produce ATP. Glucose and fatty acids are the main respiratory substrates; a wide range of carbon compounds can be used. Gas exchange ≠ respiration but interdependent: O₂ in and CO₂ out by simple diffusion across the plasma membrane (independently, not one-for-one); respiration creates the gradients that drive diffusion, gas exchange supplies O₂ and removes CO₂.
  • ✓C1.2.5Aerobic vs anaerobic in humans. Aerobic: glucose + oxygen → CO₂ + water; >30 ATP per glucose; oxygen used as electron acceptor; carbs, lipids, amino acids (after deamination) usable; reactions start in cytoplasm, continue in mitochondria. Anaerobic: glucose → lactate; 2 ATP per glucose; cytoplasm only, mitochondria NOT required; only carbohydrates usable; no water produced. Yeast anaerobic: glucose → ethanol + CO₂. Used for maximal power: weightlifters, sprinters to 400 m, sprint finishes; lactate tolerance limits duration.
  • ✓C1.2.5Oxygen debt: after vigorous muscle contractions, lactate must be broken down, which requires oxygen — the demand that builds up during anaerobic respiration is the oxygen debt; clearing it can take several minutes. Lactate concentration limit ≈ the reason we can only sprint ~400 m.
  • ✓C1.2.6Rate of cell respiration from: oxygen uptake, CO₂ production, or consumption of glucose/substrate. Rate = amount ÷ time (e.g. 50 mm³ O₂ in 10 min = 5 mm³ min⁻¹); calculated rate = dependent variable. Independent variables: temperature, type of organism, active vs inactive. Respirometer: sealed container + KOH (absorbs CO₂) + capillary tube with fluid (volume change = O₂ uptake); convert mm to volume using tube diameter; control temperature (thermostatic water bath) and pressure (identical control tube without tissue).
  • ✓C1.2.7Oxidation = loss of electrons, reduction = gain; always occur together (redox, electron transfer). Benedict's test: Cu²⁺ (blue) + electrons from sugar → Cu atoms (red/orange precipitate); sugar oxidised. NAD (nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide) = main electron carrier; substrates oxidised by removal of two hydrogen atoms (dehydrogenation); NAD⁺ accepts 2 electrons + 1 proton → NADH; other proton (H⁺) released.
  • ✓C1.2.8Glycolysis (cytoplasm, no O₂, different enzyme per step): Stage 1 phosphorylation — 2 ATP invested, glucose → fructose-1,6-bisphosphate; Stage 2 lysis — splits to 2 triose phosphate (3C); Stage 3 oxidation — hydrogen removed, accepted by NAD → reduced NAD, bisphosphoglycerate forms; Stage 4 ATP formation — 4 ATP produced. Net per glucose: 2 pyruvate (3C) + 2 reduced NAD + net 2 ATP (4 − 2).
  • ✓C1.2.9Pyruvate → lactate regenerates NAD in anaerobic respiration: reduced NAD transfers 2 H to pyruvate (cytoplasm); 2 pyruvates per glucose regenerate all NAD used in glycolysis → glycolysis continues indefinitely (2 ATP/glucose) as long as glucose available and lactate not too high. Lactic fermentation: yoghurt, kimchi, sauerkraut, silage (lactic acid lowers pH, prevents decomposition).
  • ✓C1.2.10Yeast (facultative anaerobe) ethanol fermentation, 2 stages: (1) decarboxylation — pyruvate −CO₂ → ethanal; (2) reduced NAD → ethanal + 2H → ethanol (+ NAD regenerated). Baking: CO₂ bubbles trapped in viscous dough make it rise; ethanol evaporates during baking. Brewing: wine (grape juice), beer (barley starch + amylase — yeast can't use starch); fermentation ends when sugar runs out or ethanol becomes toxic (~15% by volume). Also bioethanol fuel.
  • ✓C1.2.11Link reaction (mitochondrial matrix; pyruvate moved in by a transporter protein in the outer membrane; aerobic only): complex of 3 enzymes does (1) decarboxylation — pyruvate (3C) −CO₂ → 2C molecule; (2) oxidation — 2 electrons removed, accepted by NAD → reduced NAD; (3) binding to coenzyme A → acetyl coenzyme A (2C). Per glucose: 2 acetyl CoA + 2 CO₂ + 2 reduced NAD.
  • ✓C1.2.12Krebs cycle (matrix): acetyl group transferred from CoA to oxaloacetate (4C) → citrate (6C); citrate converted back to oxaloacetate via 2 decarboxylations (2 CO₂) + 4 oxidations (dehydrogenations), electrons to NAD or FAD. Per turn: 3 reduced NAD + 1 reduced FAD + 1 ATP + 2 CO₂; one acetyl consumed. All carbon of sugar/fat removed as CO₂ here or in the link reaction. Oxaloacetate regenerated — the cycle turns.
  • ✓C1.2.13-14ETC (inner mitochondrial membrane; cristae increase area): first carrier accepts electron pair from reduced NAD (→ NAD, carrier gains energy); reduced NAD from glycolysis, link reaction and Krebs cycle; reduced FAD enters partway (less energy). Three main carriers act as proton pumps: first two pump 4 H⁺ each per pair, third pumps 2 → 10 H⁺ per pair from reduced NAD, 6 from reduced FAD. Protons pumped from matrix → intermembrane space against the gradient; energy stored as the proton gradient (high [H⁺] intermembrane space, low [H⁺] matrix). Role of ETC: generate and maintain the proton gradient.
  • ✓C1.2.15Chemiosmosis: protons flow down their gradient (intermembrane space → matrix) through ATP synthase; energy used to phosphorylate ADP → ATP (endergonic). ATP synthase: transmembrane region (proton channel) + globular region (active sites in matrix). Rotor = drum of 10 c subunits (1 proton each) + stalk γ; two misaligned half-channels (a) force rotation; stalk spins inside ring of α/β subunits (3 β, one ATP each per rotation); rotor arm (b₂ + δ) holds the ring. Yield: 10 protons per rotation → 2.5 ATP per reduced NAD, 1.5 per reduced FAD.
  • ✓C1.2.16-17Oxygen = terminal electron acceptor (strongest affinity for electrons): accepts electrons from the final carrier + protons from the matrix → water (~half litre/day in humans). Without O₂: ETC carriers stay reduced, reduced NAD accumulates, link reaction + Krebs stop; only glycolysis continues (2 ATP vs 32 aerobic). Lipids vs carbohydrates: carbs 17 kJ/g (glycolysis first → anaerobic possible; >50% of mass is oxygen); lipids 37 kJ/g ≈ 2× (fatty acids → acetyl groups in matrix → Krebs; aerobic only; ~90% of mass is C + H). Both oxidised; both energy-yielding foods and stores.
