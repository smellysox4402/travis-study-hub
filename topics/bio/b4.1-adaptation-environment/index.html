B4.1.1

HABITAT

A habitat is the place in which a community, species, population or organism lives — described geographically and physically.

A habitat is the place where an organism, population, species or community lives. Its description includes both the geographical location (where) and the physical conditions (e.g. temperature, moisture, soil).

B4.1.2

ADAPTATIONS TO THE ABIOTIC ENVIRONMENT

Organisms are adapted to the abiotic conditions of their habitat — e.g. a grass of sand dunes and a tree of mangrove swamps.

SAND DUNES

A dune grass

Adapted to low water, moving sand and high salt exposure — e.g. deep roots, rolled leaves and waxy cuticles to reduce water loss.

MANGROVE

A mangrove tree

Adapted to waterlogged, salty swamps — e.g. aerial prop roots for support and breathing, and salt-excreting glands, plus vivipary (seed germinates on the tree).

B4.1.3

ABIOTIC VARIABLES AFFECTING DISTRIBUTION

Abiotic variables determine where species can live. Adaptations give a species its distribution.

  • Plants: light, temperature, water, soil pH, mineral availability, salinity.
  • Animals: temperature, water, salinity, oxygen levels, light.
  • A species' adaptations determine which set of abiotic conditions it can survive in — giving it its distribution.
B4.1.4

RANGE OF TOLERANCE & TRANSECTS

Each species has a range of tolerance of a limiting factor. You should use transect data to correlate distribution with an abiotic variable.

  • A species can tolerate a range of values of a limiting factor; outside this range it cannot survive or reproduce.
  • For a limiting factor (the factor nearest its limit), a small change has a big effect.
  • Application: use a transect (a line across the habitat) to record species presence and the abiotic variable (e.g. pH, moisture, salinity), then correlate them.
B4.1.5

CONDITIONS FOR CORAL REEF FORMATION

Coral reefs form under specific conditions: water depth, pH, salinity, clarity and temperature.

  • Temperature: warm (typically 23–29 °C).
  • Water depth: shallow, so light reaches the zooxanthellae.
  • Salinity: stable, normal seawater salinity.
  • Clarity: clear water (little sediment) for light.
  • pH: near seawater pH (avoid acidification).
B4.1.6

ABIOTIC FACTORS DETERMINE BIOME DISTRIBUTION

For a given temperature and rainfall pattern, one natural ecosystem is expected — temperature and water determine biomes.

Terrestrial biomes are determined mainly by temperature and rainfall. For any combination of the two, one natural ecosystem type is likely — e.g. hot + wet = tropical rainforest; hot + dry = desert; cold + wet = tundra.

B4.1.7

BIOMES

Biomes are groups of ecosystems with similar communities due to similar abiotic conditions and convergent evolution.

Biomes are large regions with similar communities, arising from similar abiotic conditions. Because the physical conditions are similar, species in different biomes of the same type often show convergent evolution — similar adaptations. You should know the climate conditions that characterise each biome.

B4.1.8

ADAPTATIONS TO HOT DESERTS & TROPICAL RAINFORESTS

Give examples of adaptations in named species of plants and animals in each habitat.

HOT DESERT

Adaptations

Plant (e.g. a cactus): thick waxy cuticle, reduced/spiny leaves, deep or extensive roots, water storage in succulent stem, CAM photosynthesis.
Animal (e.g. camel): stores fat in hump, tolerance to water loss, efficient kidneys, large surface area for heat loss.

RAINFOREST

Adaptations

Plant (e.g. a tall emergent tree): buttress roots, tall trunk, large leaves, dense canopy position.
Animal (e.g. a sloth): camouflage with algae in fur, slow movement, claws for gripping trees.
