C2.2 NEURAL SIGNALLING

C2.1 ran the guest list. C2.2 is the sound system: the wiring that stores charge, the drop that fires down the cable, and the handoff at the DJ booth where the record changes decks.

Act 1 · C2.2.1

THE WIRING 🔌

Two communication systems run the club: the slow mail (hormones) and the fast cable (neurons). This act is the cable.

WHO IS ON THE WIRING

The nervous system runs on neurons — about 85 billion of them. Each neuron carries a nerve impulse, which is an electrical signal.

  • Cell body — cytoplasm + nucleus. The DJ's mixer: it interprets incoming info and decides what to send.
  • Dendrites — short, branched fibres. They collect signals, e.g. between neurons in the brain or spinal cord.
  • Axon — one very elongated fibre. The speaker cable: e.g. from your toes or fingers all the way to the spinal cord.

Impulse direction: dendrites → cell body → axon → next cell. One way, always.

THE TWO SYSTEMS

Endocrine = glands → hormones → blood. Slow, lasts hours, wide broadcast.

Nervous = neurons → impulses → dedicated cables. Fast (milliseconds), targeted, then it's over.

Exam loves: "the nervous system transmits electrical impulses along neurons; the endocrine system transmits chemical signals in the blood."

ELECTRICAL, NOT ELECTRONS

Impulses are electrical but no electrons travel down the axon. The signal is moving ions — charged particles — crossing the membrane.

Electricity = energy of any charged particle, not just electrons. Say this in the exam and you sound like you own the place.

Act 2 · C2.2.2

THE BATTERY 🔋

A resting neuron is a charged battery: −70 mV, inside negative. Nothing fires until this charge is built and held.

THE STANDBY VOLTAGE

Stick microelectrodes inside and outside any living cell and you'll read a membrane potential of 10–100 mV. Cytoplasm is negative compared to the fluid outside.

A neuron at rest sits at about −70 mV — the resting potential. (For scale: a liver cell sits at about −40 mV. Neurons are just more charged.)

CHARGER 1 — THE PUMP

Sodium–potassium pumps actively pump 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in, using ATP.

The unequal 3:2 ratio creates a charge imbalance (more + leaving than entering) and concentration gradients for both ions.

CHARGER 2 — THE LEAK

Ions leak back by diffusion — slowly, but the membrane is ~50× more permeable to K⁺ than Na⁺.

So K⁺ leaks out faster than Na⁺ gets in, widening the charge imbalance. The battery holds because the leak is slow.

CHARGER 3 — THE ANIONS

Inside the fibre sit negatively charged proteins (organic anions).

They can't cross the membrane, so they permanently tip the inside negative. Third reason, don't forget it.

Pump proof: the textbook's data-based question uses ouabain, which inhibits the Na⁺/K⁺ pump. Brain tissue treated with ouabain uses less oxygen — because the pump's active transport is a huge share of the brain's ATP (and oxygen) bill. Inhibit the pump, demand for ATP crashes.
Act 3 · C2.2.3 + C2.2.8

THE DROP ⚡

The action potential is all-or-nothing: below the threshold the floor stays quiet, at the threshold the whole system fires. No half-drops.

ALL-OR-NOTHING

An action potential is a sudden spike in membrane potential with two main phases:

  • Depolarization — potential flips from negative to positive (Na⁺ floods in).
  • Repolarization — potential flips back from positive to negative (K⁺ floods out).

Both phases are caused by movement of positively charged ions across the membrane — not by movement of electrons.

The drop: stimulus pushes the membrane to threshold → Na⁺ channels open → positive feedback → spike to +30 mV → K⁺ channels open → back to −70, with a brief undershoot while the pump recharges.

THE DOOR POLICY — VOLTAGE-GATING

The Na⁺ and K⁺ channels are voltage-gated: they open and close in response to changes in membrane potential. That's the whole mechanism of the drop.

  1. Resting −70 mV: Na⁺ channels shut. The pump quietly holds the charge.
  2. Stimulus pushes to −50 mV (the threshold): some Na⁺ channels open, Na⁺ diffuses in (outside is ~10× more concentrated).
  3. Positive feedback: Na⁺ entering depolarizes further → opens more Na⁺ channels → faster entry → the membrane rockets from −50 to about +30 mV.
  4. Na⁺ channels close after only 1–2 ms — and stay shut (refractory).
  5. K⁺ channels open (also for 1–2 ms): K⁺ diffuses out down its gradient → repolarization back toward −70 mV.
  6. Ball-and-chain: a protein "ball" swings into the K⁺ channel and blocks it; the pump re-establishes the gradients over a few milliseconds.

Below threshold? No drop. The pump simply re-establishes −70 mV. That's why the action potential is all-or-nothing — the threshold must be reached for the positive feedback to start.

Exam phrasing: "Depolarization is caused by the inward diffusion of sodium ions through voltage-gated channels (positive feedback). Repolarization is caused by the outward diffusion of potassium ions." Name the ion, name the direction — that's 3 marks right there.

THE NUMBERS

Resting: −70 mV. Threshold: −50 mV. Peak: ≈ +30 mV.

Na⁺ outside ≈ 10× inside. Channels open 1–2 ms each.

Positive feedback only during depolarization. Repolarization is just K⁺ leaving — no feedback loop.

WHY ALL-OR-NOTHING

Below −50 mV: nothing. At/above −50 mV: the full spike, every time.

The strength of a signal is coded in frequency (how many drops per second), not drop size.

A bigger stimulus doesn't make a bigger action potential — it makes more of them.

Act 4 · C2.2.9

THE WAVE 🎧

The drop doesn't travel. Each patch of membrane does its own drop, triggered by the patch next to it. A relay, not a wire.

LOCAL CURRENTS

When one patch depolarizes, its sodium concentrations change: high Na⁺ inside, low Na⁺ outside.

  • Inside the axon, Na⁺ diffuses along from the depolarized patch to the still-polarized neighbour.
  • Outside, the gradient runs the opposite way — Na⁺ flows back toward the depolarized patch.
  • These ion movements are local currents. They raise the neighbour's membrane potential toward threshold.
  • Hit −50 mV → the neighbour's Na⁺ channels open → its own full action potential. The wave steps forward.

ONE-WAY STREET

The impulse only travels one direction along a neuron — two reasons:

  • The impulse can only be initiated at one terminal of the neuron and only passed on at the other.
  • After a drop, the patch is refractory (Na⁺ channels inactivated) — so the wave can't propagate backwards into the patch it just left.

The refractory period is also why there's a ceiling on firing rate: no drop, no backflow.

Act 5 · C2.2.4

THE EXPRESS LANE 🚀

Speed matters when the danger is real. Two upgrades exist: fatter cables and insulated cables.

UPGRADE 1 — FATTER CABLE

Wider fibres have lower resistance, so impulses travel faster.

  • Human nerve fibre: ~1 µm diameter → about 1 m/s.
  • Squid giant axon: up to 500 µm → 25 m/s — runs the jet-propulsion escape response.
  • Earthworms keep just three giant axons, used only for predator escape. Fat cables cost space and resources.

UPGRADE 2 — MYELIN

Myelin is a coating of Schwann cells wrapped around the fibre, with bare gaps between them called nodes of Ranvier.

The impulse jumps from node to node (saltatory conduction) instead of crawling through every patch — up to 100 m/s. One hundred times faster than an unmyelinated human fibre.

The express lane: myelin insulates, so the signal only regenerates at the bare nodes of Ranvier — it leaps between them instead of crawling the whole axon.
Exam contrast: diameter vs myelination are two different speed upgrades. Bigger diameter = less resistance (squid 25 m/s). Myelin = jumping between nodes (up to 100 m/s). Don't mix them up in a data question.
Act 6 · C2.2.5 + C2.2.6 + C2.2.7

THE HANDOFF 🎵

The axon is one DJ's cable. The synapse is the booth change: the record (neurotransmitter) is physically handed to the next deck.

THE BOOTH

A synapse is a junction between two cells. Three types exist:

  • Sensory receptor cell ↔ neuron — in sense organs.
  • Neuron ↔ neuron — in the brain and spinal cord.
  • Neuron ↔ effector — muscle fibres or gland cells (effectors "effect" the response).

Signals cross one way only. The presynaptic neuron brings the impulse in; the postsynaptic neuron carries it away. Between them: a fluid-filled gap only ~20 nm wide.

The handoff: impulse → Ca²⁺ in → vesicles fuse → ACh crosses the cleft → receptor-channel opens → Na⁺ in → EPSP. Acetylcholinesterase clears the floor.

THE RECORD DROP — STEP BY STEP

  1. The impulse reaches the end of the presynaptic neuron — the presynaptic membrane.
  2. Its depolarization opens Ca²⁺ channels; calcium diffuses into the knob.
  3. Ca²⁺ makes vesicles (packets of neurotransmitter) move to the membrane and fuse with it.
  4. Neurotransmitter is released into the gap by exocytosis.
  5. ACh diffuses across the 20–40 nm gap (fast — it's only 2–4 membrane thicknesses) and binds receptors on the postsynaptic membrane.
  6. Binding opens ion channels — either the receptor IS a channel, or it opens a separate one.
  7. Na⁺ diffuses in → the membrane potential rises (less negative) — the excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP).
  8. If the EPSP is strong enough to reach threshold, a full action potential fires and propagates away from the synapse.
  9. The neurotransmitter is rapidly broken down and removed — so one signal causes one response.

ACETYLCHOLINE — THE RECORD

Built in the presynaptic neuron from choline (from the diet) + an acetyl group (from aerobic respiration).

Used at neuromuscular junctions and many other synapses. When it binds its receptor, the channel opens and Na⁺ enters → EPSP.

THE CLEANUP CREW

Acetylcholinesterase (AChE) in the gap chops ACh into choline + acetate within a fraction of a second.

Choline is reabsorbed into the presynaptic neuron and rebuilt into fresh ACh. Fast cleanup = sharp, repeated signalling.

Exam sequence (memorize this order): impulse → Ca²⁺ enters → vesicles fuse → exocytosis → diffusion across cleft → receptor binding → ion channels open → Na⁺ in → EPSP → threshold → action potential → enzyme cleanup.

SELF-TEST ✍

Click an answer. Instant feedback. No mercy.

1. A neuron at rest sits at about −70 mV. Which is NOT a reason the inside stays negative?

✅ Correct — the pump moves Na⁺ OUT (3:2 ratio), plus K⁺ leak and organic anions keep the inside negative.
❌ Na⁺ is pumped OUT, not in. Resting negative = pump (3 out / 2 in) + fast K⁺ leak + organic anions.

2. What exactly happens during depolarization?

✅ Na⁺ in → positive feedback → rapid climb from −50 to about +30 mV. No electrons involved.
❌ Depolarization = Na⁺ IN. K⁺ out is repolarization. And impulses run on ions, never electrons.

3. Why is the action potential described as all-or-nothing?

✅ Threshold gates everything. Signal strength is coded in frequency, not spike size.
❌ All-or-nothing = threshold gate. Bigger stimulus = more spikes, never a bigger spike.

4. During depolarization, Na⁺ entry opens more Na⁺ channels, which lets in more Na⁺. This is:

✅ Positive feedback, and it's the engine of the rapid spike. (Repolarization has no such loop.)
❌ Positive feedback. Each bit of Na⁺ entry recruits more Na⁺ channels — a snowball, not a thermostat.

5. Why can't an action potential travel backwards along an axon?

✅ The refractory period = one-way valve. Also why there's a max firing rate.
❌ The refractory period. After a drop, Na⁺ channels are inactivated, so the wave can only move into fresh membrane.

6. A squid giant axon (500 µm) conducts at ~25 m/s while a human fibre (~1 µm) does ~1 m/s. Why the difference?

✅ Diameter. And it's expensive — that's why earthworms keep only three giant axons, for escape.
❌ Diameter = resistance. (Myelin is the OTHER speed upgrade, and human fibres have it too.)

7. In a myelinated fibre, where does the action potential actually regenerate?

✅ Nodes only — that's what makes conduction saltatory (jumping), up to 100 m/s.
❌ Nodes of Ranvier. The impulse jumps gap to gap instead of regenerating every patch.

8. What role does calcium play at the synapse?

✅ Ca²⁺ is the trigger for release. The neurotransmitter (ACh) crosses the cleft, not calcium.
❌ Ca²⁺ in → vesicle fusion → exocytosis. The neurotransmitter itself crosses the gap.

9. After ACh binds its receptor and the EPSP fires, how does the signal stop?

✅ Enzymatic breakdown, then recycling. One record = one drop.
❌ Acetylcholinesterase chops ACh into choline + acetate. Choline gets reabsorbed and rebuilt into new ACh.

10. What makes an EPSP strong enough to trigger an action potential?

✅ Threshold again. Sub-threshold EPSPs just fade; the pump restores the rest.
❌ The EPSP must push the membrane to threshold. Below it, no action potential.
