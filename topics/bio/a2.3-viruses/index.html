A2.3.1

STRUCTURAL FEATURES COMMON TO VIRUSES

All viruses share a few features: small fixed size, nucleic acid as genetic material, a protein capsid, no cytoplasm, and few or no enzymes.

  • Small, fixed size (measured in nanometres).
  • Nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) as the genetic material.
  • A capsid made of protein (the outer shell).
  • No cytoplasm.
  • Few or no enzymes.
FIG 1 — A virus: a protein capsid enclosing a nucleic acid core, with no cytoplasm and practically no enzymes.
A2.3.2

DIVERSITY OF STRUCTURE IN VIRUSES

Viruses are highly diverse in shape and structure. Their genetic material can be RNA or DNA, single- or double-stranded; some also have an envelope.

  • Genetic material: DNA or RNA, and each can be single- or double-stranded.
  • Shape: varies widely (icosahedral, helical, complex).
  • Many viruses have an extra outer envelope (a lipid membrane with proteins) around the capsid.
A2.3.3

THE LYTIC CYCLE

Viruses rely on a host cell for energy, nutrition, protein synthesis and other life functions. In the lytic cycle the virus replicates and destroys the host.

Using bacteriophage lambda as an example, the lytic cycle has these phases: attachment → injection of the viral nucleic acid → replication and protein synthesis using host machinery → assembly of new phages → lysis (the host cell bursts), releasing new viruses.

FIG 2 — The lytic cycle: the phage attaches, injects its nucleic acid, hijacks the host to make new phages, then lyses the cell to release them.
A2.3.4

THE LYSOGENIC CYCLE

In the lysogenic cycle the viral genome integrates into the host DNA and is copied with it, without immediately destroying the host.

Again using bacteriophage lambda: after injection the viral DNA can instead integrate into the host chromosome (becoming a prophage). It is then replicated and passed on every time the host divides — no lysis occurs. Later it may switch to the lytic cycle and destroy the host.

A2.3.5

EVIDENCE FOR SEVERAL ORIGINS OF VIRUSES

The diversity of viruses suggests several possible origins from other organisms.

Viruses share an extreme form of obligate parasitism — they can only survive inside a host. Their great diversity (different genetic material, shapes, hosts) suggests that viruses have arisen several times, from genetic material of other organisms that became able to replicate independently and be transmitted.

A2.3.6

RAPID EVOLUTION IN VIRUSES

Some viruses evolve very rapidly, with important consequences for treatment.

  • Why so fast: very short generation times, huge numbers, high mutation rates (especially RNA viruses), and strong selection (from immune systems, drugs, vaccines).
INFLUENZA

Antigenic drift & shift

Rapid mutation changes surface proteins (haemagglutinin/neuraminidase), so the immune system no longer recognises it → new strains each season, needing new vaccines.

HIV

High mutation + resistance

Reverse transcriptase makes copying errors frequently → rapid mutation → resistance to drugs and immune evasion, so treatment must use combinations.

Consequence for treatment: because viruses mutate fast, a single drug or vaccine can quickly stop working — that is why we need combination therapy and updated vaccines.
