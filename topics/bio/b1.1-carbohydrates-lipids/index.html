B1.1.1

THE CHEMISTRY OF CARBON

Carbon's chemical properties allow it to form the huge diversity of compounds on which life is based.

Carbon forms up to four covalent bonds (sharing electrons), allowing single bonds and combinations of single and double bonds with carbon and other non-metals. This lets it form chains, branched and ring structures — giving an enormous diversity of molecules.

B1.1.2

CONDENSATION & MACROMOLECULES

Macromolecules are polymers made by condensation reactions that link monomers. Examples: polysaccharides, polypeptides, nucleic acids.

A condensation reaction joins two monomers, removing a water molecule as a by-product, forming a covalent bond. Repeating this links monomers into long polymers — polysaccharides, polypeptides and nucleic acids.

B1.1.3

HYDROLYSIS

Hydrolysis breaks polymers into monomers by splitting water — the reverse of condensation.

In hydrolysis, a water molecule is split to provide the −H and −OH groups that are added to the monomers being released. This is how polymers are digested into monomers.

B1.1.4

MONOSACCHARIDES

Recognise pentoses and hexoses as monosaccharides (ring forms). Glucose links its properties to its function.

  • Pentoses — 5-carbon sugars (e.g. ribose).
  • Hexoses — 6-carbon sugars (e.g. glucose, galactose, fructose).
  • Glucose is soluble (transported in blood), chemically stable, and yields energy on oxidation — making it an ideal fuel.
B1.1.5

POLYSACCHARIDES AS ENERGY STORAGE

Starch (plants) and glycogen (animals) are compact, largely insoluble energy stores.

  • Starch (plants) and glycogen (animals) store energy.
  • Both are compact because of coiling and branching during polymerisation.
  • Both are largely insoluble due to their large molecular size (so they don't disturb osmotic balance).
  • α-glucose monomers are added/removed by condensation/hydrolysis to build or mobilise energy stores.
B1.1.6

CELLULOSE — STRUCTURAL POLYSACCHARIDE

Cellulose's alternating β-glucose gives straight chains that form strong, insoluble structures.

Cellulose is made of β-glucose in alternating orientation, giving straight chains that can be grouped in bundles and cross-linked with hydrogen bonds. This makes cellulose strong and insoluble — ideal for plant cell walls.

FIG 1 — Cellulose: alternating β-glucose gives straight chains that form strong, hydrogen-bonded bundles.
B1.1.7

GLYCOPROTEINS & CELL-CELL RECOGNITION

Glycoproteins (proteins with attached carbohydrates) are involved in cell-cell recognition — ABO blood antigens are an example.

Glycoproteins (proteins with carbohydrate chains) on cell surfaces allow cells to recognise each other. The ABO blood group antigens are a classic example — the type of antigen you have determines your blood type (A, B, AB or O).

B1.1.8

HYDROPHOBIC PROPERTIES OF LIPIDS

Lipids dissolve in non-polar solvents but are only sparingly soluble in water. They include fats, oils, waxes and steroids.

Lipids dissolve in non-polar solvents but are only sparingly soluble in water. They include fats, oils, waxes and steroids. This hydrophobicity is why lipids are used as membranes, energy stores and insulation.

B1.1.9

TRIGLYCERIDES & PHOSPHOLIPIDS

One glycerol can link three fatty acids (triglyceride) or two fatty acids + a phosphate group (phospholipid), by condensation.

  • Triglyceride = 1 glycerol + 3 fatty acids.
  • Phospholipid = 1 glycerol + 2 fatty acids + 1 phosphate group.
  • Both are formed by condensation reactions.
  • Phospholipids are amphipathic (hydrophilic head + hydrophobic tails).
B1.1.10

B1.1.11

TRIGLYCERIDES IN ADIPOSE TISSUE

Triglycerides stored in adipose tissue are used for energy storage and thermal insulation.

Adipose tissue stores triglycerides. Functions: long-term energy storage (high energy per gram) and thermal insulation (reducing heat loss in endotherms).

B1.1.12

PHOSPHOLIPID BILAYERS

Phospholipid bilayers form because of the hydrophobic and hydrophilic regions — the molecules are amphipathic.

Phospholipids are amphipathic: they have a hydrophilic (water-loving) head and hydrophobic (water-hating) tails. In water the heads face out and the tails face in, forming a bilayer — the basis of all cell membranes.

FIG 2 — A phospholipid bilayer: hydrophilic heads face the water on both sides; the hydrophobic tails point inward.
B1.1.13

STEROIDS PASS THROUGH THE BILAYER

Non-polar steroids can pass through the phospholipid bilayer. Oestradiol and testosterone are examples.

Because steroids are non-polar, they are hydrophobic and can pass directly through the phospholipid bilayer without needing a transport protein. Oestradiol and testosterone are examples of steroid hormones that do this.

Exam point: steroids = identify from molecular diagrams (four fused carbon rings) and note that their non-polar nature lets them cross the membrane (unlike charged molecules).
