D2.2.1

GENE EXPRESSION

Gene expression is the mechanism by which information in genes has effects on the phenotype.

Gene expression = the process by which the information in a gene affects the phenotype. The common stages are transcription (gene → mRNA) and translation (mRNA → protein).

D2.2.2

REGULATING TRANSCRIPTION

Proteins that bind to specific DNA sequences regulate transcription — promoters, enhancers and transcription factors.

  • Promoters — DNA sequences where transcription starts.
  • Enhancers — sequences that increase transcription.
  • Transcription factors — proteins that bind to these specific base sequences and switch genes on or off.
D2.2.3

CONTROL OF mRNA DEGRADATION

Regulating how long mRNA lasts controls how much protein is made.

In human cells, mRNA may persist from minutes to days before being broken down. Controlling the rate of mRNA degradation is one way to regulate translation — the longer mRNA lasts, the more protein is made.

D2.2.4

EPIGENESIS

Epigenesis is the development of patterns of differentiation in a multicellular organism — DNA base sequences are NOT altered.

Epigenesis = the development of patterns of differentiation in the cells of a multicellular organism. Crucially, DNA base sequences are not altered by epigenetic changes — only gene expression changes.

D2.2.5

GENOME, TRANSCRIPTOME & PROTEOME

No cell expresses all its genes; the pattern of expression determines the cell.

  • Genome — all the DNA (genes) in a cell (same in all cells).
  • Transcriptome — all the mRNA a cell makes (varies by cell type).
  • Proteome — all the proteins a cell makes (varies).
D2.2.6

METHYLATION & HISTONES — EPIGENETIC TAGS

Methylation of the promoter and histones in nucleosomes are examples of epigenetic tags.

  • Methylation of cytosine in the promoter — represses transcription.
  • Histone modification in nucleosomes — alters how tightly DNA is wound, affecting transcription.
D2.2.7

EPIGENETIC INHERITANCE

Epigenetic changes to gene expression can be inherited.

Epigenetic inheritance = phenotypic changes from altered gene expression can be passed on to daughter cells (and sometimes to offspring) — without changes in the DNA base sequence.

D2.2.8

ENVIRONMENTAL EFFECTS ON GENE EXPRESSION

The environment can alter gene expression — e.g. air pollution changing methyl tags on DNA.

Environmental factors can alter epigenetic tags. For example, air pollution can alter methyl tags on DNA, changing gene expression.

D2.2.9

EPIGENETIC TAGS IN OVUM & SPERM

Most, but not all, epigenetic tags are removed from the ovum and sperm (and then re-set) — some persist.

After fertilisation, most but not all epigenetic tags are removed from the ovum and sperm, and then largely re-set. Any tags that persist can be inherited — a possible epigenetic origin of phenotypic differences between individuals.

D2.2.10

MONOZYGOTIC TWIN STUDIES

Identical (monozygotic) twins are used to study environmental effects on gene expression.

Monozygotic twins share the same genome, so differences between them (phenotype or gene expression) are due to environment. Over time, their epigenetic patterns can diverge — evidence of environmental effects on gene expression.

D2.2.11

EXTERNAL FACTORS AFFECTING GENE EXPRESSION

Hormones and biochemicals (e.g. lactose or tryptophan in bacteria) affect gene expression.

  • Hormone — e.g. a hormone binding a receptor can switch genes on/off.
  • Biochemical in bacteria — e.g. lactose or tryptophan controlling the expression of bacterial genes (operon model).
FIG 1 — The lac operon: a repressor blocks transcription; when lactose is present it binds the repressor, switching the genes on.
