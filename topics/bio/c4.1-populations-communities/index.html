C4.1.1

POPULATIONS

A population is an interacting group of organisms of the same species living in an area; members normally breed.

A population is a group of organisms of the same species living in an area. Its members normally breed with each other, and different populations of the same species are reproductively isolated from each other.

C4.1.2

ESTIMATING POPULATION SIZE BY RANDOM SAMPLING

We estimate rather than count every individual, and sampling must be random.

It's usually impractical to count every individual, so we estimate. Sampling must be random (not biased) to give a representative estimate of the whole population.

C4.1.3

RANDOM QUADRAT SAMPLING

Use random quadrat sampling to estimate population size for sessile (non-moving) organisms.

For sessile organisms (plants and sessile animals where individuals can be counted), place random quadrats (square frames) and count individuals inside. Estimate = mean per quadrat × total area ÷ quadrat area.

C4.1.4

CAPTURE–MARK–RELEASE–RECAPTURE & THE LINCOLN INDEX

Use the Lincoln index to estimate population size of motile organisms.

For motile organisms: capture and mark a sample, release them, then recapture a second sample. Lincoln index: Population = (M × N) ÷ R, where M = marked in first sample, N = total in second sample, R = recaptured (marked) in second sample.

C4.1.5

CARRYING CAPACITY & COMPETITION

Carrying capacity is the maximum population size the environment can sustain; competition for limited resources limits it.

The carrying capacity is the largest population size that can be sustained by available resources (food, water, shelter, space). Competition for these limited resources limits population size.

C4.1.6

NEGATIVE FEEDBACK & DENSITY-DEPENDENT FACTORS

Density-dependent factors regulate population size by negative feedback.

Populations may fluctuate due to density-independent factors (e.g. weather, fire — affect regardless of density), but density-dependent factors (e.g. competition, disease, predation) have an effect that increases as density rises — a negative feedback that stabilises population size around the carrying capacity.

C4.1.7

POPULATION GROWTH CURVES

Population growth has a lag phase, then exponential growth, then slows at carrying capacity.

  • Lag phase — slow initial growth (few individuals, adapting).
  • Exponential (log) phase — rapid growth because resources are abundant; each generation adds more.
  • Growth then slows as resources become limiting, approaching the carrying capacity.
C4.1.8

MODELLING THE SIGMOID GROWTH CURVE

Collect data (e.g. yeast or duckweed) and model the sigmoid (S-shaped) growth curve.

Population growth typically follows an S-shaped (sigmoid) curve: slow start → exponential rise → levelling off at the carrying capacity. You can collect data on yeast or duckweed and plot this curve.

C4.1.9

COMPETITION vs COOPERATION (INTRASPECIFIC)

Members of the same species compete for limited resources but may also cooperate.

  • Intraspecific competition — within a species, for limited resources (food, mates, territory).
  • Cooperation — e.g. hunting together (wolves), social insects, or sharing parental care.
C4.1.10

A COMMUNITY

A community is all the interacting organisms in an ecosystem — all the populations.

A community comprises all the populations in an area, including plants, animals, fungi and bacteria, and their interactions.

C4.1.11

INTERSPECIFIC RELATIONSHIPS

Herbivory, predation, interspecific competition, mutualism, parasitism and pathogenicity are categories of interspecific relationship.

TypeEffect
Herbivoryan animal eats plants (one +, one −)
Predationone kills and eats the other (+/−)
Interspecific competitiondifferent species compete for a resource (−/−)
Mutualismboth benefit (+/+)
Parasitismone benefits, the host is harmed (+/−)
Pathogenicitya pathogen causes disease (+/−)
C4.1.12

MUTUALISM

Mutualism benefits both species. Examples: root nodules in Fabaceae, mycorrhizae in Orchidaceae.

  • Root nodules in Fabaceae (legumes) — nitrogen-fixing bacteria live in nodules, providing usable nitrogen; the plant supplies carbohydrates.
  • Mycorrhizae in Orchidaceae (orchids) — fungal partner aids water/mineral uptake; the plant supplies sugars.
C4.1.13

RESOURCE COMPETITION — ENDEMIC vs INVASIVE

An invasive species can have a competitive advantage over an endemic (native) species for resources.

An invasive species can outcompete an endemic (native) species in acquiring resources. This competitive advantage (e.g. faster growth, no natural predators, better resource use) underpins why invasive species reduce biodiversity.

C4.1.14

TESTS FOR INTERSPECIFIC COMPETITION

Competition is indicated (not proven) if one species is more successful in the absence of the other.

Interspecific competition is indicated (not proven) if a species is more successful when the other is absent. Removing one and seeing if the other thrives suggests competition, but doesn't prove the mechanism.

C4.1.15

THE CHI-SQUARED TEST FOR ASSOCIATION

Use a chi-squared test on presence/absence data to test for an association between two species.

The chi-squared test compares observed counts of two species' presence/absence (in quadrats) with expected counts. If χ² exceeds the critical value, the two species are significantly associated (positive = occur together; negative = avoid each other).

C4.1.16

PREDATOR–PREY RELATIONSHIPS

Predator-prey relationships are a density-dependent control of animal populations — study a real case.

In a classic predator-prey cycle (e.g. lynx and snowshoe hare), the prey population rises, then the predator population rises (more food), which crashes the prey, then the predator crashes — an oscillating, density-dependent negative feedback.

C4.1.17

TOP-DOWN & BOTTOM-UP CONTROL

Populations in communities can be controlled from the top (predators) or the bottom (resources); one is usually dominant.

  • Top-down control — higher trophic levels (predators) control lower ones.
  • Bottom-up control — resources at the base (producers/nutrients) control higher levels.
  • Both are possible, but one is likely to be dominant in a given community.
C4.1.18

ALLELOPATHY & ANTIBIOTIC SECRETION

Allelopathy and antibiotic secretion both release a chemical into the environment to deter competitors.

Allelopathy — a plant releases chemicals that inhibit the growth of competing plants. Antibiotic secretion — organisms (e.g. some fungi/bacteria) release antibiotics that kill competitors in the environment. Both are chemical interference/competition.
