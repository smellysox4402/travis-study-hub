B2.1 MEMBRANES & MEMBRANE TRANSPORT

B2.1.1

LIPID BILAYERS — THE BASIS OF CELL MEMBRANES

Phospholipids and other amphipathic lipids naturally form continuous sheet-like bilayers in water.

Because phospholipids are amphipathic (hydrophilic head + hydrophobic tails), in water they spontaneously arrange into a bilayer — the basis of all cell membranes.

B2.1.2

LIPID BILAYERS AS BARRIERS

The hydrophobic core of the membrane has low permeability to large molecules and hydrophilic (charged) particles.

The hydrophobic hydrocarbon chains in the membrane core resist water and charged/ionic particles, so the bilayer is impermeable to large molecules and ions, and only small non-polar molecules pass freely.

B2.1.3

SIMPLE DIFFUSION

Oxygen and CO₂ move directly between phospholipids by simple diffusion.

Because O₂ and CO₂ are small and non-polar, they diffuse straight through the phospholipid bilayer down their concentration gradient — no protein or energy needed.

B2.1.4

INTEGRAL & PERIPHERAL PROTEINS

Membrane proteins have diverse structures, locations and functions; integral ones are embedded, peripheral ones are on the surface.

  • Integral proteins — embedded in one or both of the bilayer.
  • Peripheral proteins — attached to the surface (not embedded).
  • Functions: transport (channels/pumps), receptors, enzymes, cell recognition (glycoproteins).
B2.1.5

OSMOSIS & AQUAPORINS

Water moves across membranes by osmosis (random movement) through the bilayer and aquaporins.

Osmosis is the movement of water across a partially permeable membrane from high to low water potential, due to random particle movement. Because membranes are impermeable to solutes but permeable to water, water moves to balance concentrations. Aquaporins are channel proteins that greatly speed up water transport.

B2.1.6

CHANNEL PROTEINS — FACILITATED DIFFUSION

Channel proteins make membranes selectively permeable by letting specific ions diffuse through.

Channel proteins form a pore through the membrane, allowing specific ions to diffuse down their gradient (facilitated diffusion) — no ATP needed. This makes the membrane selectively permeable.

B2.1.7

PUMP PROTEINS — ACTIVE TRANSPORT

Pumps use energy from ATP to transfer specific particles across membranes against their gradient.

Pump proteins use ATP to move specific ions or molecules against their concentration gradient (active transport). This is how cells maintain ion gradients across membranes.

B2.1.8

SELECTIVITY IN MEMBRANE PERMEABILITY

Facilitated diffusion and active transport are selective; simple diffusion is not selective, only acting per the gradient.

The membrane is selectively permeable because channel and pump proteins let only specific substances through (selective). Simple diffusion is not selective — anything small and non-polar moves by gradient.

B2.1.9

GLYCOPROTEINS & GLYCOLIPIDS

Carbohydrate chains attached to proteins (glycoproteins) or lipids (glycolipids) sit on the extracellular surface, involved in recognition.

The carbohydrate structures of glycoproteins and glycolipids are located on the extracellular surface of the membrane. They are used in cell-cell recognition (e.g. ABO blood antigens).

B2.1.10

THE FLUID MOSAIC MODEL

You should be able to draw a 2D representation of the fluid-mosaic model, including phospholipids, integral & peripheral proteins, glycoproteins and cholesterol.

The membrane is a fluid mosaic — a fluid bilayer (lipids can move) with many mosaic proteins embedded. You should be able to draw the bilayer with: phospholipids, integral and peripheral proteins, glycoproteins, glycolipids and cholesterol.

AHL · B2.1.11

FATTY ACID COMPOSITION & FLUIDITY

Unsaturated fatty acids have lower melting points, so membranes with more of them are more fluid.

Unsaturated fatty acids (with C=C double bonds) have lower melting points and make the bilayer more fluid; saturated fatty acids pack more tightly and make it less fluid. So the fatty-acid composition controls membrane fluidity.

AHL · B2.1.12

CHOLESTEROL & MEMBRANE FLUIDITY

Cholesterol sits in the membrane and acts as a modulator of fluidity in animal cells.

Cholesterol is found between the phospholipids in animal membranes. It acts as a modulator of fluidity — at high temperatures it reduces fluidity (stiffens), and at low temperatures it prevents the membrane from becoming too rigid. So it keeps the membrane in the right fluid state.

AHL · B2.1.13

MEMBRANE FLUIDITY, ENDOCYTOSIS & EXOCYTOSIS

Membrane fluidity allows vesicles to form and fuse — endocytosis and exocytosis.

  • Exocytosis — a vesicle fuses with the membrane, releasing contents (e.g. secretion of proteins, hormones).
  • Endocytosis — the membrane folds in to engulf material (e.g. phagocytosis of particles, pinocytosis of fluid).
AHL · B2.1.14

GATED ION CHANNELS IN NEURONS

Some ion channels open/close (gated) in response to a stimulus; e.g. neurotransmitter-gated nicotinic ACh receptors and voltage-gated Na⁺/K⁺ channels.

  • Nicotinic acetylcholine receptors — a neurotransmitter-gated ion channel (opens when ACh binds).
  • Sodium and potassium channels — voltage-gated, open in response to a change in membrane potential (used in action potentials).
AHL · B2.1.15

SODIUM-POTASSIUM PUMPS

The Na⁺/K⁺ pump is an exchange transporter using ATP; it generates membrane potentials.

The Na⁺/K⁺ pump uses ATP to move 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in per cycle. It is an exchange transporter and is important for generating and maintaining membrane potentials (e.g. the resting potential of neurons).

AHL · B2.1.16

SODIUM-DEPENDENT GLUCOSE COTRANSPORT

The Na⁺-glucose cotransporter is an example of indirect active transport, used to absorb glucose in the small intestine.

In sodium-dependent glucose cotransporters, Na⁺ moves down its gradient (set up by the Na⁺/K⁺ pump) and "pulls" glucose against its gradient at the same time. This is indirect active transport — ATP is used indirectly (to maintain the Na⁺ gradient). It's how glucose is absorbed by the small intestine.

AHL · B2.1.17

CELL ADHESION

Cells adhere to form tissues using cell-adhesion molecules (CAMs); different CAMs are used for different types of adhesion.

Cell-adhesion molecules (CAMs) on cell surfaces let cells stick together to form tissues. Different forms of CAM are used for different types of cell-cell adhesion, so cells recognise and bind to the correct partners.
