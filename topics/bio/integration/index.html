IB Biology HL · C3.1 Integration of Body Systems (SL + AHL)

How the brain, nervous system, endocrine system and blood co-ordinate the body's responses and homeostasis across body systems.

SL + AHL 23 statements Brain · Nerves · Hormones Blood · Phytohormones
C3.1.1 – C3.1.2

THE INTEGRATION HIERARCHY

System integration = subsystems working together so the whole organism performs its overall function — and the body is built as a hierarchy of smaller subsystems inside bigger ones.

🎛️

One body, many systems, one coordinating centre

system integration · subsystems · hierarchy · emergent properties

System integration is the coordination of the component parts of a body so they work together to perform an overall function. A cheetah is only a lethal hunter because its skeletal, muscular, respiratory and circulatory systems all fire in sequence — study any single system alone and you can't predict the sprint. That's an emergent property: the whole does something no part can do.

Integration is hierarchical: organelles are components of cells, cells of tissues, tissues of organs, organs of organ systems, and organ systems of the whole organism. Failure of even one small group of cells can kill the organism — kidney failure can kill the whole organism, not just the kidney. In humans, eleven organ systems are integrated this way.

FIG 1 — The hierarchy ladder: organelles → cells → tissues → organs → organ systems → organism. Integration means each level's parts cooperate so the level above can do its overall job — and a single broken component can bring the whole system down.
C3.1.1

System integration 🎛️

Component parts of the body are coordinated so the whole performs an overall function. Example: cheetah hunting needs skeleton + muscle + respiration + circulation working as one.

C3.1.2

Hierarchy of subsystems 🪜

Organelles → cells → tissues → organs → organ systems → organism. Tissues can contain more than one cell type (e.g. alveolar type 1 + type 2 cells in the lungs).

FRAGILITY

Weakest link 💥

Because subsystems depend on each other, failure of a single group of cells (kidney failure, heart failure) threatens the whole organism — no system is an island.

Exam gold: "integration" questions want a specific example of coordination between systems — e.g. during a sprint, the nervous system signals muscles (Act 5), the heart speeds up (Act 12), ventilation rises (Act 12) and epinephrine redirects blood (Act 10). Name at least two systems working together, not just one organ.
C3.1.3

THE THREE CHANNELS — NERVE & HORMONE

The body communicates through three channels: nerves (fast electrical signals), hormones (chemical signals in the blood) and blood (transport). Each has its own speed, reach and job.

🎙️

Nerves, hormones and blood

nervous signalling · hormonal signalling · blood transport · effectors

Nervous signalling is the wired mic: electrical impulses travel along neurons directly to one specific target — a muscle or gland — in milliseconds. Hormonal signalling is the radio broadcast: chemical messengers travel in the bloodstream and reach every cell, but only cells with the matching receptor (tuned-in listeners) respond. It's slower — seconds to minutes — but lasts longer and reaches everywhere.

The third channel is blood transport itself: the roadie crew. Blood carries materials (glucose, oxygen, amino acids) and energy between organs, and removes carbon dioxide and other wastes. Production-line systems like the digestive, gas-exchange, urinary and reproductive systems all plug into the blood to deliver or collect goods. Every system in the body is connected to all three channels at once.

FIG 2 — Three channels, three speeds. Nervous: wired, millisecond, one target. Hormonal: broadcast via blood, slower, but any cell with the receptor can respond. Blood: the physical transport system that moves materials, energy and the hormones themselves.

Exam gold: the classic comparison question asks "explain why hormonal responses are slower but longer-lasting than nervous ones." Answer: hormones must diffuse into the blood, circulate to target cells and bind receptors (slow), but they persist until metabolised (long). Nervous impulses are instant and stop when the signal stops.
C3.1.4

THE BRAIN & NERVOUS SYSTEM

The brain is the central integration organ — every sensory input lands on the desk, gets processed, some of it is stored as memory, and instructions go back out to muscles and glands.

🧠

All inputs in, all instructions out

sensory receptors · cerebrum · memory · learning · decision making

The brain receives information from sensory receptors — not just the sense organs in the head (eyes, ears, nose, tongue, skin) but receptors buried inside other organs too, like pressure receptors in blood vessel walls. The brain processes this information, stores some of it as memory, and sends instructions to muscles and glands.

Learning is essential for memory — you can't store what you never learned. The cerebrum, the folded upper part of the brain, is where memory and learning live. Processing the input → deciding → signalling effectors: that's the brain deciding which response to make, based on what the receptors are reporting.

FIG 3 — The brain: information flows in from external sense organs AND internal receptors (e.g. pressure receptors in blood vessel walls), the brain integrates it, stores what matters, and sends instructions to muscles and glands. Learning is the door into memory.
Remember: "central integration" means the brain combines many inputs at once — it's not a reflex relay, it's a decision desk. And memory requires learning first: no learning, nothing to store.
C3.1.5

THE ENDOCRINE GLANDS

The spinal cord is the back office that handles unconscious business — reflexes and automatic jobs — so the brain does not have to think about everything.

🗄️

Conscious vs unconscious control

CNS · grey matter · white matter · reflex · voluntary vs involuntary

The central nervous system (CNS) = brain + spinal cord. The spinal cord sits in the vertebral column and connects to the body via 31 pairs of spinal nerves. In cross-section: white matter outside (myelinated axons carrying signals to and from the brain) and grey matter inside (cell bodies of motor neurons + interneurons, packed with synapses).

The spinal cord coordinates unconscious processes — especially reflexes — which are faster because the signal never travels up to the brain and back. Conscious control happens only when you're awake, is voluntary, uses striated (skeletal) muscle, and is coordinated by the cerebral hemispheres. Unconscious control runs awake or asleep, is involuntary, and drives gland secretion + smooth muscle contraction. Swallowing starts consciously (tongue push) but becomes unconscious once food hits the pharynx; vomiting is unconscious (abdominal muscles, not gut-wall muscle).

FIG 4 — The back office. The spinal cord's grey matter (motor neuron cell bodies + interneurons + synapses) coordinates unconscious reflexes; white matter carries signals to and from the brain. Conscious control = awake, voluntary, striated muscle, cerebral hemispheres. Unconscious = involuntary, glands + smooth muscle, works asleep.
CONSCIOUS

Front-of-house control 🎤

Only when awake · voluntary · striated (skeletal) muscle · coordinated by the cerebral hemispheres. Examples: standing up, choosing to speak, deliberate swallowing start.

UNCONSCIOUS

Back-office control 🗄️

Awake or asleep · involuntary · gland secretion + smooth muscle contraction · reflexes coordinated by the spinal cord. Examples: pain reflex (Act 7), heart rate (Act 12).

Exam gold: swallowing is the classic boundary case — it begins voluntarily (tongue pushes food back, striated muscle) then becomes unconscious once touch receptors in the pharynx fire and the brainstem takes over. Vomiting uses abdominal muscles, not gut-wall muscle — the gut's own muscle would push the wrong way.
C3.1.6 – C3.1.7

SENSORY INPUT & MOTOR OUTPUT

Sensory neurons carry input from receptors into the CNS; motor neurons carry output from the cerebral hemispheres to muscles (the speakers). One wire in, one wire out.

🔌

Sensory in · motor out

sensory neuron · afferent · motor neuron · efferent · receptor · effector · cerebral hemispheres

Sensory neurons (afferent = "carrying in") convey impulses from receptor cells or nerve endings to the CNS. Receptors come in all flavours: nerve endings in the skin for touch and heat, specialised receptor cells like the rods and cones of the retina, plus internal monitors — stretch receptors in muscles, baroreceptors in artery walls, chemoreceptors in blood vessel walls. A sensory axon can be nearly a metre long (big toe → spinal cord).

Motor neurons (efferent = "carrying out") convey impulses from the CNS to muscles. The primary motor cortex in the cerebral hemispheres starts the signal; it travels down to a motor neuron whose cell body and dendrites sit in the grey matter of the spinal cord, and whose axon synapses with a second motor neuron that reaches one specific muscle. The impulse makes muscle fibres contract — or gland cells secrete. Voluntary moves like standing up run this whole wire.

FIG 5 — One wire in, one wire out. Sensory neurons carry receptor input to the CNS; the motor cortex in the cerebral hemispheres sends output through motor neurons to specific skeletal muscles (or glands). Sensory axons can stretch nearly a metre from toe to spinal cord.
SENSORY · C3.1.6

Input wire 🎙️

Receptors: skin nerve endings (touch, heat) · specialised cells (rods/cones) · internal: stretch receptors in muscles, baroreceptors in artery walls, chemoreceptors in vessel walls. 12 cranial + 31 spinal nerve pairs.

MOTOR · C3.1.7

Output wire 🔊

Primary motor cortex (cerebral hemispheres) → motor neuron in spinal grey matter → second motor neuron → one specific muscle. Impulse = contraction, or secretion from a gland.

Exam gold: learn the direction words — sensory neurons are afferent (arriving), motor neurons are efferent (exiting). And motor control is a two-neuron relay: the signal crosses a synapse in the spinal cord grey matter before reaching the muscle — that's the synapse an exam diagram will ask you to label.
C3.1.8

THE SPINAL CORD & REFLEXES

Nerves are bundles of nerve fibres wrapped in a protective sheath — the nerve bundles. Some fibres are insulated (myelinated) for speed, some run bare.

🧵

Fibres, sheaths and insulation

nerve fibre · protective sheath · myelinated · unmyelinated · myelin sheath · axon

A nerve is a bundle of nerve fibres (axons) enclosed in a protective sheath. Sizes vary wildly: the sciatic nerve in the thigh is about 20 mm wide; the optic nerve packs 770,000 to 1.7 million fibres; small nerves have fewer than 100. Most nerves carry both sensory and motor fibres — but the optic nerve is purely sensory and the oculomotor nerve purely motor.

In a transverse section you can see the difference: myelinated fibres show a dark ring of myelin sheath around the axon (an insulator that speeds up impulses), while unmyelinated fibres have no sheath and conduct more slowly. The myelin comes from Schwann cells wrapping the axon.

FIG 6 — A nerve in cross-section: protective sheath outside, mixed fibres inside. Myelinated fibres show a dark myelin ring and conduct fast; unmyelinated fibres run bare and slower. Most nerves mix sensory and motor fibres; a few are single-purpose.
Data question skill: examiners love a photomicrograph of a nerve section with a scale bar. Read the magnification off the scale bar, count or measure the fibres, and use the ring pattern to distinguish myelinated (ring visible) from unmyelinated (no ring).
C3.1.9

THE PAIN PATHWAY

The pain reflex is the fastest pathway: three neurons and one synapse in the spinal cord, and the foot is off the drawing pin before the brain even hears about it.

⚡

The reflex arc — minimum crew, maximum speed

reflex arc · free sensory nerve ending · interneuron · grey matter · motor neuron · effector

A reflex is a rapid, involuntary response to a stimulus, wired with the fewest possible neurons. Pain needs no special receptor cell — a free sensory nerve ending in the skin does the job. The pain reflex arc: free nerve ending → sensory neuron → single interneuron (relay) in the grey matter of the spinal cord → motor neuron → skeletal muscle (the effector) contracts and the foot lifts off the sharp object.

The brain is not part of the circuit — that's why it's fast. You feel the pain after the foot has already moved. Some reflexes do involve the brain: pupil constriction in bright light is coordinated by the brain, not the cord. The whole sequence of cells is the reflex arc.

FIG 7 — The pain express. A free sensory nerve ending detects the pin; the sensory neuron enters the spinal cord grey matter, a single interneuron relays to a motor neuron, and skeletal muscle lifts the foot. Only three cells — the brain hears about it afterwards.
1

Receptor 🎯

Free sensory nerve ending in the skin — no separate receptor cell needed for pain or heat.

2

Relay 🧩

Single interneuron in the grey matter of the spinal cord passes the signal straight across.

3

Effector 💪

Motor neuron → skeletal muscle contracts → limb withdraws. Reflex arc complete.

Exam gold: "why is a reflex faster than a conscious response?" — the impulse only travels sensory → interneuron → motor in the spinal cord; a conscious response would need the extra journey up to the brain and back. Also know one counter-example: pupil constriction is a reflex coordinated by the brain, so not all reflexes bypass it.
C3.1.10

THE CEREBELLUM & MOVEMENT

The cerebellum coordinates the timing of muscles: it does not decide which muscles work, but it coordinates their timing so every move is smooth and balanced.

🕺

Coordination, not decision

cerebellum · coordination · balance · muscle tone · motor timing

The cerebellum ("little brain") sits below the cerebral hemispheres at the rear of the skull. Its job: coordinate skeletal muscle contraction and balance. It does not decide which muscles contract — that's the motor cortex — but it fine-tunes the timing of contractions so movements are smooth, precise and balanced.

Standing still, walking, riding a bike, typing without looking — all rely on the cerebellum quietly adjusting muscle timing. It regulates gait, maintains muscle tone and keeps your balance, but it never initiates a contraction itself. Damage to it = clumsy, uncoordinated, off-balance movement (ataxia).

FIG 8 — The floor manager at work. The motor cortex decides which muscles contract; the cerebellum receives input from balance organs and stretch receptors and fine-tunes the timing so movement stays smooth, precise and balanced. It coordinates — it never initiates.
Exam gold: the standard two-mark question: "what does the cerebellum do?" — (1) coordinates skeletal muscle contraction and (2) maintains balance. And the trap to avoid: it does not initiate contractions or make decisions — those belong to the motor cortex / cerebral hemispheres.
C3.1.11

CIRCADIAN RHYTHMS

The body runs on a ~24-hour internal clock. When the lights go down, the pineal gland broadcasts melatonin — the radio signal that tells every room it's time to sleep.

🌙

Circadian rhythms, the SCN and melatonin

circadian rhythm · SCN · hypothalamus · pineal gland · melatonin · 460–480 nm light

Circadian rhythms are cycles of about 24 hours that persist even with no external cues — they're generated internally. The clock lives in the suprachiasmatic nuclei (SCN), two small groups of cells in the hypothalamus. The SCN controls melatonin secretion by the pineal gland: melatonin rises in the evening, peaks during the night and drops at dawn — and because the liver removes it rapidly, blood levels swing fast. High melatonin = drowsiness and sleep; falling melatonin = waking. It also helps drive the night-time drop in core body temperature.

Light keeps the clock on track: special retinal ganglion cells detect light at 460–480 nm (blue light) and signal the SCN to adjust melatonin to the day-night cycle. Without light cues the rhythm drifts to slightly longer than 24 hours — which is why jet lag and night shifts feel so rough. Babies only develop a regular day-night rhythm at about 3 months.

FIG 9 — The night shift. Blue light (460–480 nm) hits retinal ganglion cells → SCN in the hypothalamus → pineal gland secretes melatonin. Melatonin climbs at dusk, peaks overnight (deep sleep + cooler core temperature) and is cleared fast by the liver at dawn — waking you up.
THE CLOCK

SCN ⏰

Two groups of cells in the hypothalamus. Generates the ~24 h rhythm internally — it keeps ticking even in constant light or darkness.

THE SIGNAL

Melatonin 💤

Secreted by the pineal gland. Rises in the evening, peaks at night, cleared rapidly by the liver at dawn. High = drowsy; falling = wake.

Mnemonic — "PMS": Pineal → Melatonin → Sleep. And the exam favourite: the wavelength of light that resets the clock is 460–480 nm (blue) — that's why screens before bed can delay sleep.
C3.1.12

EPINEPHRINE & THE FIGHT-OR-FLIGHT

Adrenaline (epinephrine) is the emergency signal: one chemical released by the adrenal glands switches the whole body into fight-or-flight mode.

🚀

Epinephrine: prepare for vigorous activity

adrenaline · adrenal glands · adrenergic receptors · fight or flight · glycogen → glucose

Epinephrine (adrenaline) is secreted by the adrenal glands, perched above the kidneys. It binds adrenergic receptors on target cells across the body — a radio broadcast with many tuned-in listeners. Its effects all prepare the body for vigorous activity:

  • Fuel: muscle glycogen → glucose for muscles; liver glycogen → glucose released into blood.
  • Air: bronchi and bronchioles dilate (smooth muscle relaxes) → easier, faster ventilation.
  • Pump: SAN speeds up → higher heart rate and cardiac output.
  • Routing: arterioles to muscle and liver dilate (more blood); arterioles to gut, kidneys, skin and extremities constrict (less blood).

Secretion is triggered by the brain — the amygdala spots danger, the hypothalamus signals the adrenals. That's why pre-race routines and nerves work: athletes deliberately raise their own epinephrine.

FIG 10 — The emergency broadcast. Epinephrine from the adrenal glands hits the heart (faster), airways (wider), muscle and liver (glycogen → glucose) and arterioles (blood rerouted to working muscle, away from gut, kidneys and skin). One signal, whole-body response.
FUEL

Glycogen → glucose 🍬

Muscle glycogen feeds the muscles directly; liver glycogen releases glucose into the blood for everyone else.

AIR + PUMP

Breathe + beat 💨💓

Bronchi/bronchioles dilate, ventilation rate rises, the SAN speeds up — oxygen delivery maxed out.

ROUTING

Blood to the action 🩸

Arterioles dilate to muscle/liver; constrict to gut, kidneys, skin, extremities. Priorities set.

Exam gold: note the contrast with nervous signalling: epinephrine reaches ALL cells, but only cells with adrenergic receptors respond — that's the "tuned-in listeners" idea from Act 2. And "adrenaline" and "epinephrine" are the same hormone — both names are accepted.
C3.1.13

THE HYPOTHALAMUS & PITUITARY

The hypothalamus is the control room linking the nervous system to the endocrine system, and the pituitary gland below it is the broadcast tower that puts its orders on air.

🎚️

Hypothalamus → pituitary → blood

hypothalamus · pituitary · anterior lobe · posterior lobe · ADH · GnRH · LH · FSH

The hypothalamus is a small region of the brain that links the nervous and endocrine systems. Its nuclei contain sensors for blood temperature, glucose, osmolarity and hormone concentrations, and it receives inputs from sense organs, the medulla, the hippocampus and the amygdala. Below it hangs the pituitary gland on a narrow stalk.

The pituitary has two lobes, both releasing hormones into blood capillaries under the hypothalamus' direction: the anterior lobe (HGH, TSH, LH, FSH, prolactin) and the posterior lobe (ADH, oxytocin). Two exam-ready examples: osmoregulation — osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus trigger ADH release from the posterior pituitary when blood is too salty (see Act 4 of homeostasis); and puberty — the hypothalamus releases GnRH → pituitary releases LH and FSH → gonads produce testosterone / oestradiol and progesterone.

FIG 11 — The control room. The hypothalamus monitors blood temperature, glucose, osmolarity and hormones, then directs the pituitary. Anterior lobe: HGH, TSH, LH, FSH, prolactin. Posterior lobe: ADH, oxytocin. Two must-know loops: osmoregulation (ADH) and puberty (GnRH → LH/FSH).
Remember the direction: the hypothalamus makes and controls; the pituitary releases. ADH is made in the hypothalamus and secreted by the posterior pituitary — the classic trick question.
C3.1.14 – C3.1.16

AUTONOMIC OUTPUT & HOMEOSTASIS

Three automatic systems run without you thinking: heart rate and ventilation are tuned by negative feedback in the brainstem, and peristalsis is driven by the gut's own nervous system.

🤖

Heart rate · ventilation · peristalsis

SAN · medulla · baroreceptors · chemoreceptors · respiratory centres · enteric nervous system

Heart rate (C3.1.14): the sinoatrial node (SAN) in the right atrium wall sets the pace. The cardiovascular centre in the medulla oblongata tunes it: a sympathetic nerve speeds it up (up to 3× resting), the vagus nerve slows it down. Baroreceptors in the aorta and carotid arteries report blood pressure; chemoreceptors there report O₂ and pH. Negative feedback: low blood pressure → speed up; high → slow down. Epinephrine (Act 10) overrides during emergencies.

Ventilation (C3.1.15): respiratory centres in the brainstem drive the diaphragm and intercostals. Stretch receptors in the lungs stop inhalation before over-inflation. Chemoreceptors in the aorta and carotids monitor blood pH (via acid-sensing ion channels): CO₂ rises → pH falls → the centres shorten the gap between breaths → you breathe faster and blow off CO₂ until pH recovers. Normal blood pH is 7.35–7.45; below 6.8 is fatal.

Peristalsis (C3.1.16): the gut wall has two smooth-muscle layers — outer longitudinal, inner circular. Peristalsis = waves of contraction: circular muscle squeezes behind the food, longitudinal muscle shortens where the food is, pushing it one way (away from the mouth). The enteric nervous system (ENS) — the gut's own microcircuits — runs it without CNS input. Swallowing starts voluntarily, then the ENS takes over for good.

FIG 12 — Three auto-pilots. Heart: SAN + medulla with baroreceptor/chemoreceptor feedback. Ventilation: brainstem centres tuned by blood-pH chemoreceptors. Gut: the enteric nervous system runs peristalsis waves (circular squeeze behind, longitudinal push ahead) with no CNS input needed.
C3.1.14

Heart rate ❤️

SAN = pacemaker. Sympathetic ↑ (up to 3×), vagus ↓. Baroreceptors (pressure) + chemoreceptors (O₂, pH) → medulla → negative feedback.

C3.1.15

Ventilation 🫁

Brainstem centres → diaphragm + intercostals. Stretch receptors cap inhalation. pH chemoreceptors: CO₂ ↑ → pH ↓ → faster breathing → CO₂ blown off.

C3.1.16

Peristalsis 🌀

Two smooth-muscle layers + enteric nervous system microcircuits. One-direction waves. Swallowing: voluntary start → ENS takeover. Defecation: sphincter control learned young.

Exam gold: both heart-rate and ventilation control share the same feedback trick — chemoreceptors in the aorta and carotid arteries monitoring blood chemistry, with the medulla as the coordinating centre. And the key peristalsis fact: the ENS works without CNS input, but swallowing is initiated voluntarily — two different bosses for one tube.
AHL · C3.1.17 – C3.1.23

PLANT RESPONSES & PHYTOHORMONES

Plants use phytohormones such as auxin, gibberellin and ethylene to coordinate growth, bending toward light and ripening fruit — with positive feedback reinforcing the response.

🌱

Tropisms, auxin and ethylene — plant integration

tropism · phototropism · phytohormones · auxin · efflux carriers · ethylene · positive feedback

Tropisms (C3.1.17) are differential growth responses to directional stimuli — positive = growing toward the stimulus, negative = away. Roots are positively gravitropic; shoots are positively phototropic and negatively gravitropic. Seedling experiments let you observe curvature qualitatively (diagrams) or quantitatively (angle of curvature). Phototropism (C3.1.18): shoots grow toward the brightest light because the shaded side grows faster — maximising light capture for photosynthesis.

Phytohormones (C3.1.19) are plant signalling chemicals controlling growth and development — auxin, gibberellins (stem growth: semi-dwarf wheat), cytokinins, ethylene, abscisic acid, brassinosteroids, jasmonic acid. The star is auxin: made in shoot tips, moved cell-to-cell by efflux carriers (C3.1.20) — membrane proteins that pump auxin out directionally, building a concentration gradient. Auxin then promotes cell elongation (C3.1.21): it triggers proton pumps that acidify the cell wall, weakening crosslinks so the wall extends and the cell grows. A side-on light gradient → uneven auxin → one side elongates more → the shoot bends. Auxin + cytokinin (C3.1.22) work synergistically in stem/root apices (division + enlargement) but antagonistically on lateral roots and buds (auxin inhibits, cytokinin stimulates — apical dominance). And ethylene (C3.1.23) drives fruit ripening via positive feedback: ripening fruit makes ethylene, which speeds ripening, which makes more ethylene — one bad apple really does turn the whole bowl.

FIG 13 — The green room in one figure: light from one side piles auxin on the shaded side via efflux carriers, so those cells elongate faster and the shoot bends toward the light (C3.1.17–21). Meanwhile ethylene gas from a ripening fruit feeds back positively to ripen the whole bowl (C3.1.23).
C3.1.17 – 18

Tropisms 🌻

Differential growth toward (positive) or away from (negative) a stimulus. Roots: +gravitropic. Shoots: +phototropic, −gravitropic. Bending = shaded side grows faster.

C3.1.19 – 21

Phytohormones + auxin 🧪

Auxin, gibberellins, cytokinins, ethylene, ABA, brassinosteroids, jasmonic acid. Auxin: made in shoot tips, moved directionally by efflux carriers, elongates cells via wall acidification.

C3.1.22 – 23

Auxin + cytokinin · ethylene 🍌

Synergistic in apices (division + growth); antagonistic on lateral roots/buds (apical dominance). Ethylene ripens fruit via positive feedback — gas spreads to neighbours.

Mnemonic — "Auxin = Away from the sun… no wait": auxin piles up on the shaded side, and that side grows faster, bending the shoot toward the light. Shaded side = more auxin = faster growth = bends toward light. Repeat it until it sticks.
Nature of Science: tropism studies are a classic accuracy vs precision vs reliability playground: measure the angle of curvature (quantitative) rather than just sketching (qualitative); repeat measurements to test reliability, and compare with controls (uniform light) for validity. Expect a data question on seedling curvature experiments.

Test yourself

✍ SELF-TEST

10 questions. Pick an answer and you'll instantly see why.

1. Which channel of integration is fastest and targets a single effector?

✅ Nerves are the wired mics: millisecond speed, one precise target (muscle or gland). Hormones are slower and reach everyone; blood is transport, not a signal channel per se.
❌ Hormonal signalling is slower and widespread (only receptor cells respond). The fast, single-target channel is the nervous one.

2. Why is a reflex faster than a conscious response?

✅ The reflex arc is a three-neuron loop in the spinal cord. A conscious response needs the extra trip to the brain and back — that detour is the delay.
❌ The speed comes from bypassing the brain entirely, not from myelin or hormones. Myelination helps, but the route is what makes reflexes fast.

3. Motor neurons are described as…

✅ Motor = efferent = exiting the CNS toward muscles (or glands). Sensory = afferent = arriving from receptors. "Efferent" and "exiting" both start with E.
❌ That's the sensory neuron's job. Motor neurons carry output away from the CNS to effectors — efferent.

4. What does the pineal gland secrete, and when does its level peak?

✅ Melatonin from the pineal gland rises at dusk, peaks at night (deep sleep, cooler core temperature) and is rapidly cleared by the liver at dawn. High = drowsy; falling = waking.
❌ ADH comes from the posterior pituitary (water balance), and epinephrine from the adrenal glands (fight or flight). The sleep hormone is melatonin, peaking at night.

5. Why does epinephrine affect some cells but not others?

✅ Epinephrine is a radio broadcast: it circulates everywhere in the blood, but only cells with matching adrenergic receptors are "tuned in". That's the difference between hormonal and nervous signalling.
❌ Hormones travel in the blood to all cells. Selectivity comes from receptors on target cells, not from delivery routes.

6. ADH is made in the hypothalamus but secreted by…

✅ The hypothalamus makes ADH, transports it down axons, and the posterior pituitary releases it into the blood. Anterior lobe handles HGH, TSH, LH, FSH, prolactin.
❌ Anterior lobe hormones are HGH, TSH, LH, FSH and prolactin. ADH and oxytocin are made in the hypothalamus and released by the posterior pituitary.

7. Blood pressure drops. Which detectors tell the medulla to speed up the heart?

✅ Baroreceptors monitor blood pressure in the aorta and carotid arteries → medulla → sympathetic nerve speeds up the SAN. Negative feedback restores pressure.
❌ Lung stretch receptors stop over-inflation during breathing; skin thermoreceptors sense temperature. Pressure is the baroreceptors' job.

8. When blood CO₂ rises, ventilation increases because chemoreceptors detect…

✅ CO₂ dissolves to form acid, so blood pH falls. Chemoreceptors in the aorta and carotids sense the pH drop via acid-sensing ion channels, and the respiratory centres shorten the gap between breaths until pH recovers (7.35–7.45).
❌ The main signal is pH, not O₂. The pH sensors respond to CO₂-driven acidity; O₂ monitoring is the backup system (hypoxia).

9. Peristalsis along most of the gut is controlled by…

✅ The ENS runs peristalsis waves (outer longitudinal + inner circular smooth muscle) without CNS input. Swallowing starts voluntarily, then the ENS takes over. Only the anus sphincter gains voluntary control, learned in early childhood.
❌ Peristalsis is unconscious and local — the gut's own nervous system. The CNS only gets involved at the start of swallowing and the end of defecation.

10. A shoot bends toward light because…

✅ Light redistributes auxin toward the shaded side via efflux carriers; auxin acidifies cell walls there, elongating those cells faster — the shoot curves toward the light. It's positive phototropism.
❌ The bright side doesn't grow faster — the shaded side does. And ethylene ripens fruit; it doesn't bend shoots.
