B2.2.1

WHAT COUNTS AS AN ORGANELLE

Organelles are discrete subunits adapted to specific functions. The cell wall, cytoskeleton and cytoplasm are NOT organelles.

Organelles are discrete subunits of a cell that are adapted to perform specific functions. Important boundaries: the cell wall, cytoskeleton and cytoplasm are NOT organelles; but nuclei, vesicles, ribosomes and the plasma membrane ARE considered organelles.

B2.2.2

THE ADVANTAGE OF SEPARATING NUCLEUS & CYTOPLASM

Separating the nucleus and cytoplasm separates gene transcription from translation, allowing post-transcriptional modification of mRNA.

Because transcription happens in the nucleus and translation in the cytoplasm, the mRNA can be modified after transcription (post-transcriptional modification) before it meets the ribosomes. In prokaryotes this separation isn't possible — mRNA can immediately meet ribosomes.

B2.2.3

ADVANTAGES OF COMPARTMENTALIZATION IN THE CYTOPLASM

Compartmentalization concentrates metabolites and enzymes, and separates incompatible biochemical processes.

  • Concentration — metabolites and enzymes are concentrated in compartments so reactions run efficiently.
  • Separation of incompatible processes — e.g. lysosomes and phagocytic vacuoles keep destructive digestive enzymes away from the rest of the cell.
AHL · B2.2.4

ADAPTATIONS OF THE MITOCHONDRION

The mitochondrion is adapted for ATP production: double membrane, small intermembrane space, large cristae surface, and Krebs-cycle enzymes in the matrix.

  • Double membrane with a small volume of intermembrane space.
  • Large surface area of cristae (folded inner membrane) for the electron transport chain.
  • Compartmentalization — the Krebs cycle enzymes and substrates are concentrated in the matrix.
FIG 1 — A mitochondrion: double membrane, small intermembrane space, large folded cristae, and the Krebs cycle in the matrix.
AHL · B2.2.5

ADAPTATIONS OF THE CHLOROPLAST

The chloroplast is adapted for photosynthesis: large thylakoid surface with photosystems, small internal fluid volumes, and Calvin-cycle enzymes in the stroma.

  • Large surface area of thylakoid membranes carrying photosystems.
  • Small volumes of fluid inside thylakoids (helps build a proton gradient).
  • Compartmentalization — the Calvin cycle enzymes and substrates are in the stroma.
FIG 2 — A chloroplast: thylakoid stacks (with photosystems) and the Calvin cycle in the stroma.
AHL · B2.2.6

FUNCTIONAL BENEFITS OF THE NUCLEAR DOUBLE MEMBRANE

The nuclear double membrane needs pores, and must break into vesicles during mitosis and meiosis.

  • Pores let mRNA and ribosome subunits exit the nucleus.
  • During mitosis and meiosis the nuclear envelope breaks into vesicles then re-forms.
AHL · B2.2.7

FREE RIBOSOMES vs THE ROUGH ER

Free ribosomes make proteins retained in the cell; ribosomes on the rough ER make proteins for transport/secretion.

  • Free ribosomes — synthesise proteins for retention in the cell (used in the cytoplasm).
  • Rough ER ribosomes (membrane-bound) — synthesise proteins for transport within the cell and secretion.
AHL · B2.2.8

THE GOLGI APPARATUS

The Golgi apparatus is involved in the processing and secretion of proteins.

The Golgi apparatus modifies (processes) proteins received from the ER and packages them into vesicles for secretion or transport to other parts of the cell.

AHL · B2.2.9

VESICLES & CLATHRIN

Vesicles transport materials between compartments; clathrin is involved in their formation.

Vesicles are small membrane-bound sacs that carry materials between compartments (e.g. from ER → Golgi → plasma membrane). Clathrin is a protein that coats the membrane to help form a vesicle (e.g. during endocytosis).
