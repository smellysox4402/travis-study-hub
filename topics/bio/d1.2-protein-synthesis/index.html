D1.2 PROTEIN SYNTHESIS

From DNA to protein: transcription (DNA → mRNA) and translation (mRNA → polypeptide), the genetic code, and (AHL) the molecular details of initiation, modification and the proteome.

D1.2.1

TRANSCRIPTION

Transcription is the synthesis of RNA using a DNA template, carried out by RNA polymerase.

Transcription is the synthesis of RNA using a DNA template. The enzyme RNA polymerase reads the DNA template and builds a complementary RNA strand (mRNA).

D1.2.2

BASE PAIRING IN TRANSCRIPTION

A on the DNA template pairs with U on the RNA strand.

Transcription uses hydrogen bonding and complementary base pairing. In RNA, adenine (A) on the DNA template pairs with uracil (U) on the RNA (instead of thymine); G pairs with C.

D1.2.3

DNA TEMPLATES ARE STABLE

A single strand can be transcribed repeatedly without the DNA base sequence changing.

Because the DNA base sequence is stable (it isn't altered during transcription), a single DNA strand can be used as a template many times to make many mRNA copies. In somatic cells that don't divide, the DNA is not replicated but still serves as the template.

D1.2.4

TRANSCRIPTION & GENE EXPRESSION

Not all genes are expressed at any given time; transcription is the first step of expression.

Transcription is required for gene expression. Not all genes in a cell are expressed at once — only certain genes are transcribed, giving each cell type its identity. The products of gene expression (RNA/proteins) are used in the cell.

D1.2.5

TRANSLATION

Translation is the synthesis of a polypeptide from mRNA — the base sequence of mRNA is translated into the amino acid sequence.

Translation is the synthesis of polypeptides from mRNA. The base sequence of mRNA is translated (read in triplets) into the amino acid sequence of a polypeptide.

D1.2.6

ROLES OF mRNA, RIBOSOMES & tRNA

mRNA binds the small ribosome subunit; two tRNAs can bind simultaneously; the ribosome has binding sites.

  • mRNA — carries the code; binds to the small subunit of the ribosome.
  • Ribosome — has binding sites; two tRNAs can bind simultaneously (to adjacent codons).
  • tRNA — carries the amino acid; its anticodon matches the mRNA codon.
D1.2.7

CODON & ANTICODON

tRNA and mRNA pair by complementary base pairing between codon and anticodon.

A codon is a triplet of bases on mRNA; an anticodon is the complementary triplet on the tRNA. They pair by complementary base pairing, so each tRNA delivers the correct amino acid.

D1.2.8

FEATURES OF THE GENETIC CODE

The code is a triplet code with degeneracy and universality.

  • Triplet code — each codon is 3 bases (64 possible, enough to code 20 amino acids + start/stop).
  • Degeneracy — more than one codon can code for the same amino acid (reduces the effect of mutations).
  • Universality — the same code is used across all organisms (evidence of common ancestry).
D1.2.9

USING THE CODON TABLE

Use a genetic-code table (mRNA codons) to deduce the amino acid sequence from an mRNA strand.

Read the mRNA in triplets (codons) from the 5′ end, look each up in the codon table, and write the corresponding amino acid sequence.

D1.2.10

ELONGATION

The ribosome moves along mRNA step by step, linking amino acids by peptide bonds to the growing chain.

During elongation, the ribosome moves stepwise along the mRNA. Each new tRNA brings an amino acid, which is joined to the growing polypeptide chain by a peptide bond.

FIG 1 — Translation: the ribosome moves along mRNA; each tRNA's anticodon matches a codon and delivers its amino acid, joined by peptide bonds.
D1.2.11

MUTATIONS & PROTEIN STRUCTURE

A point mutation can change the protein structure.

A mutation is a change in the DNA base sequence. A point mutation (a single base change) can alter the mRNA codon, and so the amino acid sequence and the protein's structure/function (e.g. the sickle-cell mutation).

AHL · D1.2.12

DIRECTIONALITY OF TRANSCRIPTION & TRANSLATION

Both transcription and translation proceed 5′→3′.

Transcription builds mRNA in the 5′→3′ direction. Translation reads the mRNA in the 5′→3′ direction (the ribosome moves from the 5′ end toward the 3′ end).

AHL · D1.2.13

INITIATION OF TRANSCRIPTION — THE PROMOTER

Transcription begins at a promoter, where transcription factors bind.

Transcription initiates at a promoter — a DNA sequence upstream of the gene. Transcription factors bind to the promoter and help RNA polymerase start transcription.

AHL · D1.2.14

NON-CODING DNA SEQUENCES

Some DNA does not code for polypeptides: regulators of gene expression, introns, telomeres, and genes for rRNA/tRNA.

Not all DNA codes for proteins. Non-coding DNA includes regulators of gene expression, introns, telomeres and genes for rRNA and tRNA (in eukaryotes).

AHL · D1.2.15

POST-TRANSCRIPTIONAL MODIFICATION

In eukaryotes, introns are removed and exons spliced together, plus a 5′ cap and poly-A tail are added.

The primary transcript is modified: introns are removed, exons are spliced together to form mature mRNA, and a 5′ cap and poly-A tail are added.

AHL · D1.2.16

ALTERNATIVE SPLICING

Splicing different combinations of exons can produce variants of a protein from a single gene.

Alternative splicing joins different combinations of exons from the same gene, producing different mRNA variants and therefore different protein forms from one gene.

AHL · D1.2.17

INITIATION OF TRANSLATION

The small ribosome subunit binds the 5′ end of mRNA, moves to the start codon, and the initiator tRNA binds.

Translation begins when the small ribosome subunit attaches to the 5′ terminal of mRNA, moves to the start codon (AUG), and the initiator tRNA (carrying methionine) binds. The large subunit then joins.

AHL · D1.2.18

MODIFICATION OF POLYPEPTIDES

Many polypeptides must be modified before they can function (e.g. insulin, collagen).

Many newly made polypeptides must be modified to become functional — e.g. insulin (a chain is cut) and collagen (procollagen is processed). Examples might also include enzymes (active-site formation), glycoproteins (carbohydrate addition), etc.

AHL · D1.2.19

RECYCLING AMINO ACIDS — PROTEASOMES

Sustaining a functional proteome requires constant protein breakdown and synthesis.

Proteins are continually broken down and replaced. Proteasomes degrade unneeded or damaged proteins, recycling the amino acids for new protein synthesis. This sustains a functional proteome.
