D2.1 CELL & NUCLEAR DIVISION

How cells replicate and divide by mitosis and meiosis, chromosome structure, cytokinesis, the cell cycle and how errors cause cancer.
D2.1 · SL + AHL 17 SYLLABUS POINTS MITOSIS · MEIOSIS · THE CYCLE
D2.1.1

WHY NEW CELLS ARE MADE

Every organism needs new cells — for growth, for maintenance, and for reproduction. And there's a law: new cells only ever come from dividing pre-existing ones.

📋

Three reasons to throw a division

Growth, maintenance, reproduction — the three jobs on the guest list

Growth — more cells, not bigger cells. An embryo goes from one zygote to trillions of cells by division.

Maintenance — replace worn-out or damaged cells. Your skin, gut lining and blood are constantly being restocked.

Reproduction — single-celled organisms (and quaking aspens!) reproduce by cell division, making genetically identical clones.

In cell division, the dividing cell is the mother cell; the products are daughter cells. The mother cell disappears as an entity — it doesn't survive beside its offspring like an animal parent does.

Cell theory, hard mode: new cells are only ever produced by division of a pre-existing cell. Trace it back far enough and every cell in your body connects to the original zygote — and beyond that, to the earliest cells on Earth. That's the continuity of life.

The quaking aspen "Pando" is one of Earth's biggest organisms — a single root system sending up thousands of genetically identical stems. All one clone, all made by cell division.

D2.1.2 · D2.1.3

CYTOKINESIS

Nuclear division sorts the chromosomes; cytokinesis actually cuts the cell in two. Animals pinch, plants build.

🍰

Two ways to slice the cake

Cytokinesis = dividing the cytoplasm between the daughter cells

Animal cells — the pinch. The plasma membrane is pulled inward around the equator, forming a cleavage furrow. The pulling is done by a contractile ring of actin and myosin — the same proteins that power muscle — sitting just inside the membrane. When the furrow reaches the centre, the cell is pinched in two.

Plant cells — the build. Plants have a stiff cell wall, so they can't pinch. Instead, microtubules form a scaffold across the equator, and vesicles travel along it and fuse into plate-shaped structures. The plates grow into two complete layers of new plasma membrane, then pectins are deposited between them by exocytosis — the middle lamella. Finally each daughter cell secretes cellulose to build its own new cell wall.

Exam shape: animal = cleavage furrow + actin/myosin ring. Plant = vesicles → cell plate → middle lamella → two new cellulose walls. Never swap them.
THE TWO WAYS: animals squeeze (contractile ring of actin + myosin); plants construct (microtubule scaffold → vesicles fuse → middle lamella → cellulose walls).
⚖️

Equal vs unequal splits

Sometimes the cytoplasm is shared evenly, sometimes one daughter gets the lion's share

Equal cytokinesis — cytoplasm split into two equal halves. Happens in growing root tips, where columns of cells all differentiate the same way.

Unequal cytokinesis — one daughter gets most of the cytoplasm. Two classic examples:

  • Budding in yeast — the nucleus divides by mitosis, then a small outgrowth (the bud) receives one nucleus but only a small share of cytoplasm. A dividing wall separates them; the bud splits off, leaving a scar. Yeast doesn't even need to double in size between divisions.
  • Oogenesis in humans — sperm production splits cytoplasm equally (four equal sperm). Eggs are the opposite: usually only ONE egg is made at a time, with huge food stores for the embryo. The first division makes one big cell + a tiny polar body; the big cell divides again — one big egg + another tiny polar body. The polar bodies get a nucleus but almost no cytoplasm, and they never develop.
Survival rule: a small daughter cell can survive if it gets a nucleus and at least one of each organelle it can't assemble from scratch — like mitochondria, which can only come from division of a pre-existing mitochondrion.
D2.1.4

MITOSIS & MEIOSIS — TWO ROLES

Mitosis = continuity. Meiosis = change. Same starting point, opposite outcomes.

🔁

Why you need a nuclear division at all

Divide the cytoplasm without dividing the nucleus first and you make a zombie cell

If a cell divides without nuclear division first, one daughter gets the nucleus and the other is anucleate — no nucleus at all. Anucleate cells can't synthesise polypeptides, so they can't grow or maintain themselves. They have limited lifespans: red blood cells, which lose their nucleus, survive only ~120 days.

So before cell division, the mother cell runs either mitosis or meiosis to make extra nuclei. Both are preceded by DNA replication.

🪩 MITOSIS — continuity

same · same · same

Produces genetically identical cells. 2n (diploid) stays 2n. Every cell keeps the same genes — so all cells in your body have the same genome, and tissue rejection never happens within your own body. It also lets a successful genome be inherited unchanged in asexual reproduction.

🎲 MEIOSIS — change

half + shuffled

Halves the chromosome number 2n → n (haploid) and generates genetic diversity. Gene pairs are dealt randomly to daughter cells — almost limitless combinations. That variation is the fuel for evolution by natural selection.

One-liner to memorise: mitosis maintains the genome and chromosome number; meiosis halves the chromosome number and shuffles the genome.
CONTINUITY vs CHANGE: pink + cyan = a homologous pair. Mitosis keeps both members in every daughter (2n). Meiosis splits each pair and shuffles the alleles (n) — four unique half-cells.
D2.1.5 · D2.1.6

CHROMOSOME STRUCTURE

Before any division, DNA must be replicated. The result: chromosomes that are now pairs of identical twins, holding hands.

🧬

From single to twin chromatids

DNA replication is a prerequisite for BOTH mitosis and meiosis

Before replication, each chromosome is one long DNA molecule — so thin that light microscopes can't see it. After replication there are two identical DNA molecules, still considered ONE chromosome, held together by loops of a protein complex called cohesin.

Each single DNA molecule is a chromatid. So a replicated chromosome = two sister chromatids (genetically identical) joined at a centromere. Chromatids on different chromosomes are non-sister chromatids and usually differ.

  • Condensation — during early mitosis/meiosis, DNA packs tighter and the chromosome becomes shorter and fatter — visible at last. The two strands can then be seen.
  • Movement — chromosomes get hauled to opposite poles by microtubules growing from microtubule organising centres (MTOCs). Microtubules attach at the centromere region, at a protein structure called the kinetochore.
  • The cut — cohesin loops are NOT cut until the start of anaphase. Only then do the sister chromatids finally separate and get pulled to opposite poles. (Old hypothesis: the centromere itself divided. Falsified.)
Memorise the timeline: replication (interphase) → condensation (prophase) → alignment (metaphase) → cohesin cut + separation (anaphase) → nuclei reform (telophase). The cohesin cut is the moment the twins let go.
THE TWINS: one chromosome, two sister chromatids, cohesin loops holding them, centromere at the pinch point, kinetochore where the spindle grabs on. Cut the cohesin and the twins split.
D2.1.7 · D2.1.8

MITOSIS — THE PMAT STAGES

Four phases, one perfectly choreographed night: condense, line up, split, reform. PMAT.

💃

The four moves (and the interlude before)

Interphase is the pre-game — the actual division is PMAT

Interphase (before): chromosomes are just dispersed chromatin — too thin to see individually. All DNA gets replicated, so every chromosome becomes two elongated sister chromatids.

  1. Prophase (pro = before) — chromosomes condense by packing DNA tightly, becoming thick enough to see. Toward the end, microtubules grow from MTOCs at the poles to form the spindle. The nuclear membrane breaks down.
  2. Metaphase (meta = after) — spindle microtubules attach to each chromatid's centromere/kinetochore. Sister chromatids attach to opposite poles. Tension tests the attachment. At the end, chromosomes are fully condensed and aligned on the equator.
  3. Anaphase (ana = up) — the cohesin loops are CUT, so sister chromatids become separate chromosomes. The kinetochore shortens the microtubules, pulling each chromosome to its pole. Chromosome arms trail behind in the viscous cytoplasm.
  4. Telophase (telos = finally) — chromosomes reach the poles and are pulled into a tight group; a nuclear membrane reforms around each set; chromosomes decondense back into chromatin. Genes can be transcribed again. Cytokinesis usually completes the split during/after this.
Exam shape: "identify the phase" from a micrograph → look for the clues: condensed visible chromosomes scattered = prophase; lined up on the equator = metaphase; chromosomes pulled apart toward two poles = anaphase; two nuclei forming = telophase; no visible chromosomes = interphase.
Lab skill — root tip squash: grow garlic roots → fix in 70% ethanol + ethanoic acid (24 h) → treat with 0.1 mol dm⁻³ HCl at 40 °C to loosen cell walls → stain with toluidine blue (binds DNA!) → squash under a coverslip → scan low power, then medium power. Count ~100 cells and classify each as interphase or a mitosis phase → mitotic index = cells in mitosis ÷ total cells.
THE FIVE-CARD SET: interphase (invisible chromatin) → prophase (condensed) → metaphase (equator) → anaphase (twins split to poles) → telophase (two nuclei + furrow).
D2.1.9 · D2.1.11

MEIOSIS — GENERATING VARIATION

Meiosis is a reduction division: 2n → n. Two divisions, four unique haploid cells. And it's where the genetic mixing happens.

🎲

Why haploid? The doubling trap

If gametes were diploid, chromosome numbers would explode every generation

Diploid (2n) = two sets of chromosomes → homologous pairs. Haploid (n) = one set, all non-homologous. In humans n = 23, so body cells are 2n = 46.

Gametes MUST be haploid, because fertilization fuses two gametes: n + n = 2n. If gametes were 2n, the zygote would be 4n, its gametes 4n → offspring 8n — the number doubles every generation. Meiosis is the reduction division that cancels this: two rounds of chromosome segregation produce four haploid cells from one diploid mother cell.

Homologous chromosomes have the same sequence of genes but can carry different alleles. Meiosis exploits this with two shuffle mechanisms:

  • Crossing over — in prophase I, homologues pair up (synapsis) forming a bivalent (4 DNA molecules). Non-sister chromatids swap segments at points called chiasmata. Result: chromatids with brand-new combinations of alleles.
  • Random orientation — at metaphase I, each bivalent lines up on the equator facing either way by chance (50/50). With n bivalents, there are 2ⁿ possible combinations. In humans: 2²³ = 8.4 million — before crossing over even joins in.
Exam fact: crossing over + random orientation + (in meiosis II, sister chromatids finally separate) = almost limitless variety in gametes. That's why sexual reproduction generates variation for natural selection.

Meiosis I separates HOMOLOGOUS chromosomes (2n → n). Meiosis II separates SISTER CHROMATIDS (like mitosis, but no replication in between). First division = reduction; second = the chromatid split.

TWO SHUFFLES, ONE RESULT: crossing over mixes alleles within a chromosome; random orientation mixes whole chromosomes between daughter cells. 2²³ possible chromosome combos in every human gamete — before crossing over is even counted.
D2.1.10

NON-DISJUNCTION

Sometimes chromosomes fail to separate. The result: gametes with an extra — or a missing — chromosome.

⚠️

One skipped beat at anaphase

Non-disjunction = a pair that should split, doesn't

Normally: homologous chromosomes separate at anaphase I, and sister chromatids separate at anaphase II. When a pair moves to the SAME pole instead of opposite ones — that's non-disjunction. The result: cells with one chromosome extra or missing.

A missing chromosome is usually lethal fast — essential genes are gone. An extra chromosome is also usually lethal, but a few trisomies survive. The famous one:

🧬 DOWN SYNDROME

trisomy 21

A non-disjunction event gives a person three copies of chromosome 21 instead of two. Features vary: hearing loss, heart and vision disorders. The most common viable human trisomy.

⚧ SEX CHROMOSOME ANOMALIES

non-lethal

Klinefelter's syndrome = XXY. Turner's syndrome = just one X. Both come from non-disjunction of the sex chromosomes.

Maternal age link: the incidence of non-disjunction (and Down syndrome) rises steeply with maternal age — from ~1 in 1,500 at 20 to ~1 in 30 by 45. Older eggs have spent longer in a halted state and are more error-prone.
Exam shape: "explain how non-disjunction leads to Down syndrome" → anaphase I or II error → gamete with two copies of chr 21 → fertilization with a normal gamete → zygote with three copies (trisomy 21).
THE ERROR CHAIN: anaphase I failure → gamete with 2 copies of chr 21 (n+1) → fertilise with a normal gamete → zygote with 3 copies → Down syndrome. Missing-chromosome gametes (n−1) usually die.

THE CELL CYCLE

Cell proliferation follows a repeating cycle: a long prep phase (interphase), then the big split (mitosis + cytokinesis).

🪩

Proliferation — the whole point

Rapid increase in cell number: division faster than death

Cell proliferation powers growth (embryos; growth plates in bones during childhood; plant meristems at root and shoot tips), cell replacement (skin epidermis — cells divide in the basal layer, make keratin, dry out, and get rubbed off at the surface) and tissue repair (wounds heal via stem cells that divide then differentiate).

G1 — Gap 1

post-mitosis

After mitosis, before replication. Each chromosome is a single DNA molecule. Active growth phase.

S — Synthesis

copy time

ALL DNA in the nucleus is replicated → identical pairs of DNA molecules held by cohesin loops. These persist until the end of metaphase.

G2 — Gap 2

pre-division

After replication, before mitosis. Each chromosome = two chromatids. Growth resumes while preparing for mitosis.

M — Mitosis

the split

Mitosis (nuclear division) + cytokinesis (cell division). Then back to G1 — or out the door to G0.

G0 (Gap zero) — cells that leave the cycle after mitosis/cytokinesis. They grow and differentiate for a specific role and never divide again (or only rarely).

Interphase is NOT rest: cells typically double in size. DNA replicates; the cytoplasm grows (new enzymes); membranes expand (more phospholipids, membrane proteins, cholesterol); organelles multiply — mitochondria and chloroplasts can only come from division of existing ones (they have DNA), Golgi bodies bud off existing ones, ribosomes are assembled in the nucleolus. Interphase is where the real work happens.

Heterochromatin (gene-poor DNA) stays condensed in interphase; the rest decondenses into chromatin so genes can be transcribed → protein synthesis.

ONE NIGHT AT CLUB SPLIT: G1 (arrive, grow) → S (copy your DNA) → G2 (check the outfit) → M (the split). G0 = the VIP lounge — present but never dancing again. Checkpoints = the bouncers.

CELL CYCLE CONTROL & CANCER

The cell cycle has checkpoints, cyclins, and brakes. When the brakes fail, you get a tumour.

🚦

Cyclins — the DJs that keep the night moving

Control of the cell cycle using cyclins

The cycle needs a coordinated sequence of changes, with checkpoints that hold cells until it's appropriate to move on — and stop division once a tissue has enough cells.

Cyclins are proteins whose levels rise and fall through the cycle. They activate cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs), which add phosphate to other proteins, switching them on. Each cyclin activates the proteins needed at a specific phase — so actions happen at the right time, and the cell only passes checkpoints when it benefits the whole organism. Unless cyclins reach a threshold concentration, the cell doesn't advance.

CyclinJob at the club
Cyclin DTriggers movement from G0 → G1 and G1 → S.
Cyclin EPrepares the cell for DNA replication in S phase.
Cyclin AActivates DNA replication inside the nucleus (S phase).
Cyclin BPromotes assembly of the mitotic spindle + other prep for mitosis.
Exam graph: cyclin concentration vs cell cycle phase — levels rise and fall as peaks. Cyclin D spikes in G1, E at the G1/S border, A in S, B at G2/M. Read the graph by matching the peak to the phase.
💥

When control is lost — mutations and tumours

Two classes of mutagen · two classes of gene · benign vs malignant

A cell that loses cycle control divides out of control, and its daughters inherit the loss — the group grows exponentially. That's a tumour. Mutagens raise the odds: carcinogenic chemicals (IARC lists 50+ as "definitely carcinogenic") and high-energy radiation (X-rays, UV — not visible light).

🚀 PROTO-ONCOGENES → ONCOGENES

the stuck accelerator

Proto-oncogenes normally promote cell proliferation (regulating expression, secondary messengers, growth factors/receptors). A mutation can turn one into an oncogene that actively promotes proliferation — genetically DOMINANT: one mutated copy of the pair is enough. Often a missense mutation makes the protein super-active.

🛑 TUMOUR-SUPPRESSOR GENES

the failed brakes

These prevent proliferation: brakes at checkpoints, DNA-repair machinery, or apoptosis for irreparably damaged cells. Mutations that stop them working are recessive — one functioning copy still produces enough protein. Base substitutions that create a stop codon → truncated, non-functional polypeptide.

One mutation is rarely enough — some tumours need ~10 mutations in a single cell. The odds per cell are tiny, but you have vast numbers of cells and a long lifetime — and each division that speeds up creates a bigger pool for the next mutation (evolution, in miniature). Most people develop tumours at some point; most are harmless or treatable.

Benign vs malignant (D2.1.17): benign = cells stick together as a single mass, unlikely to cause harm — NOT cancer. Malignant = poor cell-to-cell adhesion → cells detach, invade neighbouring tissue, or ride blood/lymph to elsewhere — metastasis → secondary tumours. Malignant = cancer. Hormone-stimulated tissues (breast, ovary, testis, thyroid) are more prone to malignancy.
Mitotic index (skill): mitotic index = number of cells in mitosis ÷ total number of cells observed. In a micrograph of a tumour, count ~100 cells, tally interphase vs mitosis stages, and compute the ratio. High mitotic index = fast division — a cancer red flag. Same technique on a root-tip squash.

✍ SELF-TEST

Ten questions, instant feedback. No peeking at the cheat sheet.

1. Which of these is a reason cells divide?

✅ Growth (more cells), maintenance (replace lost cells) and reproduction — plus repair. Cells don't divide to get bigger themselves.
❌ Division makes MORE cells, not bigger ones. The three reasons: growth, maintenance, reproduction.

2. How does an ANIMAL cell carry out cytokinesis?

✅ Animals pinch: a contractile ring of actin and myosin pulls the membrane inward until the cell splits.
❌ That's the plant way! Animals use the cleavage furrow + contractile ring; plants build a cell plate from vesicles.

3. During human oogenesis, cytokinesis is…

✅ Eggs hoard cytoplasm for the embryo: one big oocyte gets almost everything; tiny polar bodies get a nucleus but little cytoplasm and never develop.
❌ Oogenesis is the classic UNEQUAL split. (Equal division is what happens in sperm production and root tips.)

4. Mitosis produces cells that are…

✅ Mitosis = continuity: identical genome, 2n maintained. It's how your body cells are made and how asexual reproduction works.
❌ Halving and shuffling is MEIOSIS. Mitosis keeps everything identical.

5. What holds sister chromatids together until anaphase?

✅ Cohesin loops bind the two sister chromatids. They're cut at the START of anaphase so the twins can separate.
❌ The kinetochore is where microtubules attach; the spindle pulls. The "glue" between chromatids is cohesin.

6. In which phase do chromosomes line up on the equator?

✅ Metaphase: fully condensed chromosomes aligned on the equator, tension-testing their spindle attachments.
❌ Prophase = condensation, anaphase = separation. The equator lineup is the metaphase signature.

7. Why must meiosis halve the chromosome number?

✅ Gametes must be haploid: two haploid gametes fuse to make a diploid zygote. Without the reduction division, chromosome numbers would double every generation.
❌ The halving exists to cancel the doubling of fertilization — keeping chromosome number constant across generations.

8. Down syndrome is caused by…

✅ Non-disjunction gives a gamete two copies of chr 21; fertilization adds a third → trisomy 21. Risk rises with maternal age.
❌ It's a chromosome-number error (trisomy 21), not a single-gene mutation and not infectious.

9. Which two mechanisms generate variation in meiosis?

✅ Crossing over swaps allele segments between non-sister chromatids; random orientation gives 2ⁿ chromosome combos (2²³ in humans).
❌ Cohesin/kinetochores are mechanics, not variation sources. Variation = crossing over + random orientation.

10. A proto-oncogene that mutates into an oncogene acts like…

✅ Oncogenes promote proliferation and are dominant — one mutated copy of the pair is enough. (Failed brakes = tumour-suppressor genes, which are recessive.)
❌ The accelerator/stuck-on is the oncogene story. Brakes failing is the tumour-suppressor story — and that's recessive.
