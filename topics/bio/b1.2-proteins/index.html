B1.2.1

THE GENERALIZED AMINO ACID

An amino acid has a central alpha carbon with an amine group, a carboxyl group, an R-group and a hydrogen atom.

Every amino acid has a central alpha carbon bonded to four groups: an amine group (−NH₂), a carboxyl group (−COOH), a variable R-group (side chain) and a hydrogen atom. It's the R-group that differs between amino acids.

FIG 1 — A generalized amino acid: alpha carbon (C) bonded to an amine group, a carboxyl group, a variable R-group and a hydrogen.
B1.2.2

CONDENSATION — FORMING DIPEPTIDES & CHAINS

Amino acids join by condensation to form dipeptides and longer chains (the peptide bond).

Two amino acids join by a condensation reaction (removing water), forming a peptide bond between the carboxyl of one and the amine of the other. Repeating this makes longer polypeptide chains.

Word equation: amino acid + amino acid → dipeptide + water.
B1.2.3

DIETARY REQUIREMENTS FOR AMINO ACIDS

Essential amino acids must come from food; non-essential amino acids can be made in the body.

  • Essential amino acids — cannot be synthesised, so must be in the diet (from protein-rich food).
  • Non-essential amino acids — can be made from other amino acids.
  • Vegan diets need care to provide all essential amino acids.
B1.2.4

THE INFINITE VARIETY OF PEPTIDE CHAINS

With 20 amino acids that can be in any order and chains of any length, the variety of proteins is enormous.

There are 20 amino acids coded for in the genetic code. Since a chain can have any number of amino acids (from a few to thousands) in any order, the number of possible proteins is effectively infinite.

B1.2.5

EFFECT OF pH & TEMPERATURE — DENATURATION

pH and temperature affect protein structure; extreme values cause denaturation.

The shape of a protein depends on weak bonds that are sensitive to conditions. High temperature or extreme pH can break these bonds, causing the protein to denature — to lose its specific 3D shape (and so its function).

AHL · B1.2.6

R-GROUPS & PROTEIN DIVERSITY

The chemical diversity of R-groups is the basis for the immense diversity in protein form and function.

R-groups can be hydrophobic or hydrophilic, and hydrophilic ones can be polar or charged, acidic or basic. This R-group diversity determines the properties of the protein and gives rise to the huge range of protein forms and functions.

AHL · B1.2.7

PRIMARY STRUCTURE → CONFORMATION

The sequence of amino acids determines the three-dimensional shape of a protein.

The primary structure is the sequence of amino acids. Because each amino acid's position is precise, the protein folds into a precise, predictable and repeatable 3D shape — so proteins are consistent despite their complexity.

AHL · B1.2.8

SECONDARY STRUCTURE

Hydrogen bonding in regular positions stabilises alpha helices and beta-pleated sheets.

The secondary structure is the local folding of the chain into alpha helices (coils) and beta-pleated sheets, stabilised by hydrogen bonds at regular positions along the backbone.

AHL · B1.2.9

TERTIARY STRUCTURE

Tertiary structure depends on hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide covalent bonds and hydrophobic interactions.

The tertiary structure is the overall 3D folding, held by: hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds (between charged groups), disulfide covalent bonds (between cysteines) and hydrophobic interactions. These can occur between R-groups far apart in the chain.

AHL · B1.2.10

POLAR & NON-POLAR AMINO ACIDS & TERTIARY STRUCTURE

In water-soluble proteins, hydrophobic amino acids cluster in the core; integral membrane proteins have hydrophobic regions to embed in membranes.

  • In globular proteins soluble in water, hydrophobic amino acids are clustered in the core (away from water).
  • Integral membrane proteins have regions of hydrophobic amino acids that let them embed in the membrane.
AHL · B1.2.11

QUATERNARY STRUCTURE

Quaternary structure is the assembly of two or more polypeptide chains; it can be non-conjugated or conjugated.

  • Non-conjugated proteins — made only of amino acids (e.g. insulin, collagen).
  • Conjugated proteins — have a non-amino-acid (prosthetic) group (e.g. haemoglobin has a haem group).
AHL · B1.2.12

FORM & FUNCTION — GLOBULAR vs FIBROUS

Globular and fibrous proteins have different shapes suited to different functions.

GLOBULAR

Spherical, soluble

Compact, roughly spherical, water-soluble — e.g. insulin (hormone), haemoglobin (transport). Their folded shape creates an active site.

FIBROUS

Long, insoluble

Long, fibrous, insoluble — e.g. collagen (structure). Strong, structural roles.
