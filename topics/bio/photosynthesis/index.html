PHOTOSYNTHESIS & THE CHLOROPLAST

Photosynthesis is an energy transformation: the chloroplast takes light energy and bakes it into chemical energy stored in carbon compounds — using hydrogen ripped out of water.

🍳

Photosynthesis feeds the whole planet

photosynthesis · energy transformation · carbon compounds · word equation · photolysis

Living organisms need complex carbon compounds to build cells and run life processes. Some organisms — plants, algae and cyanobacteria — make all of them from simple inorganic starting materials, using only light. That process is photosynthesis: light energy is converted into chemical energy in carbon compounds (carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids). This single transformation supplies most of the chemical energy needed for life processes in ecosystems — every calorie you've ever eaten was originally produced by photosynthesis.

The word equation hides a subtle trick: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen. The carbon in glucose comes from CO₂, but the hydrogen needed to reduce CO₂ into a sugar comes from splitting water — photolysis ("photo" = light, "lysis" = loosen). Water splits into separated protons (H⁺) and electrons, and the oxygen left over is just the exhaust.

FIG 1 — Photosynthesis: light energy into chemical energy. Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy in carbon compounds. Word equation: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen. The hydrogen that reduces CO₂ comes from photolysis of water; the oxygen is the waste product.
C1.3.1

Energy transformation 🍳

Light energy is converted into chemical energy stored in carbon compounds — carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids. This supplies most of the chemical energy for life processes in ecosystems.

C1.3.2

Where the hydrogen comes from 🧪

Reducing CO₂ to glucose needs hydrogen. It comes from photolysis of water: 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻. Photolysis only runs when light supplies the energy.

BIG PICTURE

One hectare of redwoods 🌲

A single hectare of Californian redwood forest can hold >4,000 tonnes of biomass — nearly all of it carbon compounds baked by photosynthesis. You are looking at the process that feeds the planet.

Exam gold — the full equation: 6CO₂ + 12H₂O + light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ + 6H₂O. For each glucose molecule, 12 water molecules are split, releasing 24 hydrogen ions. That's why the equation needs 12 waters, not 6 — the extra 6 are there to donate hydrogen, and 6 waters come out the other side.
C1.3.3

THE OXYGEN VENT — O₂ RELEASED

Where does the oxygen actually come from? Not from the carbon dioxide — it's a waste product of splitting water.

💨

The vent that leaks the good stuff

Every oxygen molecule in your next breath was made by splitting water, never by breaking down CO₂. The light-dependent reactions split water in a process called photolysis ("light-splitting"): the light energy is used to rip water apart into oxygen, hydrogen ions and electrons. The oxygen is waste — the plant vents it out through the stomata and gets on with making food.

AKA: the most generous exhaust pipe in biology — it's the source of nearly all the O₂ in Earth's atmosphere.

💧 Photolysis in one line

Light energy splits 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ inside photosystem II. Electrons get used in the electron transport chain, protons help build the gradient, and O₂ is dumped.

🔬 The isotope trick

Feed a plant water labelled with heavy ¹⁸O: the O₂ bubbles come out labelled. Feed it ¹⁸O-CO₂ instead: no labelled O₂. Case closed — the oxygen is water's contribution.

🌍 Planetary fallout

Billions of years ago cyanobacteria flooded the oceans with O₂. Iron rusted into banded iron formations, then the atmosphere went oxygen-rich — enabling aerobic respiration and, eventually, us.

Why it matters for the exam: the word equation "CO₂ + H₂O → glucose + O₂" hides the truth — the carbon in glucose comes from CO₂, but the oxygen released comes from H₂O. Get that the right way round and you've beaten the classic trap.

CHLOROPHYLL & OTHER PIGMENTS

Separation of photosynthetic pigments by chromatography — and the Rf values that identify them.

🎨

Accessory pigments

A leaf pigment extract is a mixture of several colours — chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, carotenes, xanthophylls. To see them, you run thin-layer chromatography (TLC): spot the extract near the bottom of a porous strip, stand it in solvent, and watch the pigments separate as the solvent climbs. Each pigment travels a different distance depending on how strongly it sticks to the strip versus how well it dissolves in the solvent. That travel distance, expressed as an Rf value, is a pigment's fingerprint.

AKA: the spice rack — each pigment is a different spice, and chromatography is the shelf that sorts them into labelled jars.

🧪 How TLC works

Pigments are extracted in propanone, spotted in a pencil line near the strip's base, and the strip stands in solvent. The solvent climbs by capillary action, dragging pigments at different rates — more soluble pigments travel further.

📏 Reading Rf

Measure from the origin (spot line) to the middle of each band, divide by the distance from origin to solvent front. Carotene 0.98 > chlorophyll a 0.59 > chlorophyll b 0.42 > xanthophylls — a fixed fingerprint for each pigment.

🍽️ Why so many spices?

Chlorophyll a dominates, but accessory pigments — chlorophyll b, carotenes, xanthophylls — absorb wavelengths chlorophyll a misses, widening the range of usable light.

Exam command words: "distinguish" = give the Rf values with the colours; "outline" = describe the TLC steps in order. You won't be asked to memorise all six Rf values exactly, but knowing carotene ≈ 1.0 > chlorophyll a > chlorophyll b > xanthophylls is a safe ranking.

THE COLOUR OF LIGHT & PHOTOSYNTHESIS

Absorption spectra of chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b and carotenoids — why leaves look green.

🌈

Reading the colour menu

White light is a buffet of colours. A pigment only absorbs the wavelengths whose photons can excite its electrons to a higher energy level — the photon's energy must match the energy gap between electron levels. Chlorophyll a absorbs strongly in the blue-violet (~430 nm) and red (~660–680 nm), and barely touches green. The green light is reflected and transmitted, which is exactly why leaves look green. An absorption spectrum is a graph of how much light a pigment absorbs at each wavelength — measured with a spectrophotometer.

AKA: the colour menu — chlorophyll orders blue and red; it reflects the green light.

⚡ Photon physics

A photon is absorbed only if its energy matches the gap between electron energy levels in the pigment. Blue photons are more energetic than red (shorter wavelength = more energy), but both can excite chlorophyll's electrons.

🍃 Chlorophyll a vs b

Chlorophyll a — the primary pigment — peaks near 430 nm and 662 nm. Chlorophyll b's peaks sit slightly differently (≈455 nm, ≈644 nm), so the pair together harvests a wider slice of the spectrum.

🥕 Carotenoids

Carotene and xanthophylls absorb blue-violet light that chlorophyll handles poorly, then pass the captured energy on. Bonus role: they protect the photosystems from excess light damage.

Nature of science: absorption spectra are quantitative data gathered with a spectrophotometer — measurable, reproducible evidence about which wavelengths a pigment can use, gained by varying the independent variable (wavelength) and recording the dependent variable (absorbance).
C1.3.6

ABSORPTION VS ACTION SPECTRA

Absorption spectra vs action spectra — what a pigment absorbs versus what the plant actually uses.

👯

Two graphs that look like twins

An absorption spectrum tells you what a single pigment absorbs (measured with a spectrophotometer). An action spectrum tells you how fast photosynthesis actually runs at each wavelength — measured by oxygen production or CO₂ consumption by the whole plant. Because the two graphs match, with peaks in blue-violet and red and a dip in green, you can conclude the wavelengths a plant absorbs are the wavelengths it uses to drive photosynthesis. Green light is absorbed poorly, so it drives little photosynthesis.

AKA: the twins — one shows the shopping list (absorption), the other shows what got cooked (action).

📉 Absorption spectrum

Measures how much light a pigment absorbs at each wavelength, using a spectrophotometer that shines one wavelength at a time through a pigment solution. A pigment property only.

📈 Action spectrum

Measures photosynthetic rate at each wavelength — the plant as a whole. Rate is tracked as O₂ production (bubble counts, oxygen electrode) or CO₂ consumption (pH change, IR gas analysis).

🔗 The conclusion

Overlaying the two shows the same double-peaked shape. Therefore the pigments' absorption pattern determines which wavelengths power photosynthesis — a classic correlation between two independently measured variables.

Spot the difference for the exam: absorption = what a pigment CAN absorb; action = what photosynthesis ACTUALLY does with each wavelength. Green light is absorbed little and drives little photosynthesis — yet most light that hits a leaf is green. That's why plants look green and why they're not more efficient.
C1.3.7

RATE/LIMITING FACTORS

Limiting factors in photosynthesis — light, temperature and CO₂ — and the experiments that reveal them.

🚦

Three taps, one sink

Photosynthesis has three main inputs you can turn up or down: light intensity, CO₂ concentration and temperature. Raise any one while the others stay fixed and the rate rises — until it plateaus, because something else has become the limiting factor. That's the whole idea: the factor in shortest supply sets the rate, like the narrowest bottleneck on a production line. Experiments (pondweed bubble counting, floating leaf discs, fermenters of algae) change one independent variable at a time and measure the rate.

AKA: the limiting factors — raise one and the rate rises, but only until the next factor becomes limiting.

💡 Light intensity

More light → more photons hit the photosystems → faster light-dependent reactions. Plateau: the Calvin cycle can't keep up, or CO₂/temperature limit it. In experiments, light intensity follows 1/distance².

🌫️ CO₂ concentration

More CO₂ → faster carbon fixation by Rubisco. Plateau: light or temperature now limits. Normal air is ~400 ppm CO₂ — plants can't use more than the air provides.

🌡️ Temperature

Photosynthesis is enzymatic — rate doubles-ish per 10 °C rise until the optimum, then enzymes (especially Rubisco's helpers) denature and the rate crashes. Night-time respiration also rises with temperature, cutting net gains.

Nature of science: the limiting-factor concept comes from controlled experiments — keep two factors constant, vary one, measure rate. Every conclusion is provisional and strengthened by repeating trials, replicating results and testing alternative explanations.
C1.3.8

THE CO₂ & O₂ BALANCE

CO₂ enrichment experiments — greenhouses and FACE — and what they show about carbon supply.

🏭

Raising the CO₂ concentration

If CO₂ is a limiting factor, then raising CO₂ should raise yield — and in greenhouses it does. Growers keep light and temperature near-optimal, then enrich the air with CO₂ (from combined heat-and-power boilers or compost) — often boosting tomato yields, especially on sunny days. FACE (Free-Air CO₂ Enrichment) takes the question outdoors: circles of towers release CO₂ over open plots of natural vegetation, holding ~550 ppm on the upwind side, while control plots get plain air. This tests how real ecosystems respond to the CO₂ levels predicted for coming decades — without walls or pots changing the result.

AKA: enriching the air with extra carbon dioxide and watching the rate rise.

🏡 Greenhouse logic

In a greenhouse, light and temperature are kept high, so CO₂ becomes the bottleneck. Enriching to 550+ ppm removes it and yields climb — tomatoes respond most on bright days when the other factors are not limiting.

🌲 FACE design

Circular arrays of towers emit CO₂ upwind of a plot, so wind carries a ~550 ppm plume over the vegetation. Paired control plots get plain air. Everything else — soil, weather, neighbours — stays natural.

🧠 What FACE found

Early boost in growth often fades: extra carbon needs nitrogen and phosphorus, and other resources eventually limit. Long-running sites (forests in Australia, England) keep monitoring — the story is provisional and still being tested.

Exam angle: remember FACE is about natural, open-air conditions (no enclosure) with control plots — that's what separates it from greenhouse enrichment. And note the greenhouse result supports the limiting-factor model from Act 6: raise CO₂, and rate rises until the next bottleneck bites.

THE LIGHT-DEPENDENT REACTIONS

Photosystems as antenna arrays — why many pigment molecules together capture more light than a single chlorophyll molecule.

🔆

One pigment is not enough; an array captures far more

A single chlorophyll molecule intercepts only a few photons per second — far too few to run a reaction centre. So the thylakoid membrane packs photosystems: arrays of roughly 100 chlorophyll molecules plus ~30 accessory pigments (carotenes, xanthophylls) that act as a light-harvesting antenna. When any antenna pigment absorbs a photon, the excitation energy hops from molecule to molecule in femtoseconds until it reaches the reaction centre — a special pair of chlorophyll molecules. There the energy ejects an electron, which the cell captures for chemistry. The reaction centre's identity gives the photosystem its name: P680 in photosystem II (absorbs ~680 nm) and P700 in photosystem I (~700 nm).

AKA: the photosystems — hundreds of pigment molecules funnelling light energy into one reaction centre.

📡 Antenna array

~100 chlorophylls + ~30 accessory pigments catch photons across a wider range of wavelengths than any single molecule could. Excitation energy hops pigment-to-pigment in femtoseconds (10⁻¹⁵ s) — no chemical intermediates needed.

🎯 Reaction centre

A special pair of chlorophylls traps the energy and ejects an electron. The electron goes to the transport chain; the pigment grabs a replacement electron (from water in PSII, from the chain in PSI).

🔢 P680 vs P700

The numbers are the wavelengths (nm) where each centre absorbs best. P680 (PSII) is the electron donor to the chain; P700 (PSI) hands electrons to NADP reductase. Both are needed, working in series.

Why arrays matter (exam favourite): a single pigment molecule absorbs too few photons and too narrow a wavelength range to be useful. The array increases photon capture by about two orders of magnitude and lets accessory pigments feed energy in — so even dim light keeps photosynthesis running.

PHOTOSYSTEMS & ELECTRON TRANSPORT

Photolysis — light-driven splitting of water in photosystem II, and the electrons, protons and oxygen it releases.

⚡

The most powerful oxidant in biology

When P680 emits an electron, it becomes positively charged — and hungry. That electron-hungry P680⁺ is one of the strongest oxidising agents found in living things, powerful enough to rip electrons off water. This happens in the oxygen-evolving complex (OEC), a cluster of manganese, calcium and oxygen atoms tucked against the thylakoid space. Two water molecules are split:

AKA: the splitting of water — providing electrons and releasing oxygen as a by-product.

🥾 The electron relay

Every photon-excited electron P680 emits must be replaced or the photosystem jams. Water is the ultimate electron donor — the only reason plants can keep losing electrons all day is that they have an infinite supply of water to cannibalise.

🔋 Gradient fuel

The 4H⁺ dumped into the thylakoid space per water pair are free protons for the chemiosmotic gradient — photolysis contributes to ATP production without costing the electron chain anything.

🪨 Planetary scale

Photolysis's waste O₂ rusted Earth's oceans (banded iron formations, ~2.5–2.8 billion years ago) before oxygen filled the air. Photosynthesis is why there's fire, rust and aerobic animals at all.

Nature of science: the OEC's manganese-calcium cluster was worked out partly by comparing structures across species and partly by spectroscopy — an example of how models of molecular machines are built from convergent evidence, then revised as techniques improve.

PROTON GRADIENTS & ATP SYNTHASE

Chemiosmosis and ATP synthase — plus the reduction of NADP by photosystem I.

🌊

Electrons fall, protons climb, ATP pours

Electrons from PSII travel down the electron transport chain (plastoquinone → cytochrome b₆f → plastocyanin), losing energy at each step. That energy pumps protons from the stroma into the thylakoid space. Because the lumen is tiny, even a few dozen protons build a steep concentration gradient — the proton motive force. Protons can only flow back to the stroma through ATP synthase, and that flow spins the enzyme like a turbine, making ATP from ADP + Pi. This is chemiosmosis. Meanwhile PSI passes electrons down a short chain to NADP reductase, which reduces NADP to reduced NADP using 2 electrons + H⁺. In cyclic photophosphorylation, PSI's electrons loop back to the chain instead — producing ATP alone.

AKA: the proton river — a one-way current of H⁺ that turns the waterwheel of ATP synthase.

🔄 Electron transport chain

Carriers in the membrane pass electrons downhill, using the released energy to pump protons into the thylakoid space. P680's electrons enter; fresh ones arrive from photolysis; PSI's electrons finish at NADP reductase.

🌀 ATP synthase

A rotary enzyme: H⁺ flowing back to the stroma turns its rotor, and each turn drives ADP + Pi → ATP. This coupling of proton flow to ATP synthesis is chemiosmosis — the same trick mitochondria use.

🔋 Two batteries

Light-dependent reactions output ATP (energy) and reduced NADP (reducing power). NADP is NAD plus an extra phosphate — its reduced form carries H⁺ and electrons to power the Calvin cycle's GP → TP step.

Exam gold — the Jagendorf evidence: chloroplast thylakoids were incubated in an acid bath (pH ~4) in the dark, so H⁺ diffused in. Dropped into alkaline buffer with ADP + Pi, they produced a burst of ATP with no light at all — because the artificial gradient alone drove ATP synthase. That experiment nailed chemiosmosis.

THE CALVIN CYCLE

The thylakoid as a functional system — one membrane doing photolysis, electron transport, proton pumping and NADP reduction, with the Calvin cycle waiting in the stroma.

🏗️

One membrane, four jobs, perfect zoning

grana · stroma lamellae · lumen · stroma · zonation of the light-dependent reactions

Disc-shaped thylakoids are stacked into grana, and unstacked stroma lamellae connect the stacks. The membrane of every thylakoid hosts the whole light-dependent factory: photolysis, electron transport, proton pumping for chemiosmosis and NADP reduction all happen here. The tiny volume of the thylakoid space (lumen) is the secret weapon — a small lumen means even a modest number of protons creates a steep concentration gradient, so ATP synthesis needs only a few dozen H⁺.

There is real zoning: PSII arrays and the cytochrome b₆f complex concentrate in the grana; PSI arrays and ATP synthases concentrate in the stroma lamellae. The fluid stroma outside holds the Calvin cycle enzymes — Rubisco and friends — and the products of the light-dependent reactions (ATP, reduced NADP) diffuse out to feed them.

🟦 Grana

Stacks of disc thylakoids packed with PSII arrays and cytochrome b₆f. Photolysis's protons are dumped into the tiny lumen here — the gradient starts building at the very first photosystem.

🟪 Stroma lamellae

Unstacked membrane extensions holding PSI arrays and ATP synthases. The proton river flows along the lumen from grana to lamellae, then spins the synthases.

🟩 Stroma

The fluid around the membranes: home of the Calvin cycle. It imports ATP + reduced NADP, exports triose phosphate, and recycles ADP + NADP back to the membrane.

Think of it as compartmentalisation: PSII and ATP synthase are in the thylakoid membrane, but all connected to the same proton gradient (the lumen). — but they're all plumbed to the same hot-water loop (the lumen). The exam wants you to say where each light-dependent reaction happens and why the small lumen matters.

THE CALVIN CYCLE — CARBON FIXATION

The Calvin cycle — Rubisco's carboxylation of RuBP, the reduction of GP to TP, and the regeneration of RuBP.

👨‍🍳

Rubisco and the Calvin cycle

Rubisco (RuBP carboxylase) · RuBP · carboxylation · GP (glycerate 3-phosphate) · TP (triose phosphate) · regeneration

The Calvin cycle runs in the stroma and uses the ATP + reduced NADP from the light-dependent reactions. Its key enzyme is Rubisco (ribulose bisphosphate carboxylase) — the most abundant enzyme on Earth, yet a slow one: it fixes roughly 3 CO₂ per second, so plants need enormous concentrations of it. Station one, carboxylation: Rubisco joins CO₂ onto ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP, 5C); the unstable 6-carbon intermediate instantly splits into two molecules of glycerate 3-phosphate (GP, 3C). Station two, reduction: GP is converted to triose phosphate (TP, 3C) using ATP for energy and reduced NADP for hydrogen/electrons. Station three, regeneration: five of every six TP molecules are recycled into three RuBP molecules, using more ATP.

One turn fixes one CO₂ (3 ATP + 2 reduced NADP). Six turns bank one hexose — 18 ATP + 12 reduced NADP per glucose. Melvin Calvin worked the cycle out with ¹⁴C-labelled CO₂ and won the Nobel Prize in 1961.

🥇 Station 1 · Carboxylation

Rubisco fixes CO₂ onto RuBP (5C) forming an unstable 6C intermediate that splits into 2 GP (3C). This is literally how inorganic carbon enters the biosphere.

🥈 Station 2 · Reduction

GP is reduced to TP: ATP supplies energy, reduced NADP supplies hydrogen and electrons. The NADP oxidised here returns to the thylakoid to be re-reduced by PSI.

🥉 Station 3 · Regeneration

5 of every 6 TP are converted back into 3 RuBP (5 × 3C = 3 × 5C), powered by ATP. Only the leftover 1 TP (one fixed carbon) leaves the line per turn.

Nature of Science: Calvin and Benson fed ¹⁴C-labelled CO₂ to algae, stopped the reaction at different times, and identified which labelled compounds appeared first — building the cycle from order-of-appearance evidence. Bassham later showed light-off makes GP rise and RuBP fall (and vice versa for CO₂-off), confirming which steps need the light-dependent products.
Exam gold — the numbers: per turn: 1 RuBP + 1 CO₂ → 2 GP → 2 TP (costs 2 ATP + 2 reduced NADP) → 5/6 TP regenerate RuBP (costs 1 more ATP per turn on average). Per glucose: 6 turns, 18 ATP, 12 reduced NADP. And remember: the cycle doesn't make glucose directly — it makes TP, and 2 TP join to form hexose phosphate.
AHL · C1.3.18 – C1.3.19

FACTORS CARBON & ENERGY USE

What photosynthesis actually produces — and why cutting light or CO₂ shuts the whole process down.

🍽️

From triose phosphate to the whole menu

sucrose · starch · triglycerides · amino acids · nitrate · interdependence of the two reaction sets

All the carbon compounds a photosynthesizing organism contains are built from Calvin cycle intermediates. Triose phosphate feeds: glucose → sucrose (the transport sugar shipped to the rest of the plant), starch (stored in the chloroplast), and, via glycolysis-like steps, fatty acids + glycerol → triglycerides (seed oils, membrane lipids). Amino acids — all 20 of them — are made from GP/TP skeletons by adding nitrogen from nitrate or ammonium; phosphate and sulfate are needed for other compounds. So every carbon in the plant traces back to a Calvin cycle intermediate.

The two halves of photosynthesis are interdependent. The light-dependent reactions (thylakoid) make ATP + reduced NADP; the light-independent reactions (stroma) spend them. Switch the light off and the Calvin cycle grinds to a halt within seconds — ATP and reduced NADP are used up and not replaced. Remove the CO₂ and the chain runs backwards into disaster: no GP forms, so reduced NADP can't be re-oxidised, NADP runs out, the electron chain backs up, and PSII can't unload its electrons — photosystem II stops working. Each half needs the other to recycle its products.

🥖 Sucrose & starch

Sucrose is the plant's transport sugar — water-soluble, shipped via phloem to roots, fruits and growing tips. Starch is the storage polymer, packed into the chloroplast when photosynthesis outruns demand (and remobilised at night).

🧈 Fats & proteins

TP feeds glycolysis-like pathways to fatty acids; glycerol also comes from TP — together they build triglycerides (seed oils, membranes). Amino acids get their skeletons from GP/TP and their nitrogen from nitrate or ammonium in the soil.

🔌 The mutual hostage situation

Light-dependent reactions need NADP back from the Calvin cycle; the Calvin cycle needs ATP + reduced NADP from the light. No light → no ATP/NADPH → the Calvin cycle stops. No CO₂ → no GP → NADP not recycled → electron chain backs up → PSII stops.

Exam gold — the causal chain (C1.3.19): the examiner wants the mechanism, not just \"they need each other\". CO₂ removed → carboxylation stops → no GP to reduce → reduced NADP accumulates unused → free NADP runs out → PSI can't pass electrons on → the electron transport chain fills up → PSII can't donate its excited electrons → PSII stops. Light removed → ATP + reduced NADP are consumed by the still-running Calvin cycle and not replaced → reduction stops → cycle halts within seconds.

✍ SELF-TEST

10 questions. Pick an answer and you'll instantly see why.

1. Photosynthesis is fundamentally…

✅ Light energy is transformed into chemical energy in carbon compounds — carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids. This supplies most of the chemical energy needed by life in ecosystems.
❌ Photosynthesis runs the other way: light energy IN, chemical energy OUT (in carbon compounds). It's not about glowing or wind.

2. Which is the correct word equation for photosynthesis?

✅ Carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen. Glucose + oxygen → CO₂ + water is respiration (the reverse). The hydrogen in glucose comes from splitting water.
❌ The glucose + oxygen version is aerobic respiration. Photosynthesis needs carbon dioxide AND water on the left, glucose AND oxygen on the right.

3. The oxygen released by photosynthesis comes from…

✅ ¹⁸O isotope-labelling proved it: label the water and the O₂ bubbles come out labelled; label the CO₂ and they don't. Photolysis: 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻.
❌ It's a classic trap — oxygen comes from water. CO₂'s carbon ends up in glucose; CO₂'s oxygen ends up in water, not in the gas bubbles.

4. In paper/thin-layer chromatography of leaf pigments, the pigment with the highest Rf value is…

✅ Rf = distance moved by pigment ÷ distance moved by solvent. Carotene travels furthest with the solvent (0.98), so it's the top band; xanthophylls lag behind.
❌ Chlorophyll b (0.42) and xanthophyll 2 (0.15) travel less far. The highest Rf means the pigment rode closest to the solvent front.

5. Leaves look green because…

✅ Chlorophyll's absorption spectrum peaks in blue-violet (~430 nm) and red (~660 nm) with a deep trough in the green — green photons aren't absorbed, so they bounce off or pass through.
❌ Green is the trough — the wavelengths chlorophyll does NOT absorb. Carotenoids absorb blue-violet light, not green.

6. An action spectrum shows…

✅ Action spectrum = photosynthetic rate per wavelength, measured via O₂ production or CO₂ consumption. It matches the absorption spectrum's peaks and troughs — what's absorbed is what's used.
❌ That's an absorption spectrum. The action spectrum measures the plant's actual output per wavelength — the two are compared to prove absorbed light drives photosynthesis.

7. In the pondweed bubble-count experiment, if you vary CO₂ concentration (using sodium hydrogencarbonate), the independent variable is…

✅ Independent variable = what the experimenter changes (here, CO₂ concentration). Bubble count is the dependent variable you measure; temperature is a control that must be kept constant.
❌ Bubble count is the dependent variable — the measurement. Temperature should be controlled (fixed), not varied. The thing you change is CO₂ concentration.

8. FACE experiments are designed to…

✅ FACE (Free-Air CO₂ Enrichment) releases CO₂ from tower rings on the upwind side to hold ~550 ppm over natural vegetation, while control plots get plain air — testing carbon storage, nutrient limits and biodiversity impacts.
❌ That's greenhouse CO₂ enrichment (a different technique). FACE is specifically open-air — no chamber walls — so results reflect real ecosystems.

9. In the Calvin cycle, Rubisco catalyses…

✅ Rubisco joins CO₂ onto RuBP (5C); the unstable 6C intermediate splits into 2 GP (3C). Glucose is never made directly — and the GP → TP reduction is a separate step using ATP + reduced NADP.
❌ Glucose forms only after six turns and further joining of TP molecules. The GP → TP reduction needs ATP + reduced NADP but isn't Rubisco's job — Rubisco does carboxylation.

10. If CO₂ is suddenly removed from a brightly lit chloroplast, photosystem II stops working because…

✅ The causal chain: no CO₂ → no carboxylation → no GP → reduced NADP accumulates unused → free NADP is exhausted → PSI and the chain can't pass electrons on → PSII's excited electrons have nowhere to go → PSII stops.
❌ Light absorption still works fine — the problem is downstream. The electron chain backs up because NADP can't be recycled, jamming PSII.

  • ✓C1.3.1-2Light energy is transformed into chemical energy in carbon compounds (carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids), supplying most of the chemical energy for life in ecosystems. Word equation: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen; the hydrogen in glucose comes from splitting water.
  • ✓C1.3.3Oxygen is a by-product of photosynthesis, produced by photolysis of water — NOT from carbon dioxide. Evidence: isotope labelling with ¹⁸O in water produces labelled O₂; ¹⁸O in CO₂ does not.
  • ✓C1.3.4Photosynthetic pigments are separated by paper/thin-layer chromatography; Rf = distance moved by pigment ÷ distance moved by solvent. Order: carotene 0.98, phaeophytin 0.81, chlorophyll a 0.59, chlorophyll b 0.42, xanthophylls 0.28 / 0.15.
  • ✓C1.3.5Absorption spectra of chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b and carotenoids: peaks in blue-violet and red, trough in green (reflected → leaves look green). A photon's energy must match the electron's energy gap for absorption to excite it.
  • ✓C1.3.6Absorption spectrum (pigment, % absorbed) vs action spectrum (whole organism, rate of photosynthesis per wavelength via O₂ production or CO₂ consumption). Their matching peaks/troughs show absorbed wavelengths are the ones used.
  • ✓C1.3.7Limiting factors: light intensity, CO₂ concentration, temperature. Techniques: pondweed bubble counting (CO₂ via sodium hydrogencarbonate), floating leaf discs (light via 1/distance²), fermenter with algae (temperature, O₂ electrode/pH probe). Independent vs dependent variables; hypotheses need repeated testing.
  • ✓C1.3.8CO₂ enrichment: greenhouses dose CO₂ (from CHP boilers/compost) on sunny days to raise yields beyond ~400 ppm; FACE (Free-Air CO₂ Enrichment) uses tower rings releasing CO₂ to ~550 ppm over open-air plots with control plots releasing air — testing carbon storage, nutrient limits and biodiversity in natural ecosystems.
  • ✓C1.3.9-11Photosystems are arrays: antenna complexes of ~100 chlorophyll + ~30 accessory pigments funnel excitation energy (femtoseconds) to a reaction centre of special chlorophylls — P680 in PSII, P700 in PSI (named for absorption peaks). A single pigment molecule is useless alone; >99% efficiency in low light. Photolysis: oxygen-evolving complex (Mn/Ca/O cluster) splits 2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻, replacing P680's electrons and feeding protons to the gradient; O₂ from early cyanobacteria formed banded iron formations.
  • ✓C1.3.12-14Chemiosmosis: the electron transport chain pumps protons into the thylakoid space; the proton gradient (proton motive force) drives ATP synthase; NADP is reduced to reduced NADP by PSI via NADP reductase (NADP + 2e⁻ + H⁺). Cyclic photophosphorylation (PSI only) makes ATP alone. Thylakoid system: PSII + photolysis in grana, PSI + ATP synthase in stroma lamellae, Calvin cycle in the stroma; small lumen volume makes steep gradients cheap.
  • ✓C1.3.15-19Calvin cycle (stroma): Rubisco carboxylates RuBP + CO₂ → unstable 6C → 2 GP; GP reduced to TP with ATP + reduced NADP; 5 of 6 TP regenerate 3 RuBP using ATP; 1 TP exits per turn (6 turns + 18 ATP + 12 reduced NADP → 1 glucose). End products: sucrose (transport), starch (storage), fatty acids/glycerol → triglycerides, all 20 amino acids (nitrate/ammonium nitrogen). Interdependence: no light → Calvin stalls in seconds; no CO₂ → no GP → NADP not recycled → chain backs up → PSII cannot function.
