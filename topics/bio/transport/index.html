B3.2 TRANSPORT

How the circulatory system moves blood, the role of blood in transport, and how plants move water (xylem) and sugar (phloem).

SL + AHL 18 statements Blood · Xylem · Phloem Circulation · Transpiration
B3.2.1

BLOOD — A TRANSPORT MEDIUM

Capillaries are the private corridors that actually actually carry out exchange: they are ultra-narrow, heavily branched, and built so thin that every single cell can exchange substances with the bloodstream directly.

🫁

Why narrow beats wide

~10 µm · capillary network · endothelium · basement membrane · fenestrations

Capillaries are the narrowest blood vessels — roughly 10 µm across, barely wide enough for red blood cells to squeeze through single file. What they lack in width they make up in numbers: they branch and rejoin into dense networks, and because each branch is narrow, the network has an enormous total surface area for exchange. A few wide tubes could never match the area that thousands of narrow ones deliver.

The wall is just one layer of endothelium cells sitting on a basement membrane — a gel of fibrous proteins that acts as a filter. Pores between the cells let plasma leak out into the tissue (that's how tissue fluid forms), while blood cells stay in. In places needing lightning-fast exchange, like the kidney's glomerulus or the small intestine, the endothelium has fenestrations — very large pores — so bigger volumes can flood through. Almost every cell in the body sits close enough to a capillary to swap gases, nutrients and wastes with the blood.

FIG 1 — The capillaries: narrow (~10 µm), heavily branched capillaries maximise surface area; a one-cell-thick endothelium on a basement membrane filter allows plasma (not blood cells) to leak out, with fenestrations boosting exchange where speed matters. The lens and cornea of the eye have no capillaries at all — they must stay transparent.
Mnemonic — "Corridor logic": many narrow vessels exchange more efficiently than one wide vessel. And remember: exchange happens ONLY in capillaries — arteries and veins are just the transport vessels between the heart and the exchange surfaces.
B3.2.2

ARTERIES, VEINS & CAPILLARIES

Spot the difference: arteries are the reinforced tunnels carrying pulses of high-pressure blood away from the heart; veins are the flexible return corridors bringing the low-pressure stream back. The micrograph tells you which is which.

🩸

Five structural differences that decide a micrograph

wall thickness · lumen width · circular vs flattened · corrugated inner surface · visible fibres

Arteries carry blood away from the heart under pulses of high pressure, so they need thick, muscular, elastic walls. Veins carry blood back to the heart as a low-pressure, steady stream, so their walls can be thin and flexible. In a microscope slide the rule is simple: compare wall thickness to lumen width.

The five code-breakers: (1) arteries have a much thicker wall; (2) arteries have a narrower lumen (the central hole) relative to the wall; (3) arteries are circular in section while veins are circular or flattened; (4) arteries show a corrugated inner surface (elastic tissue puckered after death); (5) fibres are clearly visible in artery walls but barely visible in veins. Find one corrugated inner surface + thick wall = artery. Thin wall + wide, floppy lumen = vein.

FIG 2 — The corridor codes: arteries show a thick wall, narrow circular lumen, corrugated inner surface and visible fibres; veins show a thin wall, wide (often flattened) lumen and a smooth inner surface. In exams the giveaway is wall thickness relative to the lumen.
Micrograph skill: when asked to distinguish artery from vein in a photomicrograph, state the wall thickness relative to the lumen first — that's the mark. Then add corrugation or fibres as supporting evidence. Never say "the artery has more blood" — you can't tell from a section.
B3.2.3

WALL STRUCTURES & BLOOD PRESSURE

The heart slams blood into arteries at high pressure, every beat. The artery wall is engineered like a pressure pipe — three layers that stretch, recoil and hold so the pulse never bursts the system.

🔧

Three layers, three jobs

tunica externa · tunica media · tunica intima · elastic recoil · collagen

Artery walls have three layers. The tunica externa (outermost) is made of collagen fibres — tough, inextensible material that stops the wall from bulging or bursting under high pressure. The tunica media (middle) is the muscle layer: smooth muscle plus a huge amount of elastic fibres (up to ~50% of the wall's dry mass in the big arteries near the heart). The tunica intima (innermost) is the smooth endothelium lining the lumen.

Why the elastic fibres? Each heartbeat inflates the artery (systole), then the elastic fibres recoil during diastole, squeezing blood onward even while the heart relaxes. That elastic recoil does three things: it smooths the flow into a steadier stream, it maintains pressure between beats so organs keep getting blood, and it reduces the heart's workload — the artery acts like a secondary pump. The narrow lumen helps too: a smaller bore keeps pressure and flow velocity high.

FIG 3 — The pressure pipe: collagen (externa) prevents bursting, elastic fibres + smooth muscle (media) stretch at systole and recoil at diastole so blood keeps flowing between beats, and the endothelium (intima) keeps the surface slick. The narrow lumen maintains high pressure and velocity.
Mnemonic — "Ex-Media-Intima": External collagen (tough), Medial muscle + elastic (the recoil), Intimal endothelium (slick). If an artery loses its elastic fibres it becomes rigid — blood pressure spikes and the heart works overtime.
B3.2.4

THE CARDIAC CYCLE & PULSE

Every heartbeat sends a pulse — a wave of pressure along the arteries. Count the pulses and you've counted the beats. Find the right corridor, use the right fingers, and the circulation makes sense.

🎵

Where to feel the beat

radial pulse · carotid pulse · 30 s × 2 · pulse oximeter

A pulse is the pressure wave that travels along arteries each time the left ventricle contracts. You can feel it anywhere an artery runs close to the surface over a bone. The classic spots: the radial pulse on the wrist (on the thumb side — the side nearest your thumb) and the carotid pulse in the neck (in the groove on either side of the windpipe).

Technique traps that cost marks: use two or three fingertips, not your thumb — the thumb has its own pulse and you'll count yours instead of the patient's. Press lightly (hard pressure squashes the artery and kills the pulse). Count for 30 seconds and multiply by 2 for beats per minute (or count a full minute for accuracy). Pulse rate at rest is roughly 60–100 bpm, and it rises with exercise, fever, fear or caffeine.

Modern clubs use a digital pulse oximeter clipped on a fingertip: it shines red and infrared light through the finger, and measures how light absorption changes as blood volume pulses with each beat — giving both pulse rate and blood oxygen saturation.

FIG 4 — The beat counter: radial pulse (wrist, thumb side) and carotid pulse (neck groove beside the windpipe) are the two classic sites. Two or three fingertips, light pressure, 30 s × 2. A pulse oximeter uses red + infrared light to read the pulse digitally.
Mnemonic — "Radial is Radical, Carotid is in the Collar": wrist = radial (thumb side), neck = carotid (beside the windpipe). Never your thumb, never heavy pressure, always time it properly.
B3.2.5

VALVES ENSURE ONE-WAY FLOW

Blood leaving the heart is under pressure from the heart's contraction. Blood coming back has almost nothing — just thin walls, pocket valves and the squeeze of your own muscles. Welcome to the one-way door system.

🚪

Three tricks that beat gravity

pocket valves · skeletal muscle pump · thin flexible walls · ~80% of blood

Veins carry blood back to the heart under low pressure — and often against gravity (hello, legs). They solve this with three adaptations. First, pocket valves: cup-shaped flaps of endothelium pointing toward the heart. Blood flowing the right way pushes them flat against the wall; blood trying to flow backwards fills the cups, making them bulge and seal the lumen. One-way doors, every few centimetres.

Second, the skeletal muscle pump: vein walls are thin and flexible, so when surrounding muscles contract (walking, running, even fidgeting), they squeeze the vein flat and push blood toward the heart — the valves make sure it can't squirt backwards. Third, those same thin walls mean veins can hold a lot: at rest about 80% of your blood volume sits in the veins, ready to be mobilised (e.g. during exercise, when veins constrict to boost the return flow).

When pocket valves wear out — from age, pregnancy or standing jobs — blood pools in the veins of the legs and stretches them into varicose veins. The door stops closing; gravity wins.

FIG 5 — The one-way door: pocket valves open for forward flow and cup shut against backflow; skeletal muscles squeeze the thin flexible walls, pushing blood toward the heart against gravity. ~80% of blood volume sits in veins at rest; valve failure → varicose veins.
Nature of Science: the muscle-pump story is a beautiful example of how physiology is tested by measurement — venous return rises when leg muscles contract, drops when they're still (long flights → swollen ankles). Quantitative evidence converted a hypothesis into a mechanism.
B3.2.6

BLOOD VESSEL DISEASE

The heart's own blood supply comes from the coronary arteries — the first branches off the aorta. When the coronary arteries supplying the heart become blocked, the heart muscle is damaged. And correlation is NOT the same as causation.

🚑

When the heart's own blood supply is blocked

coronary arteries · atheroma · occlusion · thrombosis · myocardial infarction

The coronary arteries are the first branches off the aorta (right coronary + left coronary, which splits into two main branches — three main arteries in total). They wrap around the heart muscle, delivering oxygen and glucose to the hardest-working muscle in the body.

If atheroma forms — fatty deposits (lipids including cholesterol, later hardened with calcium salts) building up in the artery wall — the lumen narrows. A narrowed coronary causes angina (chest pain on exertion) and shortness of breath; a fully blocked one causes myocardial infarction (heart attack) — the muscle downstream dies from oxygen starvation. Complete blockage is usually a thrombus (blood clot) forming on the rough surface of an atheroma, and high blood pressure makes clots more likely by damaging the endothelium.

Risk factors: hypertension, smoking, saturated fat, obesity, excess salt, alcohol, sedentary lifestyle, genetic predisposition, age. Notice how many are lifestyle — that's why the exam loves asking which factors a patient could change.

FIG 6 — The blocked coronary arteries (first branches of the aorta) supply the heart; atheroma narrows them, thrombus blocks them, and the muscle starves. Data links saturated fat intake to CHD death rate with a strong positive correlation — but correlation is not causation.
Nature of Science — correlation vs causation: a correlation coefficient between −1 and +1 quantifies how closely two variables move together. Strong correlation (e.g. saturated fat ↔ CHD, r close to +1) shows association, not cause. To claim causation you need more: a plausible mechanism, controlled studies, and elimination of confounding variables (smoking, exercise, income). This exact distinction is a guaranteed exam favourite.
B3.2.7 · B3.2.17

FLUID & WATER TRANSPORT IN PLANTS

How does water climb 100 m up a tree with no pump at the top? The leaf doesn't push — it pulls. Transpiration generates the tension, and a continuous rope of cohesive water molecules carries the pull all the way down to the roots.

💧

Transpiration pull: the cohesion-tension mechanism

evaporation · capillary action · tension · cohesion · adhesion · cavitation

Water evaporates from the cell walls of the spongy mesophyll in the leaf and diffuses out through the stomata. The cell walls are a mesh of cellulose fibres — water clings to them by adhesion (hydrogen bonds), so as the outer water evaporates, capillary action drags more water along the mesh, like a paper towel wicking up a spill. That pull is transmitted to the xylem sap, putting it under tension (negative pressure).

Here's the genius bit: water molecules stick to each other by cohesion — hydrogen bonds make the whole xylem column behave like one long rope. When the top of the rope is pulled (tension from evaporation), the entire column moves upward, right down to the roots. Because the column is continuous, the pull isn't limited by atmospheric pressure (which alone can only lift water ~10.4 m). That's how 100 m trees work: cohesion, not suction. The process is passive — the energy comes from the sun (heat) driving evaporation, not from living cells pumping.

If the column breaks — a bubble forms (called cavitation) — that pipe is out of action until it refills. Plants pay for it by building redundancy: many vessels in parallel.

FIG 7 — The water pipe network: evaporation from mesophyll cell walls → capillary action through cellulose → tension in the xylem → the cohesive water column is pulled up like a rope, with adhesion to the walls. Passive (sun-driven), beats the 10.4 m suction limit, and cavitation breaks pipes.
Mnemonic — "TEAC": Transpiration (evaporation) → Energy from sun → Adhesion to walls + capillary action → Cohesion pulls the column. Tension is just what the column feels when it's being pulled.

B3.2.17 · The Spring Rise — root pressure

Transpiration pull usually does the work — but sometimes it can't (at night, in high humidity, or in spring before leaves have opened). Then the roots take over with root pressure. Living root cells actively pump mineral ions into the xylem vessels, making the sap hypertonic. Water then follows by osmosis, building up positive pressure in the xylem that pushes sap upward — often enough to make cut stems "bleed" sap in spring. Root pressure works by pushing; transpiration pull works by pulling. Two mechanisms, same pipes, different seasons.

Exam favourite: "Suggest how water moves up a 100 m tree." Answer chain: evaporation at leaves → tension → cohesion keeps column continuous → pull transmitted down → water absorbed at roots. Mentioning the 10.4 m limit of suction is the mark-winner that shows you know WHY cohesion matters.
B3.2.8

WATER & MINERAL UPTAKE BY ROOTS

Xylem is the water pipe network, and it's built by specialist pipe fitters: dead cells stripped of everything except a rigid, waterproof, ringed wall. Perfect for carrying tension without collapsing.

🧱

Dead cells, perfect pipes

xylem vessels · lignified walls · pits · end walls removed · rings and spirals

Xylem vessels are formed from columns of cells stacked end to end. As they mature, the cells die: their contents (cytoplasm, nucleus, membranes) break down, and their end walls are largely removed — leaving one long, hollow, continuous tube with minimal resistance to flow. Dead cells can't block the pipe with their own biology, and they can't be damaged by the negative pressure inside.

The walls are thickened with lignin, a tough waterproof polymer, in rings or spirals (in young, still-stretching vessels) or more extensively with pits (in mature vessels). Lignified walls do two jobs: they withstand tension — the negative pressure of transpiration would crush a soft-walled tube — and they keep water in. The pits (unthickened gaps) are where water actually moves in and out of the vessel to the surrounding tissues.

Because the cells are dead, transport through xylem is passive — no energy input, just tension, cohesion and the sun. The same columns also give mechanical support: that's why woody stems stand up.

FIG 8 — The pipe fitters: xylem vessels are columns of dead cells with end walls removed (continuous tubes), ring/spiral lignin thickenings (tension-proof, stretchable in youth), and pits for water entry/exit. Flow is entirely passive.
Mnemonic — "Dead Hollow Lignified Pitted" — four words that answer every xylem adaptation question. Compare with phloem (alive, nucleated companion cells) — the exam loves the contrast.
B3.2.9 · B3.2.10

ACTIVE TRANSPORT & TISSUE FLUID

Plant transport follows a route plan. Stems put the transport corridors in a ring near the edge; roots put them in the middle, xylem star-shaped. Learn the two plans and the microscope becomes predictable.

📐

Stem vs root: two layouts, one logic

plan diagram · epidermis · cortex · vascular bundles · pith · star xylem

Stem (dicot) TS: a ring of vascular bundles sits just inside the epidermis, surrounding a big central pith, with cortex between the bundles and the surface. In each bundle the xylem faces the centre (inside) and the phloem faces the outside, with cambium between them. Putting the bundles in a ring gives the stem strength against bending.

Root (dicot) TS: the vascular tissue is central, with a star-shaped xylem core and phloem in the gaps between the star's points. A ring of endodermis wraps the central cylinder, the cortex (large thin-walled cells) bulks out the root, and the epidermis bears root hairs that absorb water and minerals.

A plan diagram is a low-power map: single lines show tissue boundaries, no individual cells. Epidermis (waterproofing), cortex (support/photosynthesis), vascular bundles, pith — that's the whole stem story; root swaps in star-xylem, endodermis and root hairs.

FIG 9 — The floor plans: stem — epidermis, cortex, ring of vascular bundles (xylem inner, phloem outer, cambium between), pith centre. Root — epidermis with root hairs, cortex, endodermis, central star-shaped xylem with phloem between the points. Draw plan diagrams with single boundary lines only.
Drawing skill: plan diagrams are drawn from low-power views — one sharp line per tissue interface, no cell detail, faint shading for density (xylem = densest). Label xylem/phloem positions correctly: stem = xylem in, phloem out; root = star xylem centre, phloem between arms.

TISSUE FLUID, LYMPH & DRAINAGE

Every minute, the corridors leak. Plasma filters out of the arterial end of capillaries, bathes the cells, and mostly drains back in at the venous end — while the lymph system mops up the overflow. That's your tissue fluid tap.

🚿

Pressure filtration at the tap

tissue fluid · pressure filtration · plasma proteins · lymph · oedema

Tissue fluid is the fluid that surrounds and bathes body cells — their internal environment. It forms by pressure filtration: at the arteriole end of a capillary, blood pressure is high enough to force fluid (plasma minus its large proteins) out through the wall pores into the tissue. Along the bed, blood pressure falls, so at the venule end the lower pressure lets most fluid drain back in. The ~20 dm³ of tissue fluid produced per day: ~17 dm³ returns to capillaries, ~3 dm³ is mopped up by the lymph system.

Plasma vs tissue fluid: tissue fluid is plasma that has been filtered — so it has no large plasma proteins (they're too big for the pores) and no blood cells. Everything else — glucose, amino acids, mineral ions, hormones, O₂, CO₂ — is present. Cells exchange with the tissue fluid: O₂ and glucose diffuse in; CO₂ and wastes diffuse out.

Lymph: the excess tissue fluid drains into blind-ended lymph vessels — thin-walled, highly permeable, with valves (one-way, like veins). It flows through lymph nodes (filtering) and eventually empties via lymph ducts into the subclavian veins, rejoining the blood. If drainage fails, fluid accumulates → oedema (swelling).

FIG 10 — The tissue-fluid tap: high pressure at the arteriole end filters plasma out; lower pressure at the venule end recovers most of it; blind-ended lymph vessels with valves drain the excess back to the blood. Failure to drain = oedema.
Mnemonic — "OUT at the start, IN at the end, LYMPH for the rest": arteriole end pushes fluid out (high pressure), venule end lets it back in (low pressure), lymph vessels collect the ~3 dm³ surplus. Large plasma proteins never leave — they stay in the blood.

THE HEART & CARDIAC MUSCLE

The heart pumps blood, and the beat sequence is the cardiac cycle: atria contract, ventricles contract, valves close — twice per second, every second. This is the heart's unidirectional flow system.

🎧

One-way flow, built in

cardiac muscle · pacemaker · atrioventricular valves · semilunar valves · septum

Cardiac muscle is specialised: cells are branched and connected, so electrical signals spread rapidly and the whole heart contracts in a coordinated wave. It's myogenic — the heartbeat starts in the heart itself, in the sinoatrial (SA) node (the pacemaker) in the right atrium wall, which fires rhythmically on its own.

In a frontal plane view you see the unidirectional plumbing: deoxygenated blood arrives via the vena cava → right atrium → through the right atrioventricular valve → right ventricle → pulmonary artery (to the lungs). Oxygenated blood returns via pulmonary veins → left atrium → left atrioventricular valve → left ventricle → aorta (to the body). The septum separates the two sides, so oxygenated and deoxygenated blood never mix.

Valves guarantee one-way flow: atrioventricular valves (right = tricuspid, left = bicuspid) stop blood squirting back into the atria when ventricles contract; semilunar valves at the artery exits stop it squirting back into the ventricles. Ventricles have thicker muscular walls than atria — and the left ventricle is thickest of all, because it pumps blood around the entire body.

The cardiac cycle: SA node fires → atrial systole (atria contract, pushing blood into relaxed ventricles) → ventricular systole (ventricles contract, AV valves shut, blood forced into arteries) → diastole (whole heart relaxes, semilunar valves shut, blood refills from veins). At ~75 bpm one full cycle ≈ 0.8 s.

FIG 11 — The heart (frontal plane): unidirectional flow, valves (AV + semilunar), the septum, SA node pacemaker, and the thicker left ventricle — plus the three-phase beat sequence (atrial systole → ventricular systole → diastole).

B3.2.14 · Single vs double circulation: fish vs mammals

Fish (single circulation)Mammal (double circulation)
Blood passes through the heartOnce per circuitTwice per circuit
Pathwayheart → gills → body → heartheart → lungs → heart → body → heart
Pressure after gas exchangeFalls passing through gills, so body gets lower pressureRe-pumped after lungs — body gets full pressure
Oxygenated / deoxygenatedMixes in one circuit (heart pumps deoxygenated blood)Separate sides — no mixing
Why it worksWater supports fish; gill capillaries are toughHigh pressure needed for the body; delicate lung capillaries get low pressure

Mammals evolved the double pump: the right heart pumps blood to the lungs at low pressure (delicate alveoli), and the left heart re-pumps it to the body at high pressure (needed for perfusion of organs like the kidneys). Fish make do with one pump and accept lower body pressure.

Cardiac cycle timing: at ~75 bpm the cycle lasts ~0.8 s. Atrial systole ~0.1 s, ventricular systole ~0.3 s, total relaxation (diastole) ~0.4 s. Question trap: the atria contract before the ventricles — the delay lets blood drain down into the ventricles before they slam shut.
AHL · B3.2.18

PHLOEM TRANSPORT & LOADING

While xylem moves water up, the phloem transports sucrose and other carbon compounds from their source in the leaf to wherever they are needed (sink). It's the only escalator in biology that runs both ways.

🛗

Sieve tubes + companion cells = the sugar lift

translocation · sieve tubes · sieve plates · companion cells · plasmodesmata · source to sink

Translocation is the transport of sucrose and other organic compounds in phloem from source (where they're made or released — e.g. photosynthesis leaves, or stores being unloaded) to sink (where they're used or stored — e.g. roots, fruits, growing buds). The escalator runs on pressure, not muscle.

The moving walkway is the sieve tube: a column of sieve tube elements joined end to end. Their end walls are perforated into sieve plates, letting sap flow through. Each element keeps a living plasma membrane but has no nucleus and very little cytoplasm — nearly everything is stripped out to leave a clear channel.

Because sieve tubes can barely run their own metabolism, each is paired with a companion cell packed with mitochondria, connected to the sieve tube by plasmodesmata (cytoplasmic channels). The companion cells do the heavy lifting: they load sucrose into the sieve tube by active transport. Water follows by osmosis, building up pressure at the source; at the sink, unloading lowers pressure — and the pressure difference drives the flow of sap from source to sink. That's the pressure-flow model.

FIG 12 — The phloem: sieve tubes (no nucleus, perforated sieve plates) carry sap; companion cells (many mitochondria) load sucrose actively via plasmodesmata; water follows by osmosis creating high pressure at the source and low pressure at the sink — pressure flow drives translocation.
Mnemonic — "Sieve strips, Companion carries": sieve tubes are stripped-back channels (no nucleus, little cytoplasm); companion cells carry the metabolism (mitochondria → ATP → active loading). Water follows solute, pressure does the rest.
Nature of Science: the pressure-flow model is a hypothesis tested with evidence — aphid stylets (insects that tap phloem) let researchers collect actual phloem sap and measure its sucrose content and pressure; radioactive tracers showed sugars moving from source to sink. Models in biology are only as good as the measurements behind them.

Test yourself

✍ SELF-TEST

10 questions. Pick an answer and you'll instantly see why.

1. Capillaries maximise exchange with tissues because…

✅ Narrow + branched = enormous surface area, and walls one endothelium cell thick let exchange happen fast. Wide or thick-walled vessels would be useless for exchange.
❌ Capillaries are the NARROWEST vessels. Their superpower is branching into dense networks with thin one-cell walls — that's what delivers surface area and speed.

2. In a micrograph, the quickest way to tell an artery from a vein is…

✅ Wall thickness vs lumen width is THE distinguishing feature. Corrugated inner surface and visible fibres back it up. Blood content tells you nothing from a section.
❌ Blood content and overall size vary with location and preparation. The reliable feature is the wall-to-lumen ratio — thick + corrugated = artery.

3. Elastic fibres in the tunica media help the circulation because…

✅ Elastic recoil = the artery acts as a secondary pump between heartbeats: smoother flow, steady pressure, less work for the heart. Collagen (externa) prevents bursting.
❌ Rigid walls would spike pressure and destroy the smoothing effect. Elastic fibres are passive — they store energy when stretched and release it on recoil.

4. When taking a pulse you should NOT use your thumb because…

✅ Your thumb has its own pulse — two fingers on the wrist (radial, thumb side) or neck (carotid) is the correct technique, with light pressure.
❌ The thumb is perfectly strong — that's the problem. It carries its own pulse, so using it risks counting your own heartbeat.

5. Blood in veins flows one-way toward the heart mainly because of…

✅ Pocket valves + the skeletal muscle pump (squeezing thin flexible walls) push blood back against gravity. The heart pulls nothing — it only pushes.
❌ Gravity is the enemy in the legs, not the helper. The heart pushes, it doesn't suck — venous return depends on valves and muscle pumps.

6. A strong positive correlation between saturated fat intake and coronary heart disease shows…

✅ Correlation quantifies association, not cause. Claiming causation needs a mechanism and controlled studies. This is the classic NOS trap.
❌ Strong correlation means the variables move together — nothing more. Confounding variables (smoking, exercise, income) could be behind it.

7. Water can reach the top of a 100 m tree because…

✅ Transpiration pull + cohesion: the whole column behaves like one rope. Atmospheric pressure alone can only lift water ~10.4 m — cohesion beats the limit.
❌ Root pressure is weak and only matters when transpiration stalls (spring, night). No vacuum exists — tension + cohesion is the mechanism.

8. Xylem vessels are adapted to transport because they are…

✅ Dead + hollow + lignified + pitted: continuous passive pipes that withstand tension without collapsing. Lignin is the tension-proofing.
❌ Xylem transport is passive — the cells are dead. Lignified walls (not thin ones) stop the tubes collapsing under the pull of transpiration.

9. Tissue fluid forms because…

✅ Pressure filtration: high pressure at the arterial end pushes fluid (plasma minus large proteins) out; low pressure at the venous end recovers most of it. Lymph drains the excess.
❌ Cells don't secrete tissue fluid, and lymph drains tissue fluid OUT (back to the blood) — it doesn't pump it in.

10. Mammals have double circulation, unlike fish, because…

✅ Double circulation = two pumps: right side sends blood to lungs at low pressure (delicate alveoli), left side re-pumps it to the body at high pressure. No mixing, thanks to the septum.
❌ Mammals pump LESS to the lungs (they're delicate) and MORE to the body — that's exactly why the second pump exists. Fish manage with one circuit.

  • ✓B3.2.1Capillaries exchange materials with tissues: ~10 µm narrow, branching networks → huge surface area, walls one endothelium cell thick on a basement membrane (protein gel filter), fenestrations where exchange is rapid (glomerulus, gut). Lens + cornea have none.
  • ✓B3.2.2-3Arteries vs veins: thicker wall, narrower lumen, circular, corrugated inner surface, visible fibres vs thin wall, wide lumen, may flatten. Artery adaptations to high pressure: tunica externa (collagen — no bursting), tunica media (elastic + smooth muscle — recoil smooths flow), tunica intima (endothelium); narrow lumen keeps pressure high.
  • ✓B3.2.4-5Pulse: one wave per heartbeat at radial (wrist, thumb side) and carotid (neck) pulses; 2–3 fingertips, light pressure, never the thumb, 30 s × 2; pulse oximeters (red + IR light). Veins: pocket valves (one-way cups), skeletal muscle pump squeezes thin flexible walls, ~80% of blood at rest in veins, valve failure → varicose veins.
  • ✓B3.2.6Coronary occlusion: coronary arteries are first branches of the aorta; atheroma (lipids incl. cholesterol + calcium salts) narrows lumen → angina + shortness of breath; thrombus → myocardial infarction. Risk factors: hypertension, smoking, saturated fat, obesity, salt, alcohol, sedentary, genetics, age. NOS: correlation (−1 to +1) ≠ causation.
  • ✓B3.2.7Transpiration + cohesion-tension: evaporation from spongy mesophyll cell walls → capillary action through cellulose → tension (negative pressure potential) in xylem → cohesion pulls the continuous column up from the roots; adhesion to walls; passive (sun-driven); cavitation breaks columns; beats the 10.4 m suction limit.
  • ✓B3.2.8Xylem structure: columns of dead cells, contents + end walls removed → continuous tubes; lignified ring/spiral thickenings (young, stretchable) or extensive walls with pits (mature); withstand tension without collapse; pits for water entry/exit; passive flow.
  • ✓B3.2.9-10Plan diagrams: stem TS — epidermis, cortex, ring of vascular bundles (xylem inner, phloem outer, cambium between), pith centre. Root TS — central star-shaped xylem, phloem between star points, endodermis, cortex, epidermis with root hairs. Single lines for tissue boundaries, no cell detail.
  • ✓B3.2.11-13Tissue fluid formation by pressure filtration (high pressure at arteriole end pushes plasma out; low pressure at venule end recovers it; ~20 dm³/day, ~17 return). Plasma vs tissue fluid: tissue fluid lacks large plasma proteins + blood cells. Lymph: blind-ended vessels, thin walls with gaps, valves, lymph ducts → subclavian veins; prevents oedema.
  • ✓B3.2.14-16Single (fish: heart → gills → body) vs double (mammal: lungs + body circuits, re-pumped) circulation. Heart frontal plane: vena cava → RA → RV → pulmonary artery; pulmonary veins → LA → LV → aorta; AV + semilunar valves = unidirectional flow; septum prevents mixing; left ventricle thickest; SA node pacemaker. Cardiac cycle: atrial systole → ventricular systole → diastole.
  • ✓B3.2.17-18Root pressure: root cells actively pump mineral ions into xylem → hypertonic sap → osmosis → positive pressure pushes sap up (spring, night, high humidity, when transpiration is insufficient). Phloem translocation: sieve tubes (no nucleus, perforated sieve plates) + companion cells (many mitochondria, plasmodesmata); active loading at source → pressure flow to sink.
