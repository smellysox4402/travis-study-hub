C4.2 TRANSFER OF ENERGY & MATTER

How energy flows through ecosystems and how matter (especially carbon) is recycled — food chains, energy pyramids, primary/secondary production, the carbon cycle and the Keeling Curve.

C4.2.1

ECOSYSTEMS AS OPEN SYSTEMS

Ecosystems are open systems — both energy and matter can enter and exit.

An open system allows both energy and matter to pass in and out. Ecosystems are open. (In closed systems, only energy can pass in and out — not matter.)

C4.2.2

SUNLIGHT — THE PRINCIPAL ENERGY SOURCE

Sunlight is the principal energy source sustaining most ecosystems, with exceptions (caves, deep ocean).

Sunlight is the main energy source for most ecosystems (via photosynthesis). Exceptions: ecosystems in caves and below light-penetration depth in oceans, where chemoautotrophs use chemical energy instead.

C4.2.3

FLOW OF CHEMICAL ENERGY

Chemical energy passes to a consumer as it feeds on the previous stage in a food chain.

Chemical energy in food is passed along a food chain: an organism at one trophic level is eaten by the next, transferring chemical energy to the consumer.

C4.2.4

FOOD CHAINS & FOOD WEBS

Food chains and food webs represent feeding relationships; arrows indicate the direction of energy flow.

  • A food chain is a single feeding sequence; a food web is all the interlinked chains in a community.
  • Arrows show the direction of energy flow (from the organism eaten to the one that eats it).
C4.2.5

ENERGY TO DECOMPOSERS

Decomposers get energy from carbon compounds in dead organic matter.

Decomposers obtain energy from carbon compounds in organic matter from dead organisms, dead parts and faeces.

C4.2.6

AUTOTROPHS

Autotrophs use external energy to synthesise carbon compounds from simple inorganic substances.

Autotrophs are self-feeders: they use an external energy source to build carbon compounds from simple inorganic substances (CO₂, water).

C4.2.7

PHOTOAUTOTROPHS vs CHEMOAUTOTROPHS

Photoautotrophs use light; chemoautotrophs use oxidation reactions as their energy source.

  • Photoautotrophs — use light (plants, algae, cyanobacteria).
  • Chemoautotrophs — use oxidation reactions (e.g. in deep-sea vents: oxidising inorganic compounds to get energy).
C4.2.8

HETEROTROPHS

Heterotrophs use carbon compounds obtained from other organisms.

Heterotrophs (animals, fungi, most bacteria) get their carbon compounds from other organisms, breaking down complex compounds into simpler ones they can use.

C4.2.9

RELEASING ENERGY — CELL RESPIRATION

Both autotrophs and heterotrophs release energy by oxidising carbon compounds in cell respiration.

Both autotrophs and heterotrophs release the energy stored in carbon compounds by oxidation during cell respiration (converting chemical energy into ATP + heat).

C4.2.10

TROPHIC LEVELS

Organisms are classified into trophic levels: producer, primary, secondary and tertiary consumer.

  • Producer — autotroph (first trophic level).
  • Primary consumer — eats producers.
  • Secondary consumer — eats primary consumers.
  • Tertiary consumer — eats secondary consumers.
C4.2.11

ENERGY PYRAMIDS

Construct energy pyramids showing energy transfer and losses between trophic levels.

An energy pyramid shows the energy available at each trophic level, with the producers at the base. Each level is smaller than the one below because energy is lost between levels.

FIG 1 — An energy pyramid: the producer level holds the most energy, with less energy at each higher trophic level.
C4.2.12

ENERGY LOSS BETWEEN TROPHIC LEVELS

Energy availability decreases at each successive stage due to large losses between trophic levels.

Much energy is lost between trophic levels (as heat, in undigested material, etc.), so less energy is available at each successive stage. Decomposers and detritus feeders are not usually counted in energy pyramids.

C4.2.13

HEAT LOSS TO THE ENVIRONMENT

Energy transfers are not 100% efficient; chemical energy is converted to heat in respiration.

In both autotrophs and heterotrophs, much of the chemical energy is converted to heat during cell respiration, and lost to the environment. Energy transfers are not 100% efficient.

C4.2.14

RESTRICTIONS ON TROPHIC LEVELS

Energy losses restrict the number of trophic levels.

Because so much energy is lost at each level, there is not enough energy to support many trophic levels. At each successive stage there are fewer organisms or smaller organisms. Typically only about 4–5 trophic levels are supported.

C4.2.15

PRIMARY PRODUCTION

Primary production is the accumulation of carbon compounds in biomass by autotrophs (e.g. g C m⁻² yr⁻¹).

Primary production is the rate at which autotrophs accumulate carbon compounds in biomass. It is measured in mass of carbon per area per time (e.g. g C m⁻² yr⁻¹).

C4.2.16

SECONDARY PRODUCTION

Secondary production is the accumulation of carbon compounds in biomass by heterotrophs.

Secondary production is the accumulation of carbon compounds in biomass by heterotrophs. Because some biomass is lost when carbon compounds are converted (respiration, waste), secondary production is much lower than primary production.

C4.2.17

THE CARBON CYCLE

Illustrate how carbon is recycled by photosynthesis, feeding and respiration.

Carbon is cycled between the atmosphere, organisms and the environment: photosynthesis takes CO₂ out and builds carbon compounds; feeding passes carbon along food chains; respiration (and decomposition, combustion) returns CO₂ to the atmosphere.

FIG 2 — The carbon cycle: photosynthesis removes CO₂, feeding moves carbon along, and respiration/decomposition/combustion return it.
C4.2.18

CARBON SINKS & SOURCES

An ecosystem is a carbon sink if photosynthesis exceeds respiration, and a source if respiration exceeds photosynthesis.

If photosynthesis > respiration, there is a net uptake of CO₂ → a carbon sink. If respiration > photosynthesis, there is net release of CO₂ → a carbon source.

C4.2.19

CO₂ FROM COMBUSTION

Burning biomass, peat, coal, oil and natural gas releases CO₂ into the atmosphere.

Combustion of biomass, peat, coal, oil and natural gas releases carbon dioxide. These carbon stores vary in how long they've been locked away (fossil fuels = ancient carbon).

C4.2.20

THE KEELING CURVE

Analyse the Keeling Curve for both annual fluctuations and the long-term rising trend.

  • Annual fluctuations — CO₂ falls in summer (more photosynthesis in the Northern Hemisphere) and rises in winter (more respiration), giving a sawtooth pattern.
  • Long-term trend — a steady rise in atmospheric CO₂ over decades, due largely to combustion of fossil fuels.
FIG 3 — The Keeling Curve: a sawtooth (seasonal photosynthesis/respiration) superimposed on a long-term rise driven largely by fossil-fuel combustion.
C4.2.21

O₂ & CO₂ INTERDEPENDENCE

Aerobic respiration depends on O₂ from photosynthesis; photosynthesis depends on CO₂ from respiration.

Aerobic respiration uses the O₂ produced by photosynthesis; photosynthesis uses the CO₂ produced by respiration. These two fluxes keep the atmosphere balanced.

C4.2.22

RECYCLING OF ALL CHEMICAL ELEMENTS

All chemical elements used by living organisms are recycled in ecosystems (not just carbon).

Not just carbon — all elements used by organisms (e.g. nitrogen, phosphorus, water) are recycled in ecosystems through nutrient cycles.
