A3.2.1

THE NEED FOR CLASSIFICATION

We classify because of the immense diversity of species, and doing so makes further study easier.

There are millions of species, so classification groups them logically. Once classified, a wide range of further study — naming, cataloguing, comparing, predicting properties — becomes possible.

A3.2.2

DIFFICULTIES WITH THE TRADITIONAL HIERARCHY

The traditional hierarchy (kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species) doesn't always match patterns of divergence.

The old system groups organisms by similar appearance, but that doesn't always reflect evolutionary relationships — convergent evolution can make unrelated organisms look alike, and some groups turn out not to be monophyletic. So the traditional hierarchy sometimes misrepresents how life actually diverged.

A3.2.3

CLASSIFICATION SHOULD FOLLOW EVOLUTIONARY RELATIONSHIPS

The ideal classification reflects evolutionary relationships — every member of a group has evolved from a common ancestor.

Ideally, a taxonomic group is monophyletic: all its members share a common ancestor and include all the descendants of that ancestor. This makes the classification meaningful and consistent with evolution.

A3.2.4

CLADES — DETERMINED BY SEQUENCES

A clade is a group with common ancestry and shared characteristics. The most objective evidence for placing organisms in a clade is their base sequences or amino acid sequences.

A clade = a common ancestor and all its descendants. The most objective way to place organisms together is not appearance but their base sequences of genes or amino acid sequences of proteins.

A3.2.5

THE MOLECULAR CLOCK

Sequence differences accumulate gradually and can be used to estimate when clades diverged — the "molecular clock".

Mutations accumulate at a roughly steady rate, so the number of sequence differences between two groups is proportional to the time since they shared a common ancestor. This lets scientists estimate divergence times — the molecular clock.

A3.2.6

CONSTRUCTING CLADOGRAMS FROM SEQUENCES

Base or amino acid sequence data is used to build cladograms. Simple sample data illustrates the method.

Organisms are grouped according to how many sequence differences they share. The more similar their sequences, the more recent their common ancestor. A cladogram is the branching diagram that results.

Nature of science: different sequence data (e.g. different genes) can give different results, so conclusions are drawn from as much data as possible — and scientists must recognise and handle this uncertainty.

A3.2.7

ANALYSING CLADOGRAMS

You should be able to deduce evolutionary relationships, common ancestors and clades from a cladogram, using the terms root, node and terminal branch.

  • Root — the most ancient ancestor at the base.
  • Node — a branching point representing a common ancestor (a speciation event).
  • Terminal branch — a tip representing a present-day species/taxon.
  • Clade — a node and all its descendants.
  • The more recent a node, the more recently the groups diverged.
A3.2.8

USING CLADISTICS TO CHECK CLASSIFICATION

Case study: transferring plant species between families when cladistics shows they don't fit the old classification.

Cladistics can reveal groups that the traditional hierarchy got wrong. For example, plant species have been reassigned between families once sequence data showed they were more closely related to a different family than their morphology suggested — demonstrating that an evidence-based, evolutionary classification can override the traditional one.

A3.2.9

THE THREE DOMAINS

Using evidence from rRNA base sequences, all organisms are classified into three domains — a level above kingdoms, proposed in 1977.

Analysis of rRNA base sequences led to the now-standard reclassification into three domains, a higher level than kingdoms: Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya. This grouping was proposed by Carl Woese in 1977 and is based on molecular evidence, not appearance.
