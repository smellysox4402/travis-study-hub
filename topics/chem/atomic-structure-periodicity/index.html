Atomic Structure & Periodicity

The atom, the electron and the periodic table — what is in the nucleus, how electrons are arranged, and how position predicts behaviour.
STRUCTURE 1.2 · 1.3 · 3.1 — ATOMS & THE TABLE S3.1.5–S3.1.10 ARE AHL (S1.2 + S1.3 + S3.1.1–3.1.4 IS SL) 20 SYLLABUS POINTS
S1.2.1

The nuclear atom

The atom is mostly empty: a dense positive nucleus with electrons around it. Rutherford found it with gold foil.

🔐

The atom: nucleus and electrons (S1.2.1)

nucleons = protons + neutrons · electrons live outside the vault

  • Nucleus = protons (+1) + neutrons (0) — together called nucleons. Electrons (−1) occupy the vast region outside.
  • Vault rules: (1) tiny compared to the atom (atom diameter ≈ 100 000 × nucleus diameter), (2) contains virtually ALL the mass, (3) positively charged.
  • Subatomic particles: proton (relative mass 1, charge +1, nucleus) · neutron (mass 1, charge 0, nucleus) · electron (mass negligible, charge −1, outside nucleus).
  • Atom diameter: 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ to 5 × 10⁻¹⁰ m. Units: 1 pm = 10⁻¹² m · 1 Å (angstrom) = 10⁻¹⁰ m. Fluorine atomic radius = 60 pm = 0.60 Å.
  • Elementary charge e ≈ 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C — protons +1e, electrons −1e.
Figure 1 — Rutherford's gold foil experiment. The alpha particles are ⁴₂He nuclei fired at the foil. Nearly all pass through; ~1 in 8000 bounces back. That falsified the plum-pudding model (amorphous positive blob) and proved the nucleus exists.
EXAM TRAP: "Most alpha particles bounced back" is WRONG — most passed straight through. Only a FEW bounced back, and THAT is the evidence for the small dense positive nucleus. Match each observation to the property it proves (nucleus small / dense / positive).

Nuclear symbols

ᴬ_Z X — A = mass number (protons + neutrons) · Z = atomic number (protons) · X = element symbol

A = Z + N (N = neutrons). Neutral atom: electrons = protons. Z is also the position on the periodic table — gold, atomic number 79, has 79 protons; ¹⁹⁷₇₉Au has 197 − 79 = 118 neutrons.

Ions change the electron count, not the vault: magnesium loses 2 electrons → ²⁴₁₂Mg²⁺ (12 p⁺, 12 n, 10 e⁻). Oxygen gains 2 → ¹⁶₈O²⁻ (8 p⁺, 8 n, 10 e⁻). Charge = protons − electrons.

Activity: an ion with 24 protons, 21 electrons, 28 neutrons → Z = 24 (Cr), A = 24 + 28 = 52, charge = 24 − 21 = +3 → ⁵²₂₄Cr³⁺.

S1.2.2 · S1.2.3

Isotopes & mass spectrometry

Isotopes differ only in neutrons; the periodic table mass is a weighted average.

⚖️

Isotopes (S1.2.2)

same Z, different N → different mass number A

  • Isotopes = atoms of the same element with different numbers of neutrons. Same protons, same electrons → same chemical properties. Different mass → different physical properties (density, mp, bp).
  • Chlorine: ³⁵₁₇Cl and ³⁷₁₇Cl. Shorthand: chlorine-37 or Cl-37 (Z is obvious from the symbol).
  • Hydrogen's trio: protium ¹H, deuterium ²H (stable), tritium ³H (radioactive — used in glowing exit signs!).
  • Heavy water: ²H₂O density 1.106 vs ¹H₂O 1.000 g cm⁻³, bp 101.4 vs 100.0 °C — physical differences you can measure.
  • Real uses: uranium enrichment (raise ²³⁵U from 0.72% to ≥3% for reactors), isotope labelling to track reaction mechanisms.
🔬

RELATIVE ATOMIC MASS — THE WEIGHTED AVERAGE

A_r = Σ (isotope mass × natural abundance) ÷ 100

  • A_r = weighted average of all isotopes' masses using their natural abundance (NA, the % of that isotope on Earth).
  • Not a whole number precisely because it's an average over the isotope mix.
  • Reverse problem (find the abundance split) works ONLY for two-isotope elements: let x = NA of isotope 1, then NA of isotope 2 = 100 − x.
WORKED EXAMPLE 1 (book p.27): A_r for iron from the table — ⁵⁴Fe 5.845%, ⁵⁶Fe 91.754%, ⁵⁷Fe 2.119%, ⁵⁸Fe 0.282%
A_r = (54 × 5.845 + 56 × 91.754 + 57 × 2.119 + 58 × 0.282) ÷ 100 = 55.91.
WORKED EXAMPLE 2: Chlorine (A_r = 35.45) from ³⁵Cl and ³⁷Cl. Let x = NA(³⁵Cl): (35x + 37(100 − x))/100 = 35.45 → 3700 − 2x = 3545 → x = 77.5%, so ³⁷Cl = 22.5%. (Actual: 75.8/24.2 — the small difference is because mass numbers are rounded versions of true isotopic masses.)
Figure 2 — A mass spectrum plots relative abundance against m/z. The tallest peak = most abundant isotope. A_r is the weighted average you compute from the peaks — here it comes out 10.8.
⚙️

The mass spectrometer (S1.2.3)

operational details are NOT assessed — but the story is

  1. Vaporize — heating filament turns the sample into gas.
  2. Ionize — high-energy electron beam knocks electrons off atoms → cations (M → M⁺ + 2e⁻).
  3. Accelerate — electric field speeds the ions up.
  4. Deflect — magnetic field bends the path. Deflection depends on m/z: lowest mass, highest charge = deflected MOST. Neutral particles aren't affected at all → never reach the detector.
  5. Detect — ions hit the detector; a computer plots the mass spectrum.

Real application: cobalt vs nickel in iron meteorites look chemically identical, but their isotope fingerprints are totally different — mass spec tells them apart instantly.

S1.3.1 · S1.3.2 · S1.3.6

Atomic spectra

Line spectra are the evidence that electron energies are quantised.

S1.3.1 · SL

THREE SPECTRA, ONE STORY

Continuous: all wavelengths, colours merge — a rainbow, 400–700 nm.

Emission: hot gas glows → bright lines on dark. Each element has its own line "barcode".

Absorption: cold gas in front of a white source → dark lines on the rainbow.

Sodium streetlight: 589.0 + 589.6 nm yellow-orange — same glow as the flame test.

S1.3.2 · SL

THE HYDROGEN RAINBOW

Four visible lines: 656 nm red (3→2) · 486 nm cyan (4→2) · 434 nm blue (5→2) · 410 nm violet (6→2).

Lines converge at higher energy — they bunch up like stairs to a ceiling.

Only discrete colours → electrons exist in discrete energy levels, not a continuum.

Transitions to n = 1 → UV · to n = 2 → visible · to n = 3 → IR.

S1.3.6 · SL

THE CEILING = IONIZATION

The limit of convergence = the energy to fully remove the electron.

Hydrogen: convergence at 9.12 × 10⁻⁸ m → E = hc/λ → 2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J per atom = 1.31 × 10³ kJ mol⁻¹ — matches the data booklet.

Same ceiling the Bohr model predicts: E₁ = −2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J.

🎚

Photon absorption and emission

c = f × λ · E = h × f · h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s · c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹

  • Light is electromagnetic radiation: gamma → X-ray → UV → visible → IR → microwave → radio.
  • Energy ∝ 1/λ: short wavelength = high frequency = high energy. Gamma has the highest energy; radio the lowest.
  • Bohr 1913: electrons orbit in fixed allowed orbits. Absorb a photon with exactly ΔE → jump to an excited state. Fall back → emit a photon of exactly that ΔE. No in-between: quantized.
  • The Bohr model only works cleanly for hydrogen — one electron. The quantum mechanical model (Schrödinger, 1926) replaces orbits with probability clouds.
  • Flame tests are the same physics: heat bumps electrons up, they fall back and emit visible photons. Li = red, Na = yellow-orange, K = lilac, Ca = brick-red, Cu = blue-green, Sr = crimson.
Figure 3 — Hydrogen's energy levels. Falling to n = 2 gives the four visible Balmer lines; the convergence limit at the top is the ionization energy (S1.3.6).
MEMORY HOOK: "656 red, 486 cyan, 434 blue, 410 violet" — the gaps shrink as n grows, so the lines bunch together. Their limit is the ionization energy. Also: 1/n² spacing — each floor sits closer to the ceiling than the last.
S1.3.3 · S1.3.4 · S1.3.5

Electron arrangement

Shells, sublevels and orbitals — the arrangement of electrons.

S1.3.3 · SL

THE 2n² RULE

Floor n holds at most 2n² electrons.

n = 1 → 2 · n = 2 → 8 · n = 3 → 18 · n = 4 → 32

That's the fire-code capacity of each floor.

S1.3.4 · SL

ROOMS: s, p, d, f

Each floor splits into sublevels of rising energy: s < p < d < f.

Orbitals per sublevel: s = 1, p = 3, d = 5, f = 7.

Shapes: s = sphere · p = dumbbell (px, py, pz at right angles) · d = cloverleaf (5) · f = complex (7).

Floor 3: 3s (2 e⁻) + 3p (6 e⁻) + 3d (10 e⁻) = 18 ✓.

S1.3.5 · SL

BEDS: ORBITALS

Orbital = region of space with a high probability (~99%) of finding the electron.

Pauli: max 2 electrons per orbital, opposite spins.

Degenerate: same-energy orbitals — the three 2p orbitals are equal.

Hund: fill degenerate orbitals singly first, same spin, before pairing.

Degenerate = "equal energy". Spreading out first minimises repulsion.

Figure 4a — Aufbau filling order. Read the ladder diagonally: 4s is filled before 3d, 6s before 4f.
Figure 4b — Pauli: two electrons per orbital, opposite spins. Hund: fill before pairing. Half-filled sublevels = stability bonus (that's Cr and Cu's excuse).
📋

WRITING CONFIGURATIONS — THE SHORTCUTS (S1.3.5)

full vs condensed · ions · the two exceptions you must know

  • Full: Ca = 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s². Condensed: [Ar] 4s². Noble-gas core + valence.
  • Ions: electrons come OUT of the highest n first (not the last-filled!): Mn²⁺ = [Ar] 3d⁵ (4s emptied), Fe²⁺ = [Ar]3d⁶, Fe³⁺ = [Ar]3d⁵.
  • The two exceptions (memorize): Cr = [Ar]4s¹3d⁵ (not 4s²3d⁴) and Cu = [Ar]4s¹3d¹⁰ (not 4s²3d⁹) — half-filled/full d wins.
  • IB only tests writing configurations up to Z = 36 (krypton).
EXAM TRAP: "4s fills first" AND "4s empties first" are BOTH true — but for different reasons. Filling: 4s is lower energy when empty. Removing: the ion's energy is minimised by stripping the outermost shell (highest n) first. So Fe → Fe²⁺ loses 4s², giving [Ar]3d⁶.
S1.3.3–S1.3.4 · SL

Quantum numbers

The four quantum numbers give every electron a unique address.

📇

FOUR NUMBERS, ONE ELECTRON (S1.3.3)

like a street address: city → street → building → bed

  • n — principal. The main energy level (shell) and the electron's average distance from the nucleus. Allowed: positive integers 1, 2, 3, 4… Higher n = higher energy, further out.
  • l — angular momentum (azimuthal). The subshell, i.e. the SHAPE of the orbital. Allowed: integers 0 up to n − 1. l = 0 → s (spherical), l = 1 → p (dumbbell), l = 2 → d, l = 3 → f.
  • mₗ — magnetic. The specific orientation of the orbital in 3D space. Allowed: integers from −l to +l (including 0). Tells you the exact number of orbitals in a subshell: s = 1, p = 3, d = 5, f = 7.
  • mₛ — spin. The direction of the electron's intrinsic spin. Allowed: +½ (spin-up) or −½ (spin-down). Pauli: no two electrons in the same orbital can share the same spin — that's the cap of 2 per orbital.
Figure 4b — The four quantum numbers as an electron's postcode. n = shell, l = shape, mₗ = orientation, mₛ = spin; the worked example is nitrogen's last electron in 2p³ (n = 2, l = 1, mₗ = −1, mₛ = +½). Deeper tutorial: Chemistry LibreTexts — Quantum Numbers.
S1.3.7

Ionization energy

Ionization energy, and the big jump that reveals the shell structure.

🔨

IE DEFINITIONS & THE BIG-JUMP TRICK (S1.3.7)

X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻ · always gaseous, always minimum energy

  • First ionization energy = minimum energy to remove one electron from one mole of gaseous atoms: X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻.
  • Successive IEs always increase — the ion gets more positively charged, so each remaining electron feels more pull.
  • The big jump happens when you break through to an inner (core) shell: after the valence electrons are gone, the next electron comes from a much lower, much more tightly held level.
  • Group = number of electrons before the big jump. Na: jump after IE₁ → group 1. Mg: jump after IE₂ → group 2. Al: jump after IE₃ → group 13.
  • IE data also confirms shell structure (S1.3.3): the jump size tells you how many electrons were in the outer level — evidence for 2n² and the whole tower model.
Figure 5 — Successive ionization energies. The wall after IE₁ (Na), IE₂ (Mg) or IE₃ (Al) marks the edge of the core shell — that count identifies the group.
WORKED EXAMPLE (book p.53–54): An element's successive IE values (kJ mol⁻¹) are 578, 1817, 2745, 11577... → IE₁–IE₃ are low and similar; IE₄ is ~4× bigger → 3 valence electrons → group 13 (aluminium-like). If asked to sketch from data: the big jump's position = group number, always.
MEMORY HOOK: IE is like pulling out nails from a board. The first nail comes out easy; each next one is welded tighter; and the moment you hit the board's core (core electrons), you need a completely different tool — the sledgehammer of IE.
S3.1.1 · S3.1.2 · S3.1.3 · S3.1.4

The periodic table

The periodic table: position predicts radius, ionization energy, electronegativity and reactivity.

S3.1.1 · SL

THE MAP'S COORDINATES

Period = row (1–7) · Group = column (1–18) · Block = which sublevel the outer electrons live in (s, p, d, f).

Mendeleev: ordered by atomic mass, left gaps, predicted gallium (eka-aluminium) before it was found. Modern table: ordered by atomic number (Moseley).

The gaps were the point — the map predicted undiscovered elements. That's a map worth framing.

S3.1.2 · SL

READING POSITION = READING CONFIG

Group number = valence electrons: groups 1–2 → the group number itself (group 2 = 2 valence e⁻); groups 13–18 → the last digit (group 17 = 7 valence e⁻).

Period number = principal quantum number n of the outer shell. Sodium is in period 3 → outer electrons in n = 3.

Position IS the electron configuration, in map form.

🧭

THE NEIGHBOURHOODS (S3.1.3)

group 1 alkali metals · group 17 halogens · group 18 noble gases · groups 3–11 transition elements

  • Metals (left + middle): lose electrons, form cations, conduct — about 80% of all elements.
  • Non-metals (top right): gain/share electrons, form anions and covalent compounds.
  • Metalloids straddle the zig-zag line (B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te): semiconductor behaviour — the "in-betweeners".
  • Group names to drop in essays: alkali metals (1), alkaline earth metals (2), halogens (17), noble gases (18), transition elements (3–11).
Figure 6 — Schematic map (not to scale): period = row (n), group = column (valence e⁻), block = outer sublevel. Trend arrows: radius shrinks across, grows down; IE/EN/EA grow across, fall down.
S3.1.4 · SL

ATOMIC RADIUS

Across a period → decreases. More protons pull the same shell tighter (nuclear charge ↑, no new shielding).

Down a group → increases. New shells = bigger tower; more shielding from inner electrons.

Effective nuclear charge Z_eff is the real driver: what the outer electrons actually feel.

S3.1.4 · SL

IONIC RADIUS

Cations < parent atom (fewer electrons, same protons → squeeze). Anions > parent atom (extra electrons → balloon).

Isoelectronic series (same configuration): size set by proton count — O²⁻ > F⁻ > Ne > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺.

Same number of electrons, more protons = smaller. That's the whole series.

S3.1.4 · SL

IE · EA · ELECTRONEGATIVITY

IE: increases across (tighter grip), decreases down (more shielding).

Electron affinity: energy released on X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g). More negative across, less negative down.

Electronegativity: power to attract bonding electrons. Across ↑, down ↓. F = 4.0, the maximum.

Metallic character follows IE: alkali metals get MORE reactive down the group (Cs explodes), halogens LESS reactive down the group (F₂ is the beast).

EXAM TRAP: Two different reasons, two different directions. "Across = higher Z, same shell, no new shielding → smaller radius, higher IE/EN." "Down = new shell + more shielding → bigger radius, lower IE/EN." Never mix the justifications.
S3.1.5 · S3.1.6 · S3.1.7 · S3.1.8 · AHL

Periodic trends (AHL)

AHL: the IE wobbles, oxidation states, and the basic-to-acidic oxide arc.

📉

THE TWO WOBBLES IN THE IE GRAPH (S3.1.5)

Be → B and N → O: the exceptions that PROVE the sublevel model

  • Be → B: IE₁(B) < IE₁(Be). The new 2p electron sits in a higher-energy, shielded sublevel — the 2s² pair blocks some nuclear charge. s-sublevel electrons penetrate more than p.
  • N → O: IE₁(O) < IE₁(N). Nitrogen's 2p³ is half-filled and stable; oxygen's extra electron is forced to pair up in an already-occupied orbital → pairing repulsion makes it easier to remove.
  • Same pattern later: Mg → Al (3s² vs 3p¹) and P → S (3p³ vs 3p⁴).
  • The takeaway: half-filled (p³, d⁵) and filled (p⁶, d¹⁰) sublevels are extra stable — this explains Cr, Cu, and the common ions of transition metals.
🧮

OXIDATION STATES — THE BOOKKEEPING (S3.1.6)

oxidation state = the charge an atom WOULD have if all bonds were ionic

  • Rules: element alone = 0 · monatomic ion = its charge · group 1 = +1, group 2 = +2 · F always −1 · H = +1 (except metal hydrides −1) · O = −2 (except peroxides −1, OF₂ +2) · sum = 0 in a neutral compound, = ion charge in a polyatomic ion.
  • Naming oxyanions with the state: manganate(VII) = MnO₄⁻ (Mn +7), sulfate(VI) = SO₄²⁻, nitrate(V) = NO₃⁻, dichromate(VI) = Cr₂O₇²⁻.
  • Transition metals rock variable states because their successive IEs are close: Fe +2/+3, Cu +1/+2, Mn +2/+4/+7 (manganese(II) oxide, manganese(IV) oxide, manganese(VII) oxide).
  • Why so many states? The 4s and 3d electrons sit at similar energies — the tower's top floors are all the same height (Act 8 digs in).
🌋

THE OXIDE ARC — BASIC → AMPHOTERIC → ACIDIC (S3.1.7)

metal oxides = Lewis bases · non-metal oxides = Lewis acids

  • Period 3 sweep: Na₂O, MgO basic → Al₂O₃ amphoteric → SiO₂, P₄O₁₀, SO₂, SO₃, Cl₂O₇ acidic. One row, full arc.
  • Basic oxides + water → hydroxides: Na₂O + H₂O → 2NaOH (strong base); MgO + H₂O → Mg(OH)₂ (weak).
  • Acidic oxides + water → acids: SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃; SO₃ + H₂O → H₂SO₄; P₄O₁₀ + 6H₂O → 4H₃PO₄; CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃.
  • Al₂O₃ amphoteric — eats both: with acid Al₂O₃ + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂O; with base Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH + 3H₂O → 2Na[Al(OH)₄].
  • Acid rain: SO₂ + NOₓ from burning fossil fuels → pH below ~5.6 → eats limestone buildings, kills lakes.
💥

ALKALI METALS vs HALOGENS (S3.1.8)

the most reactive metal family vs the most reactive non-metal family

  • Alkali + water: 2M + 2H₂O → 2MOH + H₂. Na fizzes into a darting molten ball; Cs detonates. Reactivity increases down (IE falls). Phenolphthalein → pink (OH⁻ made).
  • Halogens steal electrons: X₂ + 2X'⁻ → 2X⁻ + X'₂. Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂ (orange-brown bromine appears). The stronger (higher) halogen oxidises the weaker one's ions.
  • Halogen reactivity decreases down (electronegativity falls): F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂. F₂ is the neighbourhood bully — it even steals from oxygen in OF₂.
WORKED EXAMPLE (S3.1.6): Oxidation state of N in sodium nitrate NaNO₃ → Na +1, O₃ = 3 × (−2) = −6 → 1 + N + (−6) = 0 → N = +5, so it's sodium nitrate(V). For Co in [CoCl₄]²⁻: 4 × (−1) = −4, overall −2 → Co = +2.
S3.1.9 · S3.1.10 · AHL

Transition elements (AHL)

AHL: transition elements — variable oxidation states and d-orbital colour.

🚪

Transition elements: the definition (S3.1.9)

partially filled d sublevel in the atom OR in any common stable ion

  • Transition element = element with a partially filled d sublevel in its atom or in any of its common stable ions (groups 3–11 of the d-block).
  • Zinc is NOT one: Zn²⁺ = [Ar]3d¹⁰ — full d, and its compounds are colourless. Scandium barely is: Sc³⁺ = [Ar] — empty d. (Book's verdict: Sc not considered a transition element — it had only +3 for decades.)
  • Signature properties: variable oxidation states, coloured complexes, paramagnetism (unpaired d electrons → attracted to a magnetic field), catalytic behaviour, high mp/density.
  • Catalyst spotlight: catalytic converters (Pt/Pd/Rh) — 2CO + O₂ → 2CO₂ and NOₓ → N₂ + O₂ in your exhaust.
  • Variable states explained by successive IEs that stay close: the 4s and 3d electrons all cost similar energy to remove. Compare: Cr shows +1 to +6; copper's bigger gaps mean fewer stable states (+1, +2).
🎨

Why transition metal complexes are coloured (S3.1.10)

ligands split the d orbitals → visible light gets absorbed → you see the complement

  • A complex = central transition-metal cation + ligands (molecules/ions with lone pairs, e.g. H₂O, Cl⁻, NH₃) joined by coordinate bonds. [Cr(H₂O)₆]³⁺ is violet.
  • Ligands split the five d orbitals into two energy sets (they're degenerate in a free ion — all equal). The gap lands in the visible range.
  • Electrons jump the gap by absorbing that exact wavelength. You see the complementary colour — opposites on the colour wheel (in the data booklet).
  • [Cr(H₂O)₆]³⁺ absorbs yellow (~585 nm) → looks violet. [CuCl₄]²⁻ looks orange-red → it absorbed blue-green (~491 nm).
  • Three factors set the gap: ligand strength (stronger ligand → bigger split → higher-energy light absorbed), identity of the central ion, and its oxidation state (Cr³⁺ vs Cr²⁺ complexes differ).
  • Zinc's [Ar]3d¹⁰ has nowhere to jump → colourless. The classic contrast: Cu²⁺ blue vs Zn²⁺ clear.
Figure 7 — Absorbed colour vs observed colour are wheel-opposites. The d-splitting gap ΔE decides which wavelength gets eaten.
WORKED EXAMPLE (book p.252): [CuCl₄]²⁻ appears the colour of 647 nm light. The wavelength ABSORBED is the complement: 491 nm. f = c/λ = 3.00 × 10⁸ / (491 × 10⁻⁹) = 6.11 × 10¹⁴ s⁻¹. E = h × f = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 6.11 × 10¹⁴ = 4.05 × 10⁻¹⁹ J — that's the d-orbital gap.
EXAM-STYLE (book p.255): [Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ pink absorbs ~540 nm; add excess Cl⁻ → [CoCl₄]²⁻ blue. Cl⁻ is a weaker ligand → smaller split → lower energy (longer wavelength) absorbed → different colour. Pink 540 nm absorbed = green eaten → pink seen; blue means yellow-orange eaten.

1. In Rutherford's gold foil experiment, MOST alpha particles...

✅ Most passed straight through — the atom is mostly empty space. The FEW that bounced back proved the tiny dense positive nucleus.
❌ Most went straight through. Only about 1 in 8000 bounced back — that's the nucleus evidence.

2. Which subatomic particle has negligible relative mass?

✅ Electron: relative mass negligible, charge −1, outside the nucleus. Proton and neutron both have relative mass 1.
❌ The electron. Protons (1, +1) and neutrons (1, 0) carry the mass in the nucleus.

3. How many electrons are in ⁶³₂₉Cu²⁺?

✅ Z = 29 protons; neutral Cu has 29 e⁻; the 2+ charge means 2 lost → 29 − 2 = 27.
❌ 29 − 2 = 27. Cations have FEWER electrons than protons.

4. Isotopes of the same element differ in their number of...

✅ Same protons (same element!), different neutrons → different mass number. Same chemical properties, different physical ones.
❌ Neutrons. Same Z, different N — that's the whole definition.

5. Boron: ¹¹B 80.1%, ¹⁰B 19.9%. Its A_r is...

✅ (11 × 80.1 + 10 × 19.9) ÷ 100 = (881.1 + 199) ÷ 100 = 10.8.
❌ (11 × 80.1 + 10 × 19.9) ÷ 100 = 10.8. Weighted average, not a plain average.

6. In a mass spectrometer, the ion deflected MOST by the magnetic field has...

✅ Lowest m/z = lightest (or most charged) = deflected most. Uncharged particles aren't deflected at all.
❌ Lowest m/z. The lightest ions bend the most in the magnetic field.

7. A hot gas viewed through a prism gives a...

✅ Emission spectrum — the element's bright-line barcode. Dark lines = absorption (cold gas in front of a source).
❌ Emission — bright lines on dark. The dark-line version is an absorption spectrum.

8. The maximum number of electrons in the n = 3 energy level is...

✅ 2n² = 2 × 3² = 18 (3s + 3p + 3d).
❌ 2n² = 18. (8 is n = 2; 32 is n = 4.)

9. How many orbitals are in a p sublevel?

✅ Three (px, py, pz) → 6 electrons max. s = 1 orbital, p = 3, d = 5, f = 7.
❌ 3 orbitals (px, py, pz). 1 = s, 5 = d.

10. The electron configuration of chromium is...

✅ [Ar]4s¹3d⁵ — the half-filled d wins. Same logic: Cu = [Ar]4s¹3d¹⁰.
❌ [Ar]4s¹3d⁵. Half-filled d⁵ is extra stable, so one 4s electron drops down.

11. Across a period (left to right), first ionization energy...

✅ Increases: higher nuclear charge, same shell, no new shielding → tighter grip. (Small wobbles at Be→B and N→O, AHL.)
❌ Increases across the period — the atoms shrink and the nucleus pulls harder.

12. An element's successive IEs (kJ mol⁻¹): 738, 1450, 7733, 10540... It belongs to group...

✅ Big jump after IE₂ → 2 valence electrons → group 2 (magnesium-like). Count the low bars before the wall.
❌ Group 2 — the huge jump comes after removing the SECOND electron.

13. Atomic radius across a period...

✅ Decreases — more protons pull the same shell tighter (no extra shielding). Down a group it grows (new shells).
❌ Decreases. Same shell, bigger Z, no new shielding → atoms shrink.

14. (AHL) Which is NOT a transition element?

✅ Zn²⁺ = [Ar]3d¹⁰ — full d sublevel, colourless compounds. Transition elements need a partially filled d in atom or common ion.
❌ Zinc. Fe and Cu both form ions with partially filled d. Zn²⁺ is 3d¹⁰.

15. (AHL) [Cr(H₂O)₆]³⁺ looks violet. What wavelength does it ABSORB?

✅ It absorbs its complement on the colour wheel — yellow — and what's left over looks violet. You never see the absorbed colour.
❌ Yellow. Observed colour = complement of absorbed colour (opposites on the wheel).

16. (AHL) Why is IE₁ of aluminium LOWER than IE₁ of magnesium?

✅ Same story as Be→B: the 3p electron sits above the 3s² pair, which shields it. The s sublevel penetrates more than p.
❌ The 3p electron is shielded by the 3s² pair → easier to remove. (Mg→Al mirrors Be→B; P→S mirrors N→O.)

17. Which quantum number tells you the SHAPE of the orbital (s, p, d, f)?

✅ l, running 0 … n − 1: 0 = s (sphere), 1 = p (dumbbell), 2 = d, 3 = f. n names the shell, mₗ the 3D orientation, mₛ the spin.
❌ l — the angular momentum quantum number. n = shell, mₗ = orientation, mₛ = spin (±½).

🎯 EXAM WALKTHROUGHS

Q1 — DEFINE [2 marks] "Define first ionization energy."

Model answer: the energy required to remove one mole of electrons (1) from one mole of gaseous atoms (1) to form one mole of gaseous 1+ ions.

"Gaseous" is the word students drop — it's worth a mark every time.

Q2 — CALCULATE [2 marks] "Boron: ¹⁰B (19.9%, 10.0) and ¹¹B (80.1%, 11.0). Calculate Ar."

Model answer: Ar = (10.0 × 19.9 + 11.0 × 80.1)/100 = (199 + 881.1)/100 = 10.8 (1 for method, 1 for answer).

Q3 — CALCULATE [2 marks] "Calculate the energy of a photon of wavelength 656 nm (h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴, c = 3.00 × 10⁸)."

Model answer: E = hc/λ = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸)/(656 × 10⁻⁹) = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵ / 6.56 × 10⁻⁷ = 3.03 × 10⁻¹⁹ J (1 for substitution, 1 for answer + units).

Q4 — DEDUCE [2 marks] "Successive IEs (kJ mol⁻¹): 496, 4563, 6913, 9544. Deduce the group, with a reason."

Model answer: Group 1 (1) — a huge jump after the first IE means the second electron comes from a full inner shell (1). (This is sodium.)

Q5 — WRITE [2 marks] "Write the full electron configuration of Cr (Z = 24)."

Model answer: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 4s¹ 3d⁵ (1) — the half-filled 3d⁵ is more stable than 4s² 3d⁴ (1).

Cr and Cu are the two exceptions. Cu = [Ar]4s¹3d¹⁰.
