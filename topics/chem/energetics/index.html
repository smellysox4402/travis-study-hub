THE THERMOMETER 🌡️

How hot is the dance floor, who's giving heat and who's taking it — and how we put a number on it.

SYSTEM, SURROUNDINGS, UNIVERSE (R1.1.1)

universe = system + surroundings — and total energy is conserved.

  • System = the reacting chemicals (the flask contents).
  • Surroundings = everything else (the flask, the air, you).
  • Energy transfers between them — most commonly as heat, but also electricity, sound or light (fireworks!).
OPEN

🍺

Matter AND energy cross the boundary (an open beaker).

CLOSED

🥤

Energy crosses, matter does not (a sealed flask in a water bath).

ISOLATED

🧊

Neither crosses (a thermos flask — the calorimeter ideal).

ENDOTHERMIC vs EXOTHERMIC (R1.1.2)

ExothermicEndothermic
Directionheat flows out of the system → surroundingsheat is absorbed from the surroundings into the system
ΔHnegativepositive
Temperaturemixture warms up (e.g. combustion, neutralisation)mixture cools down (e.g. NH₄NO₃ dissolving — feels cold)

MEMORY HOOK: EXit = EXothermic — energy EXits the system. EnTER = ENdothermic — energy ENters.

RELATIVE STABILITY (R1.1.3)

Whether a reaction is endo or exo comes down to which side is more stable:

  • Products more stable (lower enthalpy) → energy released → exothermic.
  • Products less stable (higher enthalpy) → energy absorbed → endothermic.
  • Lower energy = more stable — same logic as a ball rolling downhill.
Figure 1 — Energy profiles. The hump is activation energy (Eₐ) — always positive, present in both directions. ΔH is the net drop (exo) or rise (endo) between reactants and products. Eₐ ≠ ΔH, ever.
EXAM TRAP: exothermic doesn't mean "fast". Petrol + oxygen is hugely exothermic but needs a spark — a huge Eₐ. Thermodynamics says WHICH way is possible; kinetics says HOW FAST (see the Kinetics guide).

STANDARD ENTHALPY CHANGE ΔH° (R1.1.4)

ΔH° = heat transferred at constant pressure, under standard conditions and with all species in their standard states. Units: kJ mol⁻¹.

  • STP = 273.15 K and 100 kPa (gases).
  • SATP = 298.15 K and 100 kPa — the practical "room temperature" standard.
  • The ° means standard conditions; the "per mole" refers to the reaction as written (look at the coefficients!).
READ THE UNITS: ΔH = −14.6 kJ mol⁻¹ for "1 mol of LiCl dissolving" means −14.6 kJ per mole of LiCl — if 2 mol of LiCl reacted, double it.

THE MONEY EQUATION: q = mcΔT (R1.1.4)

q = m × c × ΔT  →  ΔH = −q / n

  • q = heat absorbed/released by the substance (J or kJ)
  • m = mass of the substance being heated (kg if c is in kJ kg⁻¹ K⁻¹)
  • c = specific heat capacity — heat to raise 1 kg by 1 K
  • ΔT = temperature change (K — a Δ of 1 K = a Δ of 1 °C, so you can use °C differences)
  • n = amount of reactant (mol) — and the minus sign because heat gained by the water = heat lost by the reaction.
Substancec / kJ kg⁻¹ K⁻¹
water4.18
ethanol2.44
copper0.385

Lower c → same heat causes a bigger temperature jump. Water's high c is why it's the calorimeter liquid of choice — big ΔT signal, gentle errors.

CALORIMETRY — READING THE DANCE FLOOR (R1.1.4)

COFFEE-CUP

☕ Solution calorimetry

React in a (styrofoam/insulated) cup of water; measure ΔT of the solution. Used for reactions in solution: neutralisation, displacement, dissolving.

BOMB

💣 Bomb calorimeter

Sample burned in a sealed steel "bomb" with excess O₂; water jacket around it. Used for combustion (fuels, foods).

  • Alcohol lamp setup: burn the fuel under a can of water, measure the water's ΔT, weigh fuel consumed → ΔHc.
  • Thermometric titration: add base to acid, plot T vs volume — the maximum temperature lands at the stoichiometric point (find it by extrapolating the two straight sections).
ERROR CONTROL — THE MARK-GRABBER: heat escapes to the surroundings → measured ΔT is too small → q too small → |ΔH| comes out smaller than the true value (systematic error). Fixes: insulate the cup, use a lid, stir, plot T vs time and extrapolate the cooling line back to the moment of mixing to recover the "true" ΔT.

WORKED EXAMPLE 1 — LiCl DISSOLVING (YOUR COURSE COMPANION, p.395)

1.15 g of LiCl (M = 42.39 g mol⁻¹) dissolves in 25 g of water; ΔT = +3.8 K.

  1. q = mcΔT = 0.025 × 4.18 × 3.8 = 0.397 kJ absorbed by the water
  2. n(LiCl) = 1.15 ÷ 42.39 = 0.0271 mol
  3. ΔH = −q/n = −0.397 ÷ 0.0271 = −14.6 kJ mol⁻¹ (exothermic dissolving)
METHOD CHECK: the ΔT is positive (water warmed), so q is positive, so ΔH must wear a minus — the reaction released the heat the water absorbed.

WORKED EXAMPLE 2 — Zn + CuSO₄ DISPLACEMENT (YOUR COURSE COMPANION, p.397)

Coffee-cup calorimeter: 1.37 g Zn powder into 25.0 cm³ of 1.0 mol dm⁻³ CuSO₄ (mass ≈ 28.8 g). q = mcΔT = 4.69 kJ.

  1. n(Zn) = 1.37 ÷ 65.38 = 0.0210 mol; n(CuSO₄) = 0.0250 × 1.0 = 0.0250 mol
  2. Limiting reactant = Zn (0.0210 < 0.0250) — per 1:1 equation
  3. ΔH = −q/n(Zn) = −4.69 ÷ 0.0210 = −223 kJ mol⁻¹
EXAM HABIT: ALWAYS check the limiting reactant before dividing q by n. Dividing by the wrong mole count is a classic full-marks-to-zero mistake.

WORKED EXAMPLE 3 — METHANOL COMBUSTION (YOUR COURSE COMPANION, p.399)

Spirit-burner method: methanol burned under a beaker of water. The book's experimental value: ΔHc(CH₃OH) = −359 kJ mol⁻¹ — versus the data booklet's −726 kJ mol⁻¹.

  • Why the gap? Heat lost to the air, the beaker and the burner never reaches the water → measured q too small.
  • Incomplete combustion (sooty flame) also releases less energy.
REAL-WORLD LESSON: this is why bomb calorimeters exist — they trap almost all the heat. The coffee-cup/set-up value is expected to be a low estimate, and examiners want you to say exactly that.

THE ROUNDABOUT 🔄

There's more than one road between two songs. Hess's law says the route doesn't matter — only where you start and where you end.

BOND-BREAKING vs BOND-FORMING (R1.2.1)

  • Bond-breaking absorbs energy (endothermic — you pay to snap the bond).
  • Bond-forming releases energy (exothermic — the system pays you back).
  • Average bond enthalpies (data booklet section 12) are mean values across many compounds — useful, but approximate.

ΔH = Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds formed)

EXAM ROUTINE: draw the structural formulas → count every bond broken (reactants) and formed (products) → multiply by its bond enthalpy → subtract. If ΔH < 0, forming stronger bonds won the energy battle.

HESS'S LAW — THE ROUNDABOUT ITSELF (R1.2.2)

Enthalpy change is independent of the pathway between initial and final states.

Enthalpy is a state function — like your elevation on a hike. Climb the mountain or walk around it, you end at the same height; the route's difficulty doesn't change the net ΔH. This lets us calculate ΔH for reactions we can't even do in the lab.

WHY IT'S LEGAL: it's the law of conservation of energy in disguise. If two different routes gave different ΔH for the same start → finish, you could create or destroy energy by looping the routes.

THE TWO FUEL TYPES OF DATA (R1.2.3)

ΔHc°

🔥 STANDARD ENTHALPY OF COMBUSTION

1 mole of substance burned completely in oxygen, standard states. C₄H₁₀: −2878 kJ mol⁻¹. Data booklet section 14.

ΔHf°

🏗️ STANDARD ENTHALPY OF FORMATION

1 mole of substance formed from its elements in standard states. ΔHf(butane) = −126 kJ mol⁻¹. Data booklet section 13.

  • Elements in their standard state have ΔHf = 0 — it costs nothing to "form" an element from itself.
  • Carbon's standard allotrope = graphite (diamond and buckminsterfullerene have nonzero ΔHf).
  • Fractional coefficients are allowed in combustion/formation equations (e.g. ½O₂ for H₂O formation) — one of the rare places fractions are correct.

THE TWO HESS FORMULAS (R1.2.4)

ΔH° = ΣΔHf°(products) − ΣΔHf°(reactants)

ΔH° = ΣΔHc°(reactants) − ΣΔHc°(products)

Note the swap! Formation: products minus reactants. Combustion: reactants minus products (because combustion always moves everything DOWN towards CO₂ + H₂O).

Figure 2 — Hess cycle for pentane formation from combustion data. Both routes from 5C + 6H₂ to C₅H₁₂ have the same ΔH. ΔHf = 5×ΔHc(C) + 6×ΔHc(H₂) − ΔHc(C₅H₁₂). Follow arrows; reverse an arrow = flip its sign.

WORKED EXAMPLE 4 — ΔHf OF PENTANE FROM COMBUSTION DATA (p.415)

Target: 5C(s) + 6H₂(g) → C₅H₁₂(l). Book data: ΔHc(C) = −394, ΔHc(H₂) = −286, ΔHc(C₅H₁₂) = −3509 kJ mol⁻¹.

  1. Multiply to match moles: 5 × (−394) = −1970; 6 × (−286) = −1716
  2. Reverse pentane combustion (it's a product here, not a reactant): +3509
  3. ΔHf = −1970 + (−1716) + 3509 = −177 kJ mol⁻¹

The data booklet quotes −173 kJ mol⁻¹ — small difference because average bond enthalpies (and rounding) make combustion-derived values approximate. Practice question 6 asks exactly this: "suggest why they differ."

WORKED EXAMPLE 5 — ΔHc OF PENTANE FROM FORMATION DATA (p.416)

Target: C₅H₁₂(l) + 8O₂(g) → 5CO₂(g) + 6H₂O(l). ΔHf(C₅H₁₂) = −173, ΔHf(CO₂) = −394, ΔHf(H₂O) = −286 kJ mol⁻¹.

  1. Reverse pentane formation: +173
  2. Products: 5 × (−394) = −1970; 6 × (−286) = −1716
  3. ΔHc = 173 + (−1970) + (−1716) = −3513 kJ mol⁻¹
WHICH FORMULA WHEN? If the question hands you ΔHf values → use the formation formula. If it hands you ΔHc values → use the combustion formula. If it hands you bond enthalpies → use bond breaking − bond forming. Three tools, one law.

THE STAIRCASE 🪜

Born–Haber: the full energy staircase from elements up to a crystal lattice — and it's just Hess's law wearing a fancy hat.

THE STAIRCASE STEPS (R1.2.5 · AHL)

To form an ionic compound MX(s) from its elements, climb (or descend) these steps — each is a standard enthalpy change:

StepProcessSignNotes
ΔHat (atomization)M(s) → M(g); ½X₂(g) → X(g)+ (endo)= sublimation enthalpy for solid metals; = ½ bond enthalpy for diatomic gases
IE (ionization energy)M(g) → M⁺(g) + e⁻+ (endo)IE₁, IE₂, … for multi-valent metals (data booklet section 9)
EA (electron affinity)X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g)usually − (exo)Exceptions: He, N (small atoms, extra electron costly); 2nd EA of O is POSITIVE
ΔHlattice (lattice enthalpy)MX(s) → M⁺(g) + X⁻(g)+ (endo) — as defined in DPgaseous ions ← solid lattice; data booklet section 16 (experimental values)
LATTICE ENTHALPY TRAP: DP chemistry ALWAYS uses the endothermic direction (breaking the lattice into gaseous ions, ΔH > 0). Industry sometimes quotes the negative lattice "formation" value — the IB exam expects the positive one.

ASSEMBLING THE CYCLE — NaF AS THE CLASSIC (R1.2.5 · AHL)

Figure 3 — Born–Haber cycle for NaF. Follow the red route (formation) vs the stair route: ΔHf = ΔHat(Na) + IE + ½BE(F₂) + EA + (−ΔHlattice). Check: 108 + 496 + 79 + (−328) + (−923) = −568 ≈ −569 (tiny rounding differences between data sources). The unknown step is always the one you solve for.
THE EXAM PATTERN: they give you a cycle with one missing value. Write "sum of the loop = 0", substitute the known arrows (flip signs for downward paths), solve. That's the whole trick.

WORKED EXAMPLE 6 — KBr LATTICE ENTHALPY (YOUR COURSE COMPANION, p.420)

Given: ΔHf(KBr) = −392, ΔHat(K) = +89, IE(K) = +419, ΔHat(Br) = +112, EA(Br) = −325 kJ mol⁻¹. Find ΔHlattice(KBr).

  1. Route from KBr(s) up to K⁺(g) + Br⁻(g): reverse formation (−(−392) = +392), then atomize K (+89), ionize (+419), atomize Br (+112), add electron (−325)
  2. ΔHlattice = 392 + 89 + 419 + 112 + (−325) = +687 kJ mol⁻¹
ALWAYS POSITIVE: lattice enthalpy in DP = breaking the lattice apart = endothermic = positive. If your answer comes out negative, you've walked the loop the wrong way.

WHY LATTICE ENTHALPY MATTERS (R1.2.5 · AHL)

  • Lattice enthalpy ∝ charge × charge / distance — bigger ionic charges (MgO vs NaCl) and smaller ions (F⁻ vs I⁻) → stronger lattice → higher ΔHlattice.
  • Born–Haber values can be compared against theoretical (ionic-model) values → deviation reveals covalent character (the ionic model is an approximation).
  • It explains melting points, hardness and solubility trends across ionic compounds.

THE FUEL TANK ⛽

What actually keeps the party running — from burning metals to hydrogen fuel cells. And the dark side: what happens when the oxygen runs short.

COMBUSTION — THE UNIVERSAL BURN (R1.3.1)

Combustion = burning in oxygen. Metals, non-metals and organic compounds all do it — different guests, same oxygen bouncer.

METALS

⚡ redox, ionic product

4Li + O₂ → 2Li₂O; 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO. Metal oxidized (loses e⁻), oxygen reduced. Half-equations: Li → Li⁺ + e⁻; O₂ + 4e⁻ → 2O²⁻.

NON-METALS

💨 acidic oxides

S + O₂ → SO₂; then 2SO₂ + O₂ → 2SO₃; then SO₃ + H₂O → H₂SO₄ — the acid-rain cascade (and industrial sulfuric acid!).

ORGANICS

🛢️ fuel class

Alkane: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ + (3n+1)/2 O₂ → nCO₂ + (n+1)H₂O. Alcohol: CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH + 3n/2 O₂ → nCO₂ + (n+1)H₂O.

  • Volatility matters: a fuel must vaporize to burn. Short-chain alkanes are more volatile → better fuels (LPG = mostly propane).
  • Octane: C₈H₁₈ + 25/2 O₂ → 8CO₂ + 9H₂O, ΔHc = −5470 kJ mol⁻¹. Ethanol: ΔHc = −1367 kJ mol⁻¹.
STATE SYMBOLS ARE MARKED: water from a car engine is H₂O(g) (steam); in the lab it's H₂O(l). The ΔH differs — examiners watch for the state in the equation.

INCOMPLETE COMBUSTION — WHEN OXYGEN RUNS SHORT (R1.3.2)

In a car engine, oxygen becomes the limiting reactant → the fuel can't fully burn:

  • Product instead of CO₂: carbon monoxide CO(g) — poisonous, binds irreversibly to haemoglobin, suffocating you from the inside (odourless killer).
  • Or elemental carbon C(s) = soot — black deposits (think a sooty Bunsen flame).
  • CH₄ + ³⁄₂O₂ → CO + 2H₂O  and  CH₄ + O₂ → C + 2H₂O
  • Incomplete combustion releases less energy than complete.
LINKING QUESTION: longer-chain hydrocarbons have a greater tendency to incomplete combustion (harder to vaporize, less mixing with O₂). Complete combustion of any hydrocarbon always gives CO₂ + H₂O — full stop.

FOSSIL FUELS — THE CLASSIC TRACKLIST (R1.3.3)

FuelCompositionProsCons
Coalsolid, up to 3% sulfurabundant, cheapmost CO₂ per GWh, SO₂ → acid rain

  • Specific energy = energy released per mass (MJ kg⁻¹). Wood < coal < ethanol < biodiesel < crude oil < diesel < gasoline < natural gas.
  • Greenhouse effect: CO₂ (≈0.04% of air) absorbs infrared, vibrates, re-emits some back to Earth → global warming. N₂ and O₂ (99%+) do NOT absorb IR — that's why CO₂'s small share matters so much.
  • Other greenhouse gases: methane, N₂O, water vapour, HFCs. Historic ice-core CO₂ peaks are far below today's levels.

BIOFUELS — THE RECYCLED BEAT (R1.3.4)

Biofuels come from biological carbon fixation — recent photosynthesis, not ancient buried carbon.

  • Photosynthesis: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ (sunlight → chemical energy)
  • Fermentation: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂ (glucose → ethanol biofuel)
  • Ethanol's combustion CO₂ is roughly offset by the CO₂ absorbed during the crop's growth → lower carbon footprint than fossil fuels, though not zero (fermentation itself emits CO₂).

FUEL CELLS — ELECTRICITY WITHOUT THE BONFIRE (R1.3.5)

A fuel cell converts a fuel's chemical energy directly to electrical energy. Unlike a battery (finite reactants sealed inside), a fuel cell is continuously supplied with fuel → keeps producing as long as it's fed.

Figure 4 — Hydrogen fuel cell. H₂ oxidized at the anode (with a Pt catalyst): H₂ → 2H⁺ + 2e⁻. Electrons take the external circuit (electricity!), protons cross the PEM, and at the cathode O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O. Overall: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O — only product is water.
  • Where does the H₂ come from? Electrolysis of water (2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂ — clean if powered by renewables) or steam reforming of hydrocarbons (produces CO + CO₂ — the fossil-fuel catch).
  • Direct-methanol fuel cell (DMFC): CH₃OH + H₂O → CO₂ + 6H⁺ + 6e⁻ at the anode; overall CH₃OH + ³⁄₂O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. Methanol is liquid (easy to store), higher energy density than H₂ gas and Li-ion batteries, but toxic and needs precious-metal catalysts (Ru, Pd).
  • Primary (voltaic) cells: irreversible, finite reactants (e.g. zinc–MnO₂). Secondary cells: rechargeable (lead-acid, Li-ion).
MARK-GRABBER: "a fuel cell converts chemical energy directly into electrical energy, with fuel supplied continuously from an external source — so it can produce electricity indefinitely, unlike a voltaic cell whose reactants are finite."

THE BOTTOM LINE 🎲

Will the party happen at all? Entropy counts the mess, and Gibbs energy is the DJ's final decision.

ENTROPY — THE MESS METER (R1.4.1 · AHL)

Entropy S = a measure of the dispersal/distribution of matter and/or energy in a system. More ways to spread the energy → higher S.

  • Same conditions: S(gas) > S(liquid) > S(solid) — gas molecules have the most freedom.
  • Boltzmann's tombstone: S = k·log W — W = number of microstates (ways energy can be arranged).
  • Melting and boiling increase S; condensing and freezing decrease it.
"DISORDER" IS A SHORTHAND: officially it's about dispersal of matter and energy. Say "more ways for energy to be distributed" in the exam — the marker words are dispersal/distribution.

THE SECOND LAW — TOTAL ENTROPY (R1.4.1 · AHL)

ΔStotal = ΔSsystem + ΔSsurroundings

ΔStotalVerdict
> 0spontaneous (forward reaction happens)
= 0equilibrium — neither direction favoured
< 0non-spontaneous as written — the REVERSE reaction is spontaneous
TRICKY BIT: a reaction can be spontaneous and still have ΔSsystem < 0 (e.g. freezing). What must be positive is the total — the surroundings can pay for the system's order by releasing heat.

PREDICTING ΔS — COUNT THE GAS MOLES (R1.4.1 · AHL)

  • States matter: solid → liquid → gas = entropy UP.
  • Moles of gas dominate: more moles of gas on the product side → ΔS > 0. Condensed phases barely count — ignore solids/liquids when gases are present.
  • NH₄Cl(s) → NH₃(g) + HCl(g): 1 mol solid → 2 mol gas → ΔS > 0 ✓
  • 2C₂H₆(g) + 7O₂(g) → 4CO₂(g) + 6H₂O(l): 9 mol gas → 4 mol gas → ΔS < 0 ✓

Quantitative: ΔS° = ΣS°(products) − ΣS°(reactants), using standard entropy values (data booklet section 13, units J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ — keep the coefficients!).

WORKED CHECK (book p.446): H₂(g) + Cl₂(g) → 2HCl(g): ΔS° = [2(187)] − [131 + 223] = +20 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ — small positive, because 2 mol gas → 2 mol gas (no mole change, tiny ordering effect). N₂O₄(g) → 2NO₂(g): (2 × 240) − 304 = +176 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ — big jump, 1 → 2 mol gas.

GIBBS ENERGY — THE DECISION EQUATION (R1.4.2 · R1.4.3 · AHL)

ΔG = ΔH − TΔS

  • ΔG = change in Gibbs energy (kJ mol⁻¹) — the maximum useful energy obtainable from the reaction.
  • It bundles the system's entropy change (ΔS, direct) with the surroundings' entropy change (via ΔH, indirect — the heat dumped into or pulled from the surroundings).
  • Spontaneous at constant pressure ⇔ ΔG < 0. Non-spontaneous ⇔ ΔG > 0. Equilibrium ⇔ ΔG = 0.
  • Watch the units: ΔH in kJ mol⁻¹, ΔS in J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ → convert ΔS to kJ (÷1000) before multiplying by T.
ΔHΔSResultSpontaneity
− (exo)+ (disorder up)ΔG always −spontaneous at ANY temperature
+ (endo)− (order up)ΔG always +never spontaneous (reverse is)
− (exo)− (order up)ΔG − at low T onlyspontaneous at LOW T (when |TΔS| < |ΔH|)
+ (endo)+ (disorder up)ΔG − at high T onlyspontaneous at HIGH T (when TΔS > ΔH)
THE THRESHOLD TEMPERATURE: when ΔH and ΔS share a sign, set ΔG = 0 → T = ΔH / ΔS is the temperature where spontaneity flips. Classic example (book p.451): CH₄ + H₂O → 3H₂ + CO with ΔH = +205 kJ mol⁻¹, ΔS = +216 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ → T = 205 ÷ 0.216 = 949 K — spontaneous above 949 K, non-spontaneous at 298 K (ΔG = +141 kJ mol⁻¹).
Figure 5 — ΔG vs T for the four sign combinations. Below the gold zero-line = spontaneous. The crossing point of any diagonal line with ΔG = 0 is T = ΔH/ΔS.

ΔG AND EQUILIBRIUM (R1.4.4 · AHL)

  • As a reversible reaction runs, ΔG approaches 0 — it becomes less negative and finally reaches zero at equilibrium (the Gibbs minimum).
  • Reaction quotient Q = [products]/[reactants] at any instant; Q = K at equilibrium.
  • Q < K → forward favoured; Q > K → reverse favoured; Q = K → equilibrium.
  • ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q  →  at equilibrium (ΔG = 0, Q = K): ΔG° = −RT ln K, so K = e^(−ΔG°/RT) (R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ — convert ΔG° to J!).
KWho wins at equilibriumΔG°
K > 1products favourednegative
K = 1nobodyzero
K < 1reactants favouredpositive
WORKED EXAMPLE 7 — HABER (book p.456): N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, ΔH = −92 kJ mol⁻¹, ΔS = −202 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. (a) ΔG = −92 − 298(−0.202) = −31.8 kJ mol⁻¹ → spontaneous at 298 K. (b) ΔG° = −RT ln K → ln K = 31800/(8.31 × 298) → K = 3.77 × 10⁵ → products hugely favoured. (c) At Q = 1 × 10⁶ (products in big excess), ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q = +2.41 kJ mol⁻¹ → not spontaneous at that instant — the reverse reaction is favoured until equilibrium.

THERMODYNAMIC vs KINETIC — THE FINAL TRAP (R1.4.3 · AHL)

⚖️

SPONTANEOUS ≠ FAST

the "ice in the cold room" trap

  • A reaction can be thermodynamically favourable (ΔG < 0) and still not happen at a visible rate — because its activation energy is high (kinetically unfavourable).
  • Example: diamond → graphite has ΔG < 0, yet diamonds don't crumble in your ring — the Eₐ is enormous.
  • ΔG says whether; kinetics (Reactivity 2.2) says how fast. Keep the two languages separate in your answers.

Prove it

SELF-TEST ✍

No peeking at the acts above. Pick an answer, get instant feedback.

1. In an exothermic reaction, heat flows...

✅ Heat flows OUT. EXit = EXothermic. ΔH is negative.
❌ Exothermic = heat leaves the system (ΔH < 0). Heat entering the system is endothermic.

2. 1.15 g of LiCl (M = 42.39) dissolves in 25 g water, ΔT = +3.8 K. What is ΔH per mole of LiCl?

✅ q = 0.025 × 4.18 × 3.8 = 0.397 kJ; n = 1.15/42.39 = 0.0271 mol; ΔH = −0.397/0.0271 = −14.6 kJ mol⁻¹. Water warmed → reaction released heat → negative.
❌ q = 0.397 kJ, n = 0.0271 mol, ΔH = −q/n = −14.6 kJ mol⁻¹. The water GAINED heat, so the reaction LOST it (negative).

3. In the coffee-cup Zn + CuSO₄ experiment, why divide q by n(Zn) and not n(CuSO₄)?

✅ Zn is limiting (0.0210 mol vs 0.0250 mol CuSO₄) — it defines how much reaction actually happened. ΔH is per mole of reaction.
❌ n(Zn) = 0.0210 < n(CuSO₄) = 0.0250 → Zn limits. Always check the limiting reactant before dividing.

4. A spirit-burner calorimetry experiment measures ΔHc of methanol as −359 kJ mol⁻¹ vs the true −726 kJ mol⁻¹. Why the gap?

✅ Heat loss = systematic error → ΔT (and q) too small → |ΔH| too small. Insulation + extrapolating the cooling curve fixes it.
❌ Heat loss to the air/beaker/burner makes the measured q an underestimate. That's the classic systematic error.

5. In ΔH = Σ(bonds broken) − Σ(bonds formed), which process ABSORBS energy?

✅ Breaking costs energy (endothermic); forming releases it. The formula subtracts the released energy from the spent energy.
❌ Bond-breaking absorbs energy. Bond-forming releases — that's why it sits on the minus side of the formula.

6. Which of these has ΔHf° = 0?

✅ Graphite is carbon's standard state — an element in its standard state has ΔHf = 0. Diamond and buckminsterfullerene are allotropes with nonzero ΔHf.
❌ Graphite — the standard allotrope of carbon. Only elements in their standard states score the zero.

7. ΔH° = ΣΔHf°(products) − ΣΔHf°(reactants). Why does the COMBUSTION version flip to reactants − products?

✅ ΔHc measures the drop TOWARD CO₂/H₂O, so the products sit at the bottom: ΔH = ΔHc(reactants) − ΔHc(products). Not a typo — learn both.
❌ The combustion formula is real: reactants − products, because combustion values measure the fall to CO₂ + H₂O. Formation stays products − reactants.

8. In DP chemistry, lattice enthalpy is...

✅ DP defines lattice enthalpy as MX(s) → M⁺(g) + X⁻(g) — lattice breaking — which is endothermic, so positive. If your Born–Haber answer is negative, you walked the loop backwards.
❌ Positive. The IB definition is the endothermic lattice-breaking direction. The exothermic "lattice formation" value is the industry convention — don't use it.

9. Incomplete combustion of a hydrocarbon can produce...

✅ Limited oxygen → CO and/or soot, and less energy released. CO binds haemoglobin irreversibly — the odourless killer.
❌ Incomplete combustion → CO and/or C(s). It releases LESS heat, never more, and never produces H₂/O₂.

10. The only product of a hydrogen fuel cell is...

✅ 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O — water only. (The catch is upstream: H₂ from steam reforming emits CO/CO₂; electrolysis is clean if renewable-powered.)
❌ Water only. CO₂ appears if the FUEL is carbon-based (DMFC), but a hydrogen fuel cell emits just H₂O.

11. Under the same conditions, which state has the HIGHEST entropy?

✅ Gas > liquid > solid — gas particles have the most freedom and the most ways to distribute energy.
❌ Gas wins. More freedom of movement → more ways to disperse energy → highest S.

12. ΔH > 0 and ΔS > 0. When is the reaction spontaneous?

✅ Endothermic + disorder up: the TΔS term grows with T and eventually beats ΔH → spontaneous at high T. Flip point: T = ΔH/ΔS.
❌ High T only. ΔH+ ΔS− is never; ΔH− ΔS+ is always; same-sign combos flip at T = ΔH/ΔS.

13. At equilibrium, ΔG and ΔG° relate as...

✅ At equilibrium Q = K and ΔG = 0 → ΔG° = −RT ln K. K > 1 → ΔG° negative → products favoured.
❌ ΔG = 0 at equilibrium, and that rearranges ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q into ΔG° = −RT ln K.

14. Diamond → graphite has ΔG < 0, yet diamonds are stable for millennia. Why?

✅ ΔG < 0 says it COULD happen; a massive Eₐ means it doesn't at any noticeable rate. Spontaneous ≠ fast.
❌ Thermodynamics says yes, kinetics says no — the activation energy is enormous. That's the thermodynamically-favourable-but-slow trap.

🎯 EXAM WALKTHROUGHS

Q1 — DEFINE [3 marks] "Define standard enthalpy change of combustion."

Model answer: the enthalpy change when one mole of a substance (1) burns completely in oxygen (1) under standard conditions (1).

Three clauses = three marks. Miss "one mole" or "complete" and you lose marks even though you "know" it.

Q2 — CALCULATE (calorimetry) [3 marks] "0.92 g of ethanol burns and heats 200 g of water by 15.0 K. (a) Calculate q. (b) State why this underestimates ΔHc."

Model answer: (a) q = mcΔT = 0.200 × 4.18 × 15.0 = 12.5 kJ (2) · (b) heat is lost to the surroundings / incomplete combustion, so less heat reaches the water (1).

Q3 — CALCULATE (bond enthalpies) [3 marks] "Estimate ΔH for CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. C–H 413, O=O 498, C=O 799, O–H 463 kJ mol⁻¹."

Model answer: broken = 4(413) + 2(498) = 2648 · formed = 2(799) + 4(463) = 3450 → ΔH = 2648 − 3450 = −802 kJ mol⁻¹ (1 for each total, 1 for the answer).

Data booklet gives −890 kJ mol⁻¹ — the gap is because bond enthalpies are averages. State that and you bank the "suggest why they differ" mark.

Q4 — CALCULATE (Gibbs) [3 marks] "CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g): ΔH = +178 kJ mol⁻¹, ΔS = +161 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Calculate ΔG at 298 K and comment."

Model answer: ΔG = 178 − (298 × 0.161) = 178 − 48.0 = +130 kJ mol⁻¹ (2) → non-spontaneous at 298 K (1). Threshold T = 178/0.161 ≈ 1106 K — spontaneous only above ~1100 K.

Q5 — CALCULATE (Hess via ΔHc) [3 marks] "Calculate ΔH for C₂H₄ + H₂ → C₂H₆ using ΔHc: C₂H₄ −1411, H₂ −286, C₂H₆ −1560 kJ mol⁻¹."

Model answer: ΔH = ΣΔHc(reactants) − ΣΔHc(products) = (−1411 − 286) − (−1560) = −137 kJ mol⁻¹ (1 for formula, 1 for substitution, 1 for answer).
