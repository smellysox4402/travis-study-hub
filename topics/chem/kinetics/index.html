Rate of reaction

Rate is the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time.

RATE OF REACTION = CHANGE IN CONCENTRATION PER UNIT TIME

rate = Δ[reactant or product] / Δt — units mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹

  • Concentration of reactants ↓ over time; concentration of products ↑.
  • Average rate = chord between two points on a concentration–time graph.
  • Instantaneous rate = gradient of the tangent at a given time.
  • Initial rate = gradient of the tangent at t = 0 — the standard exam ask.

Rate is NOT constant — the curve flattens as reactants run out. Initial rate is the only clean rate to compare.

HOW WE MEASURE RATE (R2.2.1)

GAS REACTIONS

Gas syringe 🎈

Volume of gas produced vs time. Or loss of mass on a balance (if gas escapes).

COLOUR CHANGES

Colorimetry 🎨

Light absorbance changes as a coloured reactant is used up or a coloured product forms.

SOLUTIONS

Titration / pH / conductivity ⚗️

Sample the mixture and titrate, or monitor pH / conductivity live as ions change.

Every method is secretly the same job: get concentration against time, then draw tangents.

Collision theory (R2.2.2)

For a reaction to happen, particles must:

  1. Collide — no contact, no reaction. Ever.
  2. Collide with the right orientation — the reactive ends must line up (like a key in a lock — not every angle works).
  3. Collide with energy ≥ activation energy (Eₐ) — enough kinetic energy to break/weaken the old bonds and start making new ones.

So rate depends on the frequency of successful collisions — not all collisions, just the ones that pass all three checks.

Activation energy (R2.2.4)

Eₐ = the minimum energy colliding particles need for a reaction to occur.

  • Old bonds must break or weaken before new bonds fully form — that costs energy.
  • At the transition state, bonds are half-broken/half-formed — highest energy point of the reaction.
  • The activated complex lives at the transition state — it's not a stable molecule, just a fleeting arrangement.
EXAM TRAP: Eₐ is NOT ΔH. Eₐ is the energy hill to climb; ΔH is the overall energy difference between start and finish. You can have a very exothermic reaction with a huge activation energy (e.g. petrol + oxygen — needs a spark first).

Factors affecting rate

What changes the rate, and how a catalyst takes an alternative path.

Knob you turnWhat happens to rateWhy (collision theory)
↑ concentrationincreasesmore particles per volume → more collisions per second
↑ pressure (gases)increasessame as concentration — gas particles squeezed closer together
↑ surface area (solids)increasesmore particles exposed → more collision sites (crushed solid > one lump)
↑ temperatureincreases a LOTtwo bonuses: faster particles → more collisions AND a far bigger share of particles with E ≥ Eₐ (see Act 3)
catalystincreaseslowers Eₐ → far more collisions become successful (see below)
RULE OF THUMB (exam-friendly): for many reactions, rate roughly doubles to quadruples for every +10 °C (0–100 °C). Temperature is the biggest lever — it changes who has enough energy, not just how often they bump.
🪪

Catalysts (R2.2.5)

a substance that increases rate but is NOT consumed

  • Provides an alternative pathway with a lower activation energy (Eₐ′ < Eₐ).
  • Is regenerated unchanged — technically both a reactant AND a product of the reaction.
  • Does NOT change ΔH — the start and finish are the same, only the hill gets smaller.
  • Usually works via a reaction intermediate — a stable-ish species that forms then breaks down (e.g. NO₂ catalysing SO₂ → SO₃ via NO).

Pencil analogy: an intermediate = pencil balancing on its flat end (can sit there a while). A transition state = pencil on its tip (falls instantly).

The energy distribution

Why temperature matters so much — it moves the whole Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution.

Figure 1 — Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution. Higher temperature = flatter, broader curve with the peak lower and shifted right. The shaded region beyond Eₐ (right of the gold line) is the fraction that can react — much bigger at T₂. The area under BOTH curves is identical (same number of particles).

Reading the Maxwell–Boltzmann curve

  • Peak of the curve = kinetic energy of the largest share of particles (the most common speed).
  • Average kinetic energy (Ēₖ) sits slightly to the right of the peak.
  • Area under the curve = total number of particles — unchanged by temperature.
  • Eₐ is fixed — temperature shifts the distribution, not the threshold.
EXAM TRAP: at higher T the peak is LOWER and further right — students often draw it taller. It's a redistribution: the same number of particles spread thinner, with a fatter high-energy tail.

TEMPERATURE vs CATALYST — TWO DIFFERENT MOVES

Temperature ↑Catalyst
EₐUNCHANGEDlowered (Eₐ′)
Ēₖ (average energy)increasedUNCHANGED
Curve shapeflatter, broader, peak rightsame curve — but the Eₐ line moves LEFT
Resultboth: more particles with E ≥ Eₐ → more successful collisions
MARK-GRABBER: "a rise in temperature does not affect Eₐ but increases the average kinetic energy; a catalyst does not affect the average kinetic energy but reduces Eₐ." Write that sentence and you've basically banked the marks.

The rate equation

How the rate depends on concentration, and why orders come from experiment, never the equation.

THE RATE EQUATION (R2.2.9 · AHL)

rate = k [A]ⁿ [B]ᵐ

  • k = rate constant . Independent of concentration, but changes with temperature and catalyst.
  • n, m = orders of reaction — the "power" each reactant has over the rate.
  • Overall order = n + m.
EXAM TRAP — THE BIGGEST ONE IN KINETICS: orders are found EXPERIMENTALLY ONLY. They are NOT the stoichiometric coefficients from the balanced equation. aA + bB → products does NOT mean rate = k[A]ª[B]ᵇ. Never, ever read orders from the equation.

Molecularity (R2.2.8 · SL)

Molecularity = the number of reacting particles taking part in a single elementary step. It's about ONE step of the mechanism, not the whole reaction.

NameParticles in the stepExample step
unimolecular1N₂O₄ → 2NO₂ (a single molecule rearranges / breaks apart)
bimolecular2NO₂ + CO → NO + CO₂ (two particles collide)
termolecular32NO + O₂ → 2NO₂ (rare — three-way collisions are unlikely)
DON'T CONFUSE THE TWO: molecularity is a property of an elementary step (a mechanism fact — always a small whole number). Order is measured from experimental data for the whole reaction. They often match for the RDS — that's why an order of 1 can mean "one particle in the slow step" — but order is always the measured exponent.
Figure 2 — How rate responds to [A]. Doubling [A]: 0th order → rate unchanged; 1st order → rate ×2; 2nd order → rate ×4. Note [B]ᵐ is held constant while you test [A]ⁿ.

ORDERS IN PLAIN WORDS (R2.2.10 · AHL)

Order in AMeaningDoubling [A] → rate
0[A] doesn't matter at all ([A]⁰ = 1)unchanged (×1)
1rate ∝ [A] — one A involved in the slow step×2
2rate ∝ [A]² — two A's (or one fast step feeding two A's in)×4

To find orders experimentally: run the reaction, change ONE concentration, keep everything else fixed, watch how the initial rate responds. That's the initial-rates method.

Units of k (R2.2.11 · AHL)

k's units come from the overall order — you need rate (mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹) divided by the concentration terms, then tidy up:

Overall orderRate equationUnits of k
0rate = kmol dm⁻³ s⁻¹
1rate = k[A]s⁻¹
2rate = k[A]²mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹
3rate = k[A]²[B]mol⁻² dm⁶ s⁻¹

Worked example 7 (book p.502): 2Fe³⁺ + 2I⁻ → 2Fe²⁺ + I₂ — doubling [Fe³⁺] doubles rate (1st order), doubling [I⁻] quadruples rate (2nd order). Overall order 3, so k = 16.2 mol⁻² dm⁶ s⁻¹, and at [Fe³⁺] = [I⁻] = 3.00 × 10⁻², rate = 4.37 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹.

PATTERN TO SPOT: for overall order n, the units of k are always mol¹⁻ⁿ dm³ⁿ⁻³ s⁻¹. Check: n = 1 → mol⁰ dm⁰ s⁻¹ = s⁻¹. ✓

Concentration–time curves (R2.2.10 · AHL)

A single experiment gives you [A] vs time. The shape of that curve hides the order — but 1st and 2nd order curves look almost identical. That's why you linearise them:

Order[A] vs t curvePlot that gives a straight lineSlope
0straight line falling[A] vs t (already linear)−k
1exponential decayln[A] vs t−k
2hyperbolic decay1/[A] vs t+k

Worked example 8 (book p.505): Mg + HCl. The [HCl] vs t curve looks like it could be 1st OR 2nd order. Plot ln[HCl] vs t → curved. Plot 1/[HCl] vs t → straight line. Verdict: second order in HCl. The linear one wins.

MARK-GRABBER: "a linear ln[A] vs t plot confirms first order; a linear 1/[A] vs t plot confirms second order." If asked to determine the order from data, ALWAYS test both plots.

Rate-determining step (R2.2.6 · R2.2.10)

Multi-step reactions are a chain of queues. The rate-determining step (RDS) is the slowest step — it controls the whole rate, exactly like the single slowest step controls the whole reaction's rate.

  • The rate equation reflects the reactants in the slow step (and any fast step that feeds them) — not the overall equation.
  • Example: 2NO₂ + F₂ → 2NO₂F has a slow step NO₂ + F₂ → NO₂F + F, so rate = k[NO₂][F₂] — first order in each, overall 2nd. The second NO₂ enters AFTER the slow step and never touches the rate.
  • Intermediates appear in steps but NEVER in the rate equation (they're made and used within the mechanism).
NATURE OF SCIENCE: the RDS lets us test mechanisms. If a proposed mechanism predicts rate = k[A]² but the lab says rate = k[A], the mechanism is wrong — experiment is the referee.

Mechanism → rate equation — worked example (R2.2.9 · AHL)

Course Companion worked example 5–6 (pp. 497–501): NO₂(g) + CO(g) → NO(g) + CO₂(g). At low temperature the reaction runs in two steps:

  • step 1 (SLOW, RDS): NO₂ + NO₂ → NO₃ + NO
  • step 2 (fast): NO₃ + CO → NO₂ + CO₂

Only species in the slow step (and fast steps feeding it) enter the rate equation → rate = k[NO₂]² — second order in NO₂, zero order in CO (CO appears only after the RDS). Overall order 2. NO₃ is the intermediate — made in step 1, eaten in step 2, absent from both the overall equation AND the rate equation.

At high temperature, the same reaction is a single bimolecular step: NO₂ + CO → NO + CO₂, so rate = k[NO₂][CO] — first order in each, overall 2. Same overall equation, different mechanism, different rate equation.

WHY THIS MATTERS IN THE EXAM: given a mechanism, you must (a) identify the RDS (the step labelled slow), (b) write the rate equation from its reactants only, (c) name the intermediate (present in steps, absent overall), (d) state the molecularity of the RDS (here: bimolecular — two NO₂ particles).
R2.2.7 (SL) · R2.2.12 · R2.2.13 (AHL)

Arrhenius

The equation that links the rate constant to temperature and activation energy.

THE ARRHENIUS EQUATION (R2.2.12 · AHL)

k = A · e^(−Eₐ/RT)

  • k = rate constant (same units as the overall rate ÷ concentrations)
  • A = Arrhenius / frequency factor — measures how often collisions happen with the right orientation. Big A = simple, symmetric particles; small A = big awkward molecules.
  • Eₐ = activation energy (J mol⁻¹ — CONVERT from kJ!)
  • R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ · T = absolute temperature in kelvin

A and Eₐ are (approximately) temperature-independent. k rises exponentially with T — that's the +10°C doubling in disguise.

STRAIGHT-LINE VERSION — THE ARRHENIUS PLOT (R2.2.12 · AHL)

ln k = (−Eₐ/R)(1/T) + ln A  →  y = mx + c

  • Plot ln k vs 1/T → a straight line (do NOT plot ln k vs T — that's a curve and loses the gradient trick).
  • gradient = −Eₐ/R → Eₐ = −gradient × R
  • y-intercept = ln A → A = e^(intercept)
EXAM TRAP — UNITS: Eₐ must be in J mol⁻¹ inside the equation (divide kJ by 1000), and T must be kelvin. The gradient has units of K, so gradient × 8.31 gives J mol⁻¹ — then convert to kJ for a sensible answer.
Figure 3 — Arrhenius plot. Steeper slope → bigger Eₐ. Two-point version (no graph needed): ln(k₁/k₂) = (Eₐ/R)(1/T₂ − 1/T₁). If concentrations are constant you can substitute rates for k.

WORKED EXAMPLE (YOUR COURSE COMPANION, p.507)

N₂O₅ decomposition (first order). The ln k vs 1/T plot passes through (0.00302, −6.8) and (0.00334, −10.8).

  1. gradient = (−6.8 − (−10.8)) / (0.00302 − 0.00334) = 4.0 / (−0.00032) = −12 500 K
  2. Eₐ = −gradient × R = 12 500 × 8.31 = 104 kJ mol⁻¹
  3. Using another point (0.00296, −6.02): ln A = ln k + Eₐ/RT = −6.02 + 104000/(8.31 × 338)... → A = 2.85 × 10¹³ s⁻¹ (same units as k for a first-order reaction)
METHOD CHECK: pick TWO points on the best-fit line (not data points, not the origin), find the gradient, multiply by −R. If a question gives you k at two temperatures, use ln(k₁/k₂) = (Eₐ/R)(1/T₂ − 1/T₁) straight away.

Energy profiles (R2.2.7 · SL)

  • Catalyst path = two small hills (two transition states) with a valley (intermediate) between them.
  • Uncatalysed path = one big hill.
  • Both start and end at the same heights → same ΔH.
  • ΔH < 0 = exothermic (products lower); ΔH > 0 = endothermic (products higher).
  • Multi-step mechanism (book figs 18–19): one intermediate → two transition states — the taller hill is the RDS's activation energy Eₐ. The intermediate (valley) is less stable than reactants; the RDS hill (Eₐ₁) is higher than the second (Eₐ₂).

SELF-TEST ✍

No peeking at the acts above. Pick an answer, get instant feedback.

1. How do you get the INITIAL rate from a concentration–time graph?

✅ Tangent at t = 0 — the steepest, cleanest point on the curve.
❌ It's the tangent at t = 0. Chords give AVERAGE rates; the initial rate is the instantaneous rate at the start.

2. What is activation energy?

✅ Minimum energy needed. The ΔH answer is the trap — that's the overall energy difference, not the hill.
❌ Eₐ = minimum energy for a reaction to occur. ΔH (reactants → products difference) is the tempting wrong answer.

3. When temperature rises, what happens to the Maxwell–Boltzmann curve?

✅ Flatter + broader + peak shifted right. Same area, fatter tail beyond Eₐ → more reactive particles.
❌ Higher T flattens and broadens the curve — peak LOWER and further right. Area stays the same.

4. How does a catalyst speed up a reaction?

✅ Alternative pathway, lower Eₐ. It does NOT touch particle energies or collision frequency.
❌ A catalyst lowers Eₐ via an alternative pathway. Raising Ēₖ is what temperature does; more collisions is concentration/pressure.

5. A catalyst changes the rate but what stays the SAME?

✅ ΔH is fixed by reactants and products. k RISES (that's the point) and Eₐ falls.
❌ ΔH stays the same. k increases and Eₐ decreases — those are exactly what the catalyst changes.

6. Where do reaction orders come from?

✅ Experiments only. The balanced equation is a lying partner — orders can be 0, fractional, anything.
❌ Experimental data, always. Never the balanced equation — that's the classic trap. (RDS helps explain orders, but you still MEASURE them.)

7. Reaction is 2nd order in A. You double [A]. Rate goes...

✅ rate ∝ [A]² → 2² = 4×. First order would be ×2, zero order ×1.
❌ Second order = [A]² → doubling gives 4×. ×2 is first order, unchanged is zero order.

8. In a multi-step reaction, the rate equation matches...

✅ The RDS controls the rate — the reaction goes as fast as its slowest step.
❌ The rate-determining step. The overall equation says nothing about the rate; the fastest step is the opposite of the bottleneck.

9. On a plot of ln k vs 1/T, what does the gradient equal?

✅ gradient = −Eₐ/R → Eₐ = −gradient × 8.31. The intercept is ln A.
❌ −Eₐ/R. Intercept = ln A; Eₐ alone would need units that don't survive the axes.

10. In the Arrhenius equation, Eₐ must be in...

✅ J mol⁻¹ — R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ demands it. kJ must be ×1000 first, and T in kelvin.
❌ J mol⁻¹. R is in J, so Eₐ must be too — convert kJ → J before plugging in.

11. An elementary step is NO₂ + CO → NO + CO₂. What is its molecularity?

✅ Bimolecular — two particles (NO₂ and CO) collide in this one step. Molecularity = particle count per elementary step.
❌ Bimolecular = 2 particles. Unimolecular = 1 (e.g. N₂O₄ → 2NO₂), termolecular = 3 (rare).

12. For rate = k[A]², what are the units of k?

✅ mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹ — mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ ÷ (mol dm⁻³)². First order → s⁻¹, zero order → mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹.
❌ Second order overall → mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹. (s⁻¹ is first order, mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ is zero order.)

13. A plot of ln[A] against time is a straight line. What does this tell you?

✅ First order. ln[A] vs t linear → 1st order (slope −k); 1/[A] vs t linear → 2nd order (slope +k); [A] vs t linear → zero order.
❌ First order. ln[A] vs t gives a straight line ONLY for first order. (Zero order: [A] vs t is linear. Second order: 1/[A] vs t is linear.)

14. 2NO₂ + CO → products runs via: slow step NO₂ + NO₂ → NO₃ + NO, then fast step NO₃ + CO → NO₂ + CO₂. What is the rate equation?

✅ rate = k[NO₂]² — the slow (RDS) step has two NO₂. CO only enters the FAST step, so it's zero order. NO₃ is the intermediate.
❌ rate = k[NO₂]². The RDS is the slow step (NO₂ + NO₂), so only [NO₂]² appears. CO reacts after the RDS → zero order in CO.

🎯 EXAM WALKTHROUGHS

Q1 — DEFINE [2 marks] "Define the term activation energy."

Model answer: "The minimum energy (1) required for colliding particles to undergo a reaction (1)."

Trap: don't drift into ΔH or temperature — that earns nothing. Two separate ideas = two marks.

Q2 — EXPLAIN [3 marks] "Explain why an increase in temperature increases the rate of a reaction, using collision theory."

Model answer: particles have a greater average kinetic energy (1) → a larger fraction now have energy ≥ Eₐ (the Maxwell–Boltzmann curve shifts so more particles sit beyond Eₐ) (1) → so the frequency of successful collisions increases (1).

Q3 — DEDUCE / IDENTIFY / STATE [3 marks] "NO₂ + CO → NO + CO₂ runs by: step 1 (slow) NO₂ + NO₂ → NO₃ + NO; step 2 (fast) NO₃ + CO → NO₂ + CO₂. (a) Deduce the rate equation. (b) Identify the intermediate. (c) State the molecularity of step 1."

Model answer: (a) rate = k[NO₂]² — only the reactants of the RDS appear (1) · (b) NO₃ — formed then consumed (1) · (c) bimolecular — two NO₂ particles (1).

CO is zero order (enters only the fast step) — saying rate = k[NO₂][CO] is the classic losing answer.

Q4 — CALCULATE [3 marks] "k = 4.5 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹ at 298 K and 1.6 × 10⁻² s⁻¹ at 318 K. Calculate the activation energy."

Model answer: ln(k₁/k₂) = (Eₐ/R)(1/T₂ − 1/T₁) → ln(4.5 × 10⁻³ / 1.6 × 10⁻²) = (Eₐ/8.31)(1/318 − 1/298) → −1.268 = (Eₐ/8.31)(−0.000211) → Eₐ = 1.268 × 8.31 / 0.000211 = 50 kJ mol⁻¹ (1 for formula, 1 for correct substitution, 1 for answer).

  • ✓R2.2.10AHL: order = exponent in the rate equation; order can describe the number of particles in the RDS; overall order = n + m; initial-rates method; linearisation (ln[A] vs t → 1st order, 1/[A] vs t → 2nd order).
  • ✓R2.2.11AHL: rate constant k is temperature dependent; units determined from overall order (0th: mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹, 1st: s⁻¹, 2nd: mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹, 3rd: mol⁻² dm⁶ s⁻¹).
  • ✓R2.2.12AHL: Arrhenius equation k = A·e^(−Eₐ/RT) uses the temperature dependence of k to determine Eₐ; ln k vs 1/T plot → gradient −Eₐ/R, intercept ln A; Eₐ in J mol⁻¹, T in kelvin.
  • ✓R2.2.13AHL: Arrhenius factor A accounts for the frequency of collisions with proper orientations (units = units of k); two-point form ln(k₁/k₂) = (Eₐ/R)(1/T₂ − 1/T₁); worked example: N₂O₅ → Eₐ = 104 kJ mol⁻¹, A = 2.85 × 10¹³ s⁻¹.
