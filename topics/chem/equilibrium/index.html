EQUILIBRIUM — THE CROWD BALANCER 🎚️

A reaction is a nightclub with a capacity limit. People flow from the bar (reactants) to the dance floor (products) and back — and at equilibrium, the same number walk each way every second. The club still looks identical, but the turnover never stops. This page is the bouncer's handbook: how the flow balances, what the scoreboard (K) tells you, how to predict every crowd shift, and how to count heads with an ICE table.

Act 1 · R2.3.1

THE TURNSTILE 🚪

What equilibrium actually IS: a crowd that never changes in size but never stops moving.

DYNAMIC EQUILIBRIUM (R2.3.1)

In a closed system, a reversible change reaches dynamic equilibrium: the rates of the forward and reverse reactions are equal.

  • Evaporation + condensation in one equation: Br₂(l) ⇌ Br₂(g). In a sealed flask, the bromine colour first darkens, then stays constant.
  • "Closed system" matters — if gas escapes, the reverse reaction can't keep up, so no equilibrium.
  • Equal rates, NOT equal amounts. The mixture can be 90% products and still be at equilibrium.
MEMORY HOOK: equilibrium is a turnstile — the same number of people push through each side per second. The crowd looks frozen; the individual people are not.
Figure 1 — The two curves meet the same plateau. Whether you start with N₂ + 3H₂ (a) or pure NH₃, the same equilibrium concentrations appear (N₂ 0.50, H₂ 1.50, NH₃ 1.00 mol dm⁻³ at 475 K) — equilibrium can be approached from either direction.

PROOF IT'S DYNAMIC (THE ISOTOPE TRICK)

Reach equilibrium with ordinary bromine, then add some radioactive 80Br₂(l). The concentrations don't change — but the gas phase almost immediately contains radioactive bromine. Evaporation and condensation are still running; they're just perfectly matched.

Same story for solids in contact with solution: excess NaCl(s) ⇌ Na⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) — a saturated solution is a dynamic equilibrium, not a frozen one.

AT EQUILIBRIUM, REMEMBER (R2.3.1 SUMMARY)

  • Chemical and/or physical changes continue at the microscopic level.
  • Forward and reverse reactions proceed at equal rates.
  • Concentrations of reactants and products remain constant (macroscopic properties — colour, density — unchanged).
  • The same equilibrium state is reached from either direction.
NO: "reactions stop at equilibrium" ✗. The changes stop only macroscopically, never microscopically.
Act 2 · R2.3.2 · R2.3.3

THE SCOREBOARD 📊

The equilibrium law: one number, K, that captures the whole crowd ratio — and what its size tells you.

THE EQUILIBRIUM LAW (R2.3.2)

For aA + bB + ... ⇌ xX + yY + ..., the ratio of equilibrium concentrations raised to their stoichiometric coefficients is a constant at constant temperature:

K = [X]x[Y]y… / [A]a[B]b…
products (forward) on top · reactants (forward) on the bottom · coefficients become exponents

N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃: K = [NH₃]² / ([N₂][H₂]³) — the classic, memorise it.

  • K values are treated as unitless (concentrations still quoted in mol dm⁻³).
  • Solids and pure liquids are left out — their concentrations don't change. For reactions in aqueous solution, the solvent (water) is also excluded: ester hydrolysis K = [HCOOH][CH₃OH] / [HCOOCH₃].
  • But in a non-aqueous reaction where water is a genuine reactant, water IS included.
WORKED EXAMPLE 1 (book p.516): Calculate K for the synthesis of ammonia at 475 K using the equilibrium data of Figure 1: [NH₃] = 1.00, [N₂] = 0.50, [H₂] = 1.50 mol dm⁻³.
K = (1.00)² / (0.50 × 1.50³) = 1 / 1.6875 = 0.59 (unitless).
Bump the starting N₂ from 1.0 to 2.5 mol dm⁻³ and you get different equilibrium concentrations (1.85, 1.13, 1.25) — but the same K: 1.25²/(1.85 × 1.13³) = 0.59. K depends on temperature ONLY, not on how you got there.

WHAT K'S SIZE MEANS (R2.3.3)

MEMORY HOOK: big K → big products. The scoreboard never lies — if K is 10⁵, the home team (products) won.
Figure 2 — K magnitude = the scoreboard. Left of centre: reactants dominate. Right: products dominate. K itself never changes with concentration or pressure — only with temperature.

K SURGERY: WHAT CHANGING THE EQUATION DOES (R2.3.3)

Change to the equationNew K
Coefficients halved (½ of everything)K′ = √K
Coefficients doubledK′ = K²
Equation reversedK′ = 1/K
Two equations added togetherK′ = K₁ × K₂
WORKED EXAMPLE 2 (book p.517): At 475 K, N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ has K = 0.59.
(a) For 2NH₃ ⇌ N₂ + 3H₂ (reversed): K = 1/0.59 = 1.7.
(b) For NH₃ ⇌ ½N₂ + 1.5H₂ (halved): K = √1.7 = 1.3.
WATCH OUT: K has no units — but you MUST still use concentrations in mol dm⁻³ when calculating it, and keep the exponents exactly as the stoichiometric coefficients (fractions allowed here!).
Act 3 · R2.3.4

THE BOUNCER 🥋

Le Chatelier's principle: when the club is disturbed, the bouncer adjusts the flow to counteract the disturbance.

LE CHATELIER'S PRINCIPLE (R2.3.4)

If a dynamic equilibrium is disturbed by a change in conditions, the balance between forward and reverse processes shifts to counteract the change and return the system to equilibrium.

  • Shift to the right → forward reaction favoured → more products.
  • Shift to the left → reverse reaction favoured → more reactants.
MEMORY HOOK: the bouncer restores order. Squeeze the club (add pressure) and he lets the crowd shrink (fewer gas molecules). Heat it up and he burns off the excess heat. Whatever you add, he pushes the reaction away from it.
🧪

EFFECT OF CONCENTRATION

Case study: chromate ⇌ dichromate (school-lab classic)

2CrO₄²⁻(aq) + 2H⁺(aq) ⇌ Cr₂O₇²⁻(aq) + H₂O(l) — yellow ⇌ orange.

  • Add acid (↑ [H⁺]) → system uses up H⁺ → shifts right → solution turns orange.
  • Add alkali (↓ [H⁺]) → reverse reaction regenerates H⁺ → shifts left → back to yellow.
  • H₂O is the solvent AND a product — but it's in huge excess, so it's left out of K and barely affects the position. Diluting with water only makes the colour paler, not yellow.

EFFECT OF PRESSURE (GASES ONLY)

Count the gas molecules on each side (ignore solids, liquids and aqueous species).

Pressure changeShiftWhy
↑ pressuretowards the side with fewer gas moleculesfewer molecules = smaller volume = pressure relieved
↓ pressuretowards the side with more gas moleculesmore molecules = larger volume = pressure restored

N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃: 4 gas molecules left, 2 right → ↑ pressure shifts right. Br₂(l) ⇌ Br₂(g): 0 gas left, 1 right → ↑ pressure shifts left (condensation). Equal gas on both sides? Pressure does nothing to the position.

Volume has the OPPOSITE effect of pressure (PV = nRT): ↓ volume = ↑ pressure. K never changes either way.

EFFECT OF TEMPERATURE — THE ONLY ONE THAT MOVES K

Treat heat as a chemical: exothermic → heat is a product (reactants ⇌ products + Q); endothermic → heat is a reactant (reactants + Q ⇌ products).

ΔHr of forward↑ temperature↓ temperature
Exothermic (ΔH < 0)shifts left, K decreasesshifts right, K increases
Endothermic (ΔH > 0)shifts right, K increasesshifts left, K decreases

Haber: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, ΔHr = −91.8 kJ mol⁻¹. Heat the mixture → K drops, less NH₃. That's why industry uses a compromise temperature.

ΔHr = 0 (e.g. H₂O + D₂O ⇌ 2DOH isotope exchange)? Temperature has no effect on position or K.

EFFECT OF CATALYSTS — SPOILER: NOTHING

A catalyst lowers the activation energy for BOTH directions equally (same pathway, reversed). So it speeds up reaching equilibrium but never moves the position or changes K.

Figure 3 — The communicating-vessels model. A catalyst widens the connecting pipe: equilibrium arrives sooner, but the final water levels (the position) are identical.

CASE STUDY: THE HABER PROCESS (book p.524)

N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ + Q — where Le Chatelier meets economics
  • High pressure (200 atm / 20 MPa): 4 → 2 gas molecules, so high pressure pushes right AND speeds the reaction.
  • Compromise temperature (400–450 °C): low T would favour the exothermic forward reaction, but kills the rate. Moderate T + iron catalyst wins overall.
  • Le Chatelier by removal: NH₃ is condensed out each cycle, and N₂/H₂ are recycled — removing product keeps pulling the equilibrium right. Modern plants reach ~98% yield.
Act 4 · R2.3.5 · AHL

THE COMPASS 🧭

Q — the same formula as K, but with the crowd RIGHT NOW instead of at equilibrium. It tells you which way the reaction is heading.

THE REACTION QUOTIENT, Q (R2.3.5 AHL)

Q is calculated exactly like K, but with actual, non-equilibrium concentrations. Compare Q to K and you know the direction of change:

  • Q < K → more reactants than equilibrium needs → forward reaction favoured (products build up until Q = K).
  • Q > K → more products than equilibrium allows → reverse reaction favoured (products break down until Q = K).
  • Q = K → already at equilibrium → no net change.
MEMORY HOOK: Q and K are two numbers fighting to be equal. If Q is behind (smaller), the reaction runs forward to catch up. If Q overshot (bigger), it reverses to come back.
Figure 4 — The Q vs K compass. K is the fixed target (constant at constant T); Q slides around it as the reaction proceeds, always heading back toward K.
WORKED EXAMPLE 3 (book p.526): For N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ at 475 K, K = 0.59. A mixture has all three species at 0.50 mol dm⁻³. Find Q and predict the direction.
Q = (0.50)² / (0.50 × 0.50³) = 0.25 / 0.0625 = 4.0.
Since Q > K (4.0 > 0.59): too much ammonia, too little N₂/H₂ → the reverse reaction is favoured (NH₃ decomposes).

GLOBAL IMPACT: SARUHASHI'S TABLE

CO₂ in seawater sits in a web of equilibria (dissolving, carbonic acid, bicarbonate…). Japanese geochemist Katsuko Saruhashi used equilibrium constants to build "Saruhashi's Table" — a method for determining CO₂ levels in seawater from pH and temperature. Her work underpins how we track ocean acidification today.

Takeaway for essays: equilibrium chemistry is how we actually measure things you can't see — from ocean CO₂ to blood pH.

THE LEDGER 🧮

Counting heads: ICE tables for equilibrium composition, and the bridge between K and Gibbs energy.

QUANTIFYING COMPOSITION WITH K (R2.3.6 AHL)

Known K + known starting amounts → equilibrium concentrations, via the ICE table (Initial / Change / Equilibrium). The change row follows the stoichiometric ratios exactly.

WORKED EXAMPLE 4 (book p.526–527): 2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2SO₃(g), K = 3.0 at 1000 K. At equilibrium [SO₂] = 0.12 and [SO₃] = 0.18 mol dm⁻³.
(a) K = [SO₃]² / ([O₂][SO₂]²) → 3.0 = 0.18² / ([O₂] × 0.12²) → [O₂] = 0.75 mol dm⁻³.
(b) Backwards from the change row: each mole of SO₃ made uses 1 mole SO₂ and ½ mole O₂. So 0.18 mol dm⁻³ SO₃ consumed 0.18 SO₂ and 0.09 O₂: initial [SO₂] = 0.12 + 0.18 = 0.30; initial [O₂] = 0.75 + 0.09 = 0.84 mol dm⁻³.
[SO₂][O₂][SO₃]
initial0.300.840
change−0.18−0.09+0.18
equilibrium0.120.750.18
Check: 0.18²/(0.75 × 0.12²) = 3.0 ✓ — the ledger balances.

THE WEAK-ACID SHORTCUT (R2.3.6 AHL)

For weak acids/bases, K is tiny, so only a tiny fraction ionises. That makes [HA]ₑq ≈ [HA]ᵢₙᵢₜᵢₐₗ — no quadratic needed (quadratics are NOT assessed in DP exams).

WORKED EXAMPLE 5 (book p.529): CH₃COOH(aq) ⇌ CH₃COO⁻(aq) + H⁺(aq), K = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵ at 298 K. Equilibrium concentrations in 0.100 mol dm⁻³ ethanoic acid?
K = [CH₃COO⁻][H⁺]/[CH₃COOH] ≈ x²/0.100 (assuming x tiny)
x² = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.100 → x = √(1.74 × 10⁻⁶) = 1.32 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ = [CH₃COO⁻]ₑq = [H⁺]ₑq.
Exact quadratic: 1.31 × 10⁻³ — the shortcut is fine (2% difference). Practice version: phenylamine, Kb = 7.41 × 10⁻¹⁰ → [OH⁻] = 8.6 × 10⁻⁶ mol dm⁻³.

K AND GIBBS ENERGY (R2.3.7 AHL)

Two thermometers, same patient: K measures the position of equilibrium; ΔG° measures spontaneity under standard conditions. The bridge:

ΔG° = −RT ln K
R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ · T in kelvin · ΔG° in J (convert from kJ!) · K = e^(−ΔG°/RT)
KΔG°Under standard conditions
K > 1ΔG° < 0forward reaction spontaneous
K < 1ΔG° > 0reverse reaction spontaneous
K = 1ΔG° = 0equilibrium — neither direction favoured
MEMORY HOOK: negative ΔG° and K > 1 always travel together — one means "products win", so does the other.
WORKED EXAMPLE 6 (book p.531–532): 2NO(g) ⇌ N₂O₂(g), K = 1.39 × 10⁻⁵ at 298 K (from spectroscopic data). ΔGf(NO) = 87.6 kJ mol⁻¹.
(a) ΔG° = −RT ln K = −8.31 × 298 × ln(1.39 × 10⁻⁵) = −2476 × (−11.18) = +27 700 J mol⁻¹ = +27.7 kJ mol⁻¹ — positive, so the reverse reaction (N₂O₂ decomposing to NO) is spontaneous. K < 1 agrees: reactants (NO) are favoured.
(b) Hess-style cycle for ΔGf(N₂O₂): ΔG° = −[2 × ΔGf(NO)] + ΔGf(N₂O₂) → 27.7 = −(2 × 87.6) + ΔGf(N₂O₂) → ΔGf(N₂O₂) = +202.9 kJ mol⁻¹ — hugely positive, confirming N₂O₂ is unstable.

PRACTICE (book Q13): CH₃COOH + CH₃CH₂OH ⇌ CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ + H₂O. Mix 1.00 mol ethanoic acid + 2.00 mol ethanol in 1.00 dm³ at 50 °C; at equilibrium 1.40 mol ethanol remains.
Reacted: 2.00 − 1.40 = 0.60 mol ethanol → 0.60 ester, 0.60 water, 0.40 acid at equilibrium (all in 1 dm³, so numbers = concentrations).
K = [ester][water] / ([acid][ethanol]) = (0.60 × 0.60) / (0.40 × 1.40) = 0.64.
Then: equal concentrations of all four species → Q = (1×1)/(1×1) = 1 > 0.64 → reverse favoured → ester hydrolysis is the spontaneous direction.

✍ THE CROWD QUIZ

14 questions. Click an answer — instant feedback, no wrong-track penalties.

1. Which statement about a system at dynamic equilibrium is correct?

✅ Equal RATES, not equal amounts. The turnstile spins both ways at the same speed.
❌ Remember: equal forward and reverse RATES — concentrations are constant but rarely equal.

2. What is the K expression for N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g)?

✅ Products over reactants, coefficients as exponents: [NH₃]²/([N₂][H₂]³).
❌ Coefficients become exponents — 3H₂ means [H₂]³, and products go on top.

3. Which change does NOT affect the value of K?

✅ K responds to temperature (and to how you write the equation) but NOT to pressure or concentration.
❌ Temperature always changes K; pressure and concentration only shift the position, never K.

4. If K = 1.7 for 2NH₃ ⇌ N₂ + 3H₂, what is K for N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃?

✅ Reversing the equation reciprocates K: 1/1.7 = 0.59.
❌ Reversed equation → K′ = 1/K → 1/1.7 = 0.59.

5. At equilibrium, Q is…

✅ Equilibrium is defined by Q = K. That's the whole compass.
❌ At equilibrium Q = K exactly — that's what "at equilibrium" means.

6. 2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) ⇌ 2SO₃(g). Increasing pressure shifts the equilibrium…

✅ ↑ pressure → side with FEWER gas molecules. 3 gas on the left, 2 on the right → right.
❌ Higher pressure favours the side with fewer gas molecules — that's the side with 2 (SO₃).

7. For an exothermic reaction (ΔH < 0), increasing the temperature…

✅ Heat is a product of an exo reaction — add heat, the system consumes it by running the reverse: left, and K drops.
❌ Exothermic = heat is a product. Adding heat pushes the reaction left and lowers K.

8. A catalyst added to a system at equilibrium will…

✅ Catalyst lowers Eₐ for both directions equally — position and K untouched, arrival time shortened.
❌ A catalyst accelerates BOTH directions — the equilibrium position and K stay exactly the same.

9. (AHL) For N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, K = 0.59. A mixture has [NH₃] = 1.0, [N₂] = 0.5, [H₂] = 0.5 mol dm⁻³. Which way does it go?

✅ Q = 1.0²/(0.5 × 0.5³) = 4.0 > 0.59 → too much product → NH₃ decomposes (reverse).
❌ Compute Q = (1.0)²/(0.5)(0.5)³ = 4.0. Since Q > K, the reverse reaction is favoured.

10. (AHL) 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃, K = 3.0. At equilibrium [SO₂] = 0.12, [SO₃] = 0.18 mol dm⁻³. What is [O₂]?

✅ 3.0 = 0.18²/([O₂] × 0.12²) → [O₂] = 0.75 mol dm⁻³.
❌ Rearrange: [O₂] = 0.18²/(3.0 × 0.12²) = 0.75 mol dm⁻³.

11. (AHL) For an acid–base equilibrium with K = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵, ΔG° at 298 K is…

✅ K < 1 ⇔ ΔG° > 0: ΔG° = −RT ln K = −8.31 × 298 × ln(1.74×10⁻⁵) = +27.2 kJ mol⁻¹.
❌ Tiny K means positive ΔG° — reactants favoured. ΔG° = −RT ln K = +27.2 kJ mol⁻¹.

12. (AHL) ΔG° = −RT ln K. If ΔG° = −3.9 kJ mol⁻¹ at 298 K, K is…

✅ ln K = 3900/(8.31 × 298) = 1.575 → K = e^1.575 ≈ 4.8 (K > 1 for negative ΔG° ✓).
❌ Convert to J first: ln K = 3900/(8.31×298) = 1.575, so K = e^1.575 ≈ 4.8.

13. (AHL) A 0.100 mol dm⁻³ solution of a weak acid has Ka = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵. [H⁺] ≈ ?

✅ x² = Ka × [HA]₀ = 1.74×10⁻⁵ × 0.100 → x = 1.32 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³.
❌ [H⁺] = √(Ka × [HA]₀) = √(1.74×10⁻⁶) = 1.32 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³.

14. (AHL) Which mixture will shift FORWARD at constant temperature?

✅ Q < K → not enough products yet → the forward reaction runs to build them up.
❌ Forward is favoured when Q < K (too few products). Q > K drives the reverse.
