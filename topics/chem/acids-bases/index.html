ACIDS & BASES — THE PROTON HANDOVER

The club has a door policy: every guest carries a proton. An acid hands it over. A base takes it. That single exchange — who gives, who takes, how much, and how hard the pH needle swings — is the whole topic. R3.1 is the study of the handover.

THE PROTON HANDOVER 🤝

The Brønsted–Lowry definition: acids donate H⁺, bases accept H⁺. One proton at a time, always in pairs.

🤝

THE HANDOVER RULE (R3.1.1)

Brønsted (Denmark) + Lowry (England), 1923 — independently, same conclusion

  • Acid = proton donor: a species that can lose an H⁺ ion.
  • Base = proton acceptor: a species that can gain an H⁺ ion.
  • Works without water: NH₃(g) + HCl(g) → NH₄Cl(s) — white smoke. HCl loses a proton, NH₃ accepts it. Arrhenius needed water; B–L doesn't.
  • Arrhenius (1884): acid → H⁺ in water, base → OH⁻ in water. Limitation: NH₃ has no oxygen and can't dissociate to OH⁻ — it makes OH⁻ by reacting with water: NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻. B–L expands, doesn't replace, Arrhenius.
🔁

CONJUGATE PAIRS — ALWAYS TWO-BY-TWO (R3.1.2)

a pair differs by EXACTLY one proton

  • HCN ⇌ H⁺ + CN⁻ → HCN/CN⁻ is a conjugate pair. With water: HCN + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + CN⁻ → two pairs: HCN/CN⁻ and H₃O⁺/H₂O.
  • H₂SO₄ and SO₄²⁻ are NOT a pair — they differ by two protons. The stepwise equations H₂SO₄ ⇌ H⁺ + HSO₄⁻ and HSO₄⁻ ⇌ H⁺ + SO₄²⁻ each contain a valid pair.
  • HSO₄⁻ is an acid in one process and a base in another — that's the fun of amphiprotic species.
🎭

AMPHIPROTIC — BOTH ROLES, ONE SPECIES (R3.1.3)

amphiprotic ≠ amphoteric

  • Amphiprotic: can donate OR accept a proton — H₂O, HCO₃⁻, HSO₄⁻, H₂PO₄⁻.
  • Amphoteric: reacts with both acids and bases — but may not have a proton to donate. ZnO + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂O AND ZnO + 2NaOH + H₂O → Na₂[Zn(OH)₄]. ZnO is amphoteric but NOT amphiprotic (no proton to donate).
  • Every amphiprotic species is amphoteric — but not vice versa. That's a classic MCQ trap.
  • Bonus: amino acids (H₂N–CH(R)–COOH) exist as zwitterions — both + and − charge in one species — the building blocks of peptides.
Figure 1 — The proton handover. Every acid–base reaction produces a new acid and a new base: the conjugate pair.
MEMORY HOOK: "A-pair" = Acid + its minus-one-proton shadow. If they differ by 2 protons (H₂CO₃ vs CO₃²⁻), it's NOT a pair — it's two separate handovers.

THE PH SCALE 📏

pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]. Every step of 1 = a ×10 swing in [H⁺]. And Kw is the invisible ruler underneath everything.

R3.1.4 · SL

THE PH RULER

pH = −log₁₀[H⁺] · [H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ

0.100 mol dm⁻³ HCl → [H⁺] = 0.100 → pH = 1.00.

pH 3 → [H⁺] = 10⁻³ = 0.001 mol dm⁻³.

At 298 K: 7 = neutral ([H⁺] = [OH⁻]) · below 7 acidic · above 7 basic.

R3.1.5 · SL

KW — THE INVISIBLE RULER

H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻ · Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 298 K.

Pure water: [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = √(10⁻¹⁴) = 10⁻⁷.

Add acid → equilibrium shifts left → [OH⁻] drops. Kw never changes (at fixed T).

Only valid for dilute aqueous solutions — battery acid (30% H₂SO₄) breaks it.

R3.1.9 · AHL

POH — THE MIRROR SCALE

pOH = −log₁₀[OH⁻] · [OH⁻] = 10⁻ᵖᴼᴴ

Since Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻], logs give: pH + pOH = 14 (298 K).

0.025 mol dm⁻³ KOH → [OH⁻] = 0.025 → pOH = 1.60 → pH = 12.40.

Logs turn ×/÷ into +/− — less error-prone than writing 10⁻¹⁴.

WORKED EXAMPLE 2 (book p.545): Calculate [OH⁻] in 0.100 mol dm⁻³ HCl at 25 °C.
[H⁺] = 0.100 (HCl fully dissociates) → Kw = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ = 0.100 × [OH⁻] → [OH⁻] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹³ mol dm⁻³. Check: 0.100 > 10⁻¹³ so [H⁺] > [OH⁻] ✓ (acidic).
Figure 2 — The pH scale (Table 1 values). Neutral = 7 only at 298 K — at 10 °C pure water has pH 7.27 but is still neutral ([H⁺] = [OH⁻]).
EXAM TRAP: "Neutral" ≠ "pH 7" away from 298 K. Kw increases with temperature, so pure water at 10 °C has pH 7.27 — but [H⁺] = [OH⁻], so it's still neutral. Always check temperature.

STRONG vs WEAK 💪

Strong ≠ concentrated. It's all about extent of ionization: does it dissociate 100% (single arrow) or barely (equilibrium sign)?

💥

THE EXTENT-OF-IONIZATION RULE (R3.1.6)

strong = full dissociation · weak = tiny equilibrium

  • Strong acid: single arrow → HCl(aq) → H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq). Zero HCl molecules left.
  • Weak acid: equilibrium sign → CH₃COOH(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + CH₃COO⁻(aq). Vinegar contains BOTH molecules and ions.
  • The 7 strong acids (memorize): HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, H₂SO₄, HClO₄, HClO₃. Everything else: assume weak.
  • Strong bases: group 1 + most group 2 hydroxides (NaOH, KOH, Ba(OH)₂). Mg(OH)₂/Ca(OH)₂ are insoluble → heterogeneous equilibria — weak-looking but for solubility reasons, not strength.
  • Weak/strong ≠ dilute/concentrated: 10 mol dm⁻³ CH₃COOH is weak and concentrated. Never say "strong solution" in an IB answer.
R3.1.7 · SL

NEUTRALIZATION MENU 🍽

Metal + acid → salt + H₂: Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂. Net ionic: Mg + 2H⁺ → Mg²⁺ + H₂. Cu and Ag don't react (below H).

Metal oxide + acid → salt + water: MgO + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂O.

Carbonate + acid → salt + CO₂ + H₂O: Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + CO₂ + H₂O. Net: CO₃²⁻ + 2H⁺ → CO₂ + H₂O.

Hydrogencarbonate: HCO₃⁻ + H⁺ → CO₂ + H₂O.

R3.1.10 · AHL

KA — THE WEAK-ACID SCORE (AHL)

HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻ · Ka = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA] · pKa = −log Ka

Larger Ka = stronger. Larger pKa = weaker.

Methanoic pKa 3.75 < ethanoic 4.76 → methanoic is stronger.

HCN Ka 6.17 × 10⁻¹⁰ — barely dissociates at all.

Same for bases: Kb, pKb. Methylamine pKb 3.34 < NH₃ 4.75 → methylamine stronger.

WORKED EXAMPLES 5–6 (AHL, book p.551–552): 0.0100 mol dm⁻³ propanoic acid has pH 3.44. (a) [H⁺] = 10⁻³·⁴⁴ = 3.63 × 10⁻⁴ → Ka = (3.63×10⁻⁴)² / 0.0100 = 1.32 × 10⁻⁵ → pKa = 4.88 (actual 4.87 ✓ approximations valid). (b) 0.100 mol dm⁻³: [H⁺] = √(Ka × c) = √(1.32×10⁻⁵ × 0.100) = 1.15 × 10⁻³ → pH = 2.94.
WORKED EXAMPLES 7–8: 0.0100 mol dm⁻³ trimethylamine pH 10.90 → pOH = 3.10 → [OH⁻] = 7.94×10⁻⁴ → Kb = 6.31 × 10⁻⁵, pKb = 4.20. At 0.100 M: [OH⁻] = 2.51 × 10⁻³ → pOH 2.60 → pH = 11.40.
R3.1.11 (AHL) — THE CONJUGATE PAYOFF: Ka × Kb = Kw for any conjugate pair. pKa + pKb = 14. The stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base — "the stronger wins." For CH₃COOH: pKa 4.76 → pKb(CH₃COO⁻) = 9.24.

THE CURVE 📈

pH curves: the shape tells you WHO is in the flask. The equivalence pH tells you WHICH indicator to pick.

📈

READING THE SHAPE (R3.1.8)

strong + strong: the classic 7.0 cliff

  • Strong acid → strong base (Fig 8): start low (pH ~1 for 0.1 M HCl), rise slowly, then the pH jump at equivalence → 7.0, flatten near pH 12–14 (excess base).
  • Strong base → strong acid: mirror image — start high, pH drop to 7.0, flatten low.
  • Equivalence point = pH 7.0 only for strong+strong (just NaCl(aq)).
  • SL stops here. The weak/weak curves are AHL (R3.1.13).
R3.1.13 · AHL

WEAK + STRONG CURVES

Weak acid + strong base: start higher (pH 3), a buffer region plateau, smaller jump, equivalence at pH > 7 (salt hydrolyses: CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COOH + OH⁻).

Strong acid + weak base: equivalence at pH < 7 (NH₄⁺ hydrolyses).

Weak + weak: no sharp jump anywhere → no indicator works.

Half-equivalence: [HA] = [A⁻] → pH = pKa.

R3.1.14 · AHL

INDICATORS = WEAK ACIDS

HInd ⇌ H⁺ + Ind⁻ — acid form and base form have different colours.

Colour flips around the indicator's own pKa (±1 pH unit).

Methyl orange: pKa 3.7 · range 3.1–4.4 · red → yellow

Bromothymol blue: pKa 7.0 · range 6.0–7.6 · yellow → blue

Phenolphthalein: pKa 9.6 · range 8.3–10.0 · colourless → pink

R3.1.15 · AHL

MATCH THE INDICATOR

The indicator's transition range must include the equivalence pH.

Strong+strong (eq 7): bromothymol blue — but any works (huge jump).

Weak acid + strong base (eq > 7): phenolphthalein. Methyl orange = big error.

Weak base + strong acid (eq < 7): methyl orange.

Weak+weak: no indicator — use a pH meter.

Figure 3 — Three pH-curve archetypes. The equivalence point lands at 7, >7, or <7 — that single number decides the indicator.
WORKED EXAMPLE 9 (AHL, book p.560): A titration produces sodium methanoate, HCOONa, at the equivalence point. Which indicator? → Split the salt: HCOO⁻ + Na⁺ → parent acid HCOOH (weak) + parent base NaOH (strong) → equivalence pH > 7 → phenolphthalein (or any indicator with pKa > 7, e.g. phenol red).

THE BUFFER SHIELD 🛡

A buffer = both halves of a conjugate pair, in one solution. It eats added acid and added base without flinching. This is why your blood stays at pH 7.4.

🛡

BUFFER ACTION (R3.1.16)

ethanoate buffer: CH₃COOH + CH₃COONa

  • Contains BOTH conjugates: CH₃COOH (weak acid) + CH₃COO⁻ (weak base, from the salt).
  • Added HCl: H⁺ + CH₃COO⁻ → CH₃COOH. Strong acid replaced by a weak acid → pH barely moves.
  • Added NaOH: OH⁻ + CH₃COOH → CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O. Strong base replaced by a weak base.
  • "A strong acid is replaced with a weak acid, and a strong base with a weak base." That's the whole trick.
  • Blood: 7.35–7.45, held by hydrogencarbonate/hydrogenphosphate/CO₂/proteins.
R3.1.17 · AHL

HENDERSON–HASSELBALCH

pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA])

Diluting by any factor lowers both [A⁻] and [HA] equally → ratio unchanged → pH unchanged.

But capacity is finite: when one conjugate is used up, the buffer dies.

R3.1.12 · AHL

SALT pH — HYDROLYSIS

"Hydrolysis is for the weak." Split the salt, find the parents:

Strong+strong → neutral (KNO₃, NaCl)

Weak acid + strong base → basic (CH₃COONa — anion hydrolyses)

Strong acid + weak base → acidic (NH₄Cl — cation hydrolyses)

Weak+weak → the stronger wins (NH₄CN: CN⁻ is a stronger base than NH₄⁺ is an acid → slightly basic)

WORKED EXAMPLE 10 (AHL, book p.563): pH of a buffer with 0.100 mol dm⁻³ CH₃COOH and 0.200 mol dm⁻³ CH₃COONa. pKa = 4.76 → pH = 4.76 + log(0.200/0.100) = 4.76 + 0.30 = 5.06.
WORKED EXAMPLE 11: A pH 11.00 buffer from CH₃NH₂ + HCl. (a) Conjugate acid CH₃NH₃⁺, base CH₃NH₂. (b) Methylamine was in excess (if HCl were in excess, all the weak base would be consumed — no buffer). (c) pKa(CH₃NH₃⁺) = 14 − 3.34 = 10.66; 11.00 = 10.66 + log([CH₃NH₂]/[CH₃NH₃⁺]) → log ratio = 0.34 → ratio = 10⁰·³⁴ = 2.19 → [CH₃NH₃⁺] : [CH₃NH₂] = 1 : 2.19.
REAL WORLD: One drop of 0.1 M HCl into 100 cm³ pure water → pH crashes 7.0 → 4.3. Same drop into a phosphate buffer → pH shifts < 0.001 (below most pH meters' resolution). That's the difference between a body and a beaker.

Prove it

SELF-TEST ✍

No peeking at the acts above. Pick an answer, get instant feedback.

1. In Brønsted–Lowry theory, an acid is best described as a...

✅ B–L acid = proton donor. Electron pair acceptor is Lewis; OH⁻ producer is Arrhenius.
❌ Proton donor. (Electron pair acceptor = Lewis acid; hydroxide producer = Arrhenius.)

2. Which is NOT a conjugate acid–base pair?

✅ H₂SO₄ and SO₄²⁻ differ by TWO protons — that's two separate pairs (H₂SO₄/HSO₄⁻ and HSO₄⁻/SO₄²⁻).
❌ H₂SO₄/SO₄²⁻ — a pair must differ by exactly one proton.

3. ZnO reacts with both HCl and NaOH. It is...

✅ ZnO reacts with both acids and bases (amphoteric) but has no proton to donate — so it's not amphiprotic. Amphiprotic ⊂ amphoteric.
❌ Amphoteric but not amphiprotic. Amphiprotic species must be able to DONATE a proton; ZnO can't.

4. A solution has pH 2. Its [H⁺] is...

✅ [H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ = 10⁻² = 0.01 mol dm⁻³.
❌ [H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ. pH 2 → 10⁻² mol dm⁻³.

5. [OH⁻] in 0.100 mol dm⁻³ HCl at 25 °C is...

✅ [H⁺] = 0.100 → [OH⁻] = Kw/[H⁺] = 1.00×10⁻¹⁴/0.100 = 1.00 × 10⁻¹³.
❌ Kw/[H⁺] = 10⁻¹⁴/0.100 = 10⁻¹³. The acid suppresses [OH⁻] way below 10⁻⁷.

6. Which is a WEAK acid?

✅ Carbonic acid is weak (⇌). The 7 strong acids: HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, H₂SO₄, HClO₄, HClO₃ — none is H₂CO₃.
❌ H₂CO₃ — carbonic acid. HNO₃ and H₂SO₄ are on the strong-acid list.

7. 10 mol dm⁻³ CH₃COOH is best described as...

✅ Strong/weak = extent of ionization; concentrated/dilute = amount. Ethanoic acid is weak at ANY concentration. "Strong solution" gets no marks in IB.
❌ Weak + concentrated. "Strong" is about dissociation, not concentration.

8. Calcium carbonate + hydrochloric acid produces...

✅ CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + CO₂ + H₂O. The carbonic acid intermediate is unstable and decomposes.
❌ Carbonate + acid → salt + CO₂ + H₂O. That's the baking-soda fizz.

9. (AHL) Which is the WEAKEST acid? Ka values: HF 6.76×10⁻⁴, HCOOH 1.78×10⁻⁴, CH₃COOH 1.74×10⁻⁵, HCN 6.17×10⁻¹⁰.

✅ Smallest Ka = weakest. HCN (6.17×10⁻¹⁰) is 10,000× weaker than ethanoic acid.
❌ HCN — smallest Ka = weakest acid. Larger pKa also = weaker.

10. (AHL) A solution of NH₄Cl is...

✅ NH₄⁺ hydrolyses: NH₄⁺ + H₂O ⇌ NH₃ + H₃O⁺ → pH < 7. Strong acid (HCl) + weak base (NH₃) → acidic salt.
❌ Acidic — the cation of a weak base hydrolyses to make H₃O⁺. "Hydrolysis is for the weak."

11. (AHL) CH₃COOH titrated with NaOH (equivalence pH ~9). Best indicator?

✅ Weak acid + strong base → equivalence pH > 7 → phenolphthalein (8.3–10.0). Methyl orange would give a huge systematic error.
❌ Phenolphthalein. The indicator's range must cover the equivalence pH, which is > 7 here.

12. (AHL) Diluting a buffer 10× changes its pH by...

✅ Both [A⁻] and [HA] drop equally → ratio (and log ratio) unchanged → pH unchanged. (Unless you dilute to absurdity — everything approaches pH 7.)
❌ Dilution scales both conjugates together — the ratio in Henderson–Hasselbalch survives. pH holds.

13. (AHL) A buffer contains CH₃COOH + CH₃COONa. Adding a little NaOH is handled by...

✅ The weak ACID (CH₃COOH) absorbs the strong base: OH⁻ + CH₃COOH → CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O. Strong base → weak base.
❌ The weak acid neutralizes added strong base. (Added strong acid gets eaten by CH₃COO⁻.)

14. (AHL) Ka × Kb for a conjugate acid–base pair equals...

✅ Adding the two equilibria multiplies their constants: HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻ plus A⁻ + H₂O ⇌ HA + OH⁻ sum to H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻, so Ka × Kb = Kw = 10⁻¹⁴ (pKa + pKb = 14).
❌ Ka × Kb = Kw. That's why pKa + pKb = 14 at 298 K.
