Act 1 · 4.1 – 4.5 · descriptive statistics

THE DJ BOOTH

First rule of the club: one rowdy guest can wreck the whole vibe. The mean feels everything; the median doesn't care.

THREE WAYS TO DESCRIBE THE CROWD

The mean is the DJ's average taste — add every value, divide by how many. The median is the middle of the queue — sort everyone, take the centre. The mode is the most common value — the vibe everyone keeps showing up in.

One extreme guest (an outlier) drags the mean with him. The median doesn't even notice. That's why house prices are quoted as medians and exam grade boundaries use means.

THE BOUNCE HOUSE: QUARTILES

Split the sorted queue into four equal groups. Q1 = 25% in, Q2 = the median (50%), Q3 = 75%. The middle 50% of guests live between Q1 and Q3 — that stretch is the IQR = Q3 − Q1.

Outlier door policy: anyone below Q1 − 1.5 × IQR or above Q3 + 1.5 × IQR doesn't get in — they're drawn as lonely dots beyond the whiskers.

THE BOUNCE HOUSE · whiskers reach the last in-bounds guest, outliers float alone. Draw this from a 5-number summary in under 20 seconds.

THE SPREAD: VARIANCE & STANDARD DEVIATION

How far does the crowd scatter from the DJ booth? Variance = the mean of the squared distances from the mean. Standard deviation = the square root of that — and it's in the same units as the data, which is why it wins.

Population: σ² = Σ(x − x̄)² / n. Sample: s² = Σ(x − x̄)² / (n − 1). You will not compute these by hand in the exam — that's what the GDC is for. You WILL be asked which dataset is more consistent (smaller σ).

EXAM NOTE — "State the standard deviation" ≠ "state the variance". Give the number in the data's units and say which group is more consistent, not just "smaller".
Act 2 · 4.6 – 4.7 · bivariate statistics

THE DANCE FLOOR MAP

Two variables, one point per guest. The pattern on the floor is correlation — and correlation is NOT the bouncer's crush.

READING THE FLOOR

Plot each guest as a point: one variable on each axis. Points marching up-right = positive correlation; down-right = negative; a cloud with no direction = no linear correlation.

Pearson's r scores it from −1 to +1. Sign = direction. Size = strength. r = −0.9 is a tighter relationship than r = +0.4 — the minus is just the slope.

Spearman's rank ρ (AI SL special): rank both lists first, then run Pearson on the ranks. Use it when the relationship is curved rather than straight, or when your data is ordinal (1st, 2nd, 3rd...).

READ THE FLOOR · correlation describes the pattern. It says nothing about who causes who.
THE CLASSIC TRAP — ice cream sales and drowning deaths both rise in summer. r is huge. Is ice cream drowning people? No. A third variable (hot weather) drives both. Exam line: "correlation does not imply causation — a confounding variable may explain the link."

THE REGRESSION LINE: y ON x

Fit the best straight line through the cloud — least squares, written y = a + bx. Its one job: predict y from a new x. The GDC hands you a and b in one command.

The line is only valid inside the range of your data. Predict for an x beyond the data and you're guessing — that's extrapolation, and the examiner docks you for it.

EXAM NOTE — "Use the regression line to estimate y when x = 8" with data spanning x = 2 to x = 6? The answer starts with "this is extrapolation, so the estimate is unreliable". Say the magic words, then give the number.
Act 3 · 4.9 · probability

GUEST LIST ODDS

The bouncer works in probabilities: P(gets in) = the guest's name on the list divided by everyone at the door.

THE THREE LAWS OF THE DOOR

Mutually exclusive — two guests can't both get in (a card can't be a heart AND a spade). P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B).

Independent — one guest's entry doesn't change the other's odds. P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B).

Conditional — "given that" changes everything. P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B). Read it as: of the guests who got in (B), what fraction were also A?

Trigger words: "given that" = conditional. "and" = multiply (if independent). "or" = add (if mutually exclusive). The examiner hides these in plain sight.

THE GUEST LIST · Venn diagrams and tree diagrams are just the same three laws drawn twice. Tree = order matters, Venn = groups overlap.
EXAM NOTE — P(A | B) is NOT the same as P(B | A). "Probability it's raining given the ground is wet" ≠ "probability the ground is wet given it's raining". Don't swap them.
Act 4 · 4.10 – 4.11 · distributions

THE LINE OUTSIDE

Two bouncers run this club: Binomial for the VIP list (counts of successes), Normal for the crowd (a smooth bell of everyone).

BINOMIAL · B(n, p)

Counts successes in n fixed trials, each with the same probability p, independent, two outcomes only.

P(X = x) = C(n, x) pˣ (1 − p)ⁿ⁻ˣ

Mean = np · Variance = np(1 − p)

NORMAL · N(μ, σ²)

Continuous, symmetric bell around the mean μ with spread σ. Heights, test scores, measurement errors.

z = (x − μ) / σ

P(X > x) = 1 − P(X < x)

GDC MOVES

BinomPDF / BinomCDF for exact and cumulative binomial.

NormalCDF for areas, InvNorm to go backwards: probability → x.

THE BELL CURVE · memorise the 68-95-99.7 rule — it answers half the normal questions without any GDC work.
BINOMIAL CONDITIONS CHECK — fixed n · independent trials · two outcomes (success/fail) · same p every trial. Miss one and it's not binomial; the examiner loves a scenario where the "success" probability changes mid-question.
EXAM NOTE — "Find P(X > 12)" on a normal curve? Compute P(X < 12) with your GDC and subtract from 1. Inverse normal (InvNorm) is the same curve walked backwards: area → x.
