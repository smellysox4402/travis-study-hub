THE BLOOD-TYPE BAR

Three alleles, four blood types, and the difference between codominance and incomplete dominance.

🩸

ABO: the VIP system with three tickets

multiple alleles · codominance · incomplete dominance · I^A I^B i

The ABO blood group is set by one gene with three alleles in the gene pool: Iᴬ, Iᴮ and i. Iᴬ codes for the A glycoprotein on red blood cells, Iᴮ for the B glycoprotein, and i for no glycoprotein.

Because you only carry two of the three: IᴬIᴬ / Iᴬi → type A · IᴮIᴮ / Iᴮi → type B · ii → type O · and IᴬIᴮ → type AB, where both glycoproteins show. That's codominance: both alleles are expressed at once, not a blend.

People make antibodies against the glycoproteins they lack — anti-A, anti-B — which is why blood transfusions must be compatible. Type AB accepts anything (universal recipient); type O can donate to anyone (universal donor).

Figure 6 · ABO blood groups: multiple alleles → 4 phenotypes. AB is codominant (both show); pink Mirabilis is incomplete dominance (a blend).
Memory: codominance = both flags fly (AB). Incomplete dominance = two buckets make a half-bucket (pink). Don't mix them — the exam loves this distinction.
D3.2.11

THE SPERM DECIDES

Sex determination: it's not the mother's egg that picks the sex — it's which sperm gets in.

⚧

X marks the spot, Y makes the call

sex chromosomes · XX · XY · SRY · autosomes

Humans have 23 pairs of chromosomes; pair 23 is the sex chromosomes. The X is big, with ~900 genes essential in both sexes. The Y is small (~55 genes) with the SRY gene — the testis-determining factor (TDF).

Females are XX, males XY. All eggs carry one X. Sperm carry either an X or a Y — so the father's sperm decides the sex: X sperm → daughter (XX), Y sperm → son (XY). 50:50.

Proof it's the Y that matters: XXY (Klinefelter's) develops male — the Y is present. XO (Turner's) develops female — no Y. SRY → gonads become testes → testosterone → male development. No SRY → ovaries → oestradiol → female.

Figure 7 · Sex determination: XX × XY → 1:1. The Y chromosome's SRY gene flips development to male; no Y → female.
Key detail: X chromosomes carry ~900 genes essential in both sexes — everyone needs at least one X. The Y is small and mostly male-specific. That asymmetry is what makes sex-linked inheritance skip generations.

THE X-FACTOR

Sex-linked disorders like haemophilia — why they mostly hit boys — and reading pedigree charts.

🩹

Haemophilia: the X-linked trap

sex linkage · X-linked recessive · Factor VIII · carrier

Haemophilia is a sex-linked genetic disorder: the gene for Factor VIII (a blood-clotting protein) lives on the X chromosome. The disease allele is recessive.

Males (XY) have only one X — so one disease allele is enough to get the disorder. Females (XX) need both X chromosomes to carry it; one healthy X makes them symptom-free carriers. That's why haemophilia is almost always in boys (~1 in 10,000) and girls are almost never affected (theoretical 1 in 100,000,000).

Carrier mother × healthy father: sons 50% affected, daughters 50% carriers. No male-to-male transmission — a son gets his Y from dad and his X from mum.

Figure 8 · Sex-linked recessive: the classic "why it's mostly boys" diagram. Sons inherit their X from mum — 50% chance of the disease allele.

Pedigree charts — reading the family album

Pedigree charts map a disorder through a family: squares = males, circles = females, filled = affected. Generations are Roman numerals down the side; individuals numbered within each generation.

  • Recessive signature: two unaffected parents produce an affected child → both parents must be carriers (Aa × Aa).
  • Dominant signature: every affected child has at least one affected parent — the trait doesn't skip generations.
  • X-linked recessive signature: mostly affected males; no father→son transmission.
  • Most of the 4,000+ identified genetic disorders are caused by very rare recessive alleles — you'd need to inherit the same rare allele from both parents. A typical person carries at least 5 rare recessive alleles that could cause a disorder.
