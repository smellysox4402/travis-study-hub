THE TICKETS

Alleles, genotypes, phenotypes — and why dominant always wins at the door.

🎫

Two tickets per gene

alleles · genotype · homozygous · heterozygous · dominant · recessive

Alleles are different versions of a gene — they can differ by as little as one base or by large sections. New alleles are born by mutation. Diploid organisms carry two alleles of most genes, one inherited from each parent.

The combination of alleles is the genotype (DD, Dd, dd). If both alleles are the same → homozygous (all gametes carry that one allele). If different → heterozygous (50% of gametes carry each allele).

The phenotype is the observable outcome — structural (curly vs straight hair) or functional (seeing red vs green). Most phenotypes come from the interaction of genotype and environment.

Figure 3 · The classic monohybrid cross. Two heterozygotes → 3:1 phenotype, 1:2:1 genotype. The dwarf trait vanished in F₁ and reappeared in a quarter of F₂.
GENOTYPE

The tickets you hold

DD, Dd or dd — the allele combination. Homozygous = same pair, heterozygous = different pair.

PHENOTYPE

What shows

The observable trait. Genotype × environment → phenotype. Not everything written in the tickets gets expressed.

DOMINANT

The bouncer

One dominant allele is enough to show the trait. Recessive only shows when both tickets are recessive (tt).

Exam classic: Mendel crossed pure-breeding tall × dwarf. All F₁ were tall (the dwarf trait "disappeared"). Self-pollinate the F₁ and the dwarf trait reappears in 1/4 of F₂ — a 3:1 ratio. That reappearance is the evidence against blending inheritance.
D3.2.6

SAME GENES, DIFFERENT VIBE

Phenotypic plasticity: the same genotype, dressed differently by the environment.

🦎

The shape-shifter's trick

phenotypic plasticity · gene expression · reversible

Organisms can respond to their environment by varying patterns of gene expression — and therefore their traits. This is phenotypic plasticity: genes are switched on or off, not changed into new alleles, so it's reversible.

Classic example: tanning. More sunlight → increased expression of the melanin gene → darker skin. Less sunlight → gene expression reverts → paler skin. Same genotype the whole time.

Sometimes plasticity is a one-way switch between alternative forms. Plants are champions: seedlings raised in the dark grow tall, pale and etiolated; in the light they're green and compact — same variety, same genotype.

Figure 4 · Phenotypic plasticity: environment flips gene expression, not the genes. Reversible adaptation to a changing world.
Exam trap: plasticity is NOT mutation and NOT Lamarckian inheritance. The genes stay identical — only their expression changes. Nothing is inherited by the next generation from this.
D3.2.7 · D3.2.8

THE 1-IN-4 TRAP

PKU: the hidden-carrier disease, and the SNPs hiding in every gene pool.

🧬

Two carriers walk into a club…

carrier · recessive disease · phenylketonuria · SNP

Phenylketonuria (PKU) is a genetic disease caused by a recessive allele of the gene for the enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase (on chromosome 12 — an autosome). The enzyme converts phenylalanine → tyrosine.

A carrier (Aa) has one normal + one disease allele: they make enough functional enzyme to stay symptom-free, but can pass the disease allele on. Two carriers don't know they're carrying — until a child inherits both recessive alleles. Chance: 1 in 4 (25%).

With two disease alleles (aa), no functional enzyme is made → phenylalanine accumulates → brain damage, intellectual disability. Preventable: screen newborns, give a low-phenylalanine diet. Boys and girls have the same 25% chance — it's autosomal.

Figure 5 · The 1-in-4 trap: two symptom-free carriers, 25% risk per child. PKU is preventable if caught at birth — which is why heel-prick screening exists.

SNPs — the single-base ticket variations

A gene pool is all the genes of all individuals in a sexually reproducing population. Alleles differ by small variations in base sequence — often just one base. Positions where different bases can occur are single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), pronounced "snips".

Even within one gene there can be many SNP positions, so a gene pool can hold many different alleles of the same gene — that's multiple alleles. Next act: the ABO blood group is the classic three-allele system.
